# Bheeshma's Vow

Vaishampayana continued his narration to King Janamejaya. Decades after the events surrounding Vyasa's birth, King Shantanu ruled Hastinapura. Though a great monarch, his heart was heavy with sorrow after losing his beloved wife, the river goddess Ganga. His only solace was their heroic son, Devavrata, whom he proudly installed as the heir-apparent to the Kuru throne.

Four years later, while strolling along the banks of the Yamuna, Shantanu was captivated by a divine, sweet fragrance. Tracing the scent, he discovered a breathtakingly beautiful woman ferrying passengers across the river. It was Satyavati (Gandhavati), the fisherman chief's daughter. Deeply smitten, Shantanu approached her father and asked for her hand in marriage.

"O King, I would gladly bestow my daughter on you," the fisherman chief replied respectfully but firmly. "But I ask for one promise: the son born to Satyavati must be your sole successor to the throne."
Unable to disinherit his noble and deserving son, Devavrata, Shantanu returned to Hastinapura in silent, brooding agony. 

Seeing his father wither away in despair, Devavrata quietly investigated and learned the truth from the king's old ministers. Without hesitation, the dutiful prince rode to the fisherman chief’s village, accompanied by Kuru elders.
"I relinquish my right to the throne," Devavrata announced boldly. "The son born of this maiden and my father shall be our king."

The fisherman hesitated. "O Prince, your word is absolute. But what if your future children contest the claim of my grandsons?"
To completely secure his father's happiness, Devavrata made a terrifying sacrifice. Raising his hand toward the heavens, he roared, "From this day, I adopt the vow of strict *Brahmacharya* (lifelong celibacy)! I shall never marry, nor father children."

The heavens trembled at his staggering sacrifice. Gods and sages showered flowers upon him from the skies, crying out, "Bheeshma! Bheeshma!" (The Terrible/The One of the Terrible Vow). From that moment on, Devavrata was known as Bheeshma. 
Overwhelmed by his son's devotion, King Shantanu granted Bheeshma a divine boon: *Ichcha Mrityu*—the power to choose the exact moment of his own death.

Shantanu and Satyavati were happily married and had two sons, Chitrangada and Vichitraveerya. After Shantanu passed away, Bheeshma crowned Chitrangada as king. However, the arrogant young king was soon killed in a fierce, three-year-long celestial duel with a Gandharva of the same name. Bheeshma then placed the minor Vichitraveerya on the throne, acting as his faithful regent.

When Vichitraveerya came of age, Bheeshma heard that the King of Kashi was hosting a grand *Swayamvara* for his three beautiful daughters: Amba, Ambika, and Ambalika. Determined to find suitable brides for his half-brother, Bheeshma crashed the ceremony. Thundering into the arena, he boldly abducted all three princesses onto his chariot, challenging the assembled kings to stop him if they dared. A massive battle ensued, but Bheeshma effortlessly defeated the entire coalition of kings, including the valiant King Shalva.

Upon returning to Hastinapura, the eldest princess, Amba, confessed that she had already given her heart to King Shalva. Respecting her wishes, Bheeshma honorably sent her to Shalva with an escort. However, King Shalva, humiliated by his defeat, cruelly rejected her. 

Meanwhile, Ambika and Ambalika were happily married to Vichitraveerya. But tragedy struck the Kuru line once more. Just seven years later, Vichitraveerya died of consumption, leaving no heir. 

Desperate to save the dynasty from extinction, Queen Satyavati turned to Bheeshma. "You are the only one who can carry forward the line," she pleaded. "Rule the kingdom and beget children with your brother's widows."
"Breach of truth is never approved by our sacred texts," Bheeshma refused gently but firmly. "I will never break my vow."

Left with no other choice, Satyavati summoned her firstborn son, the great ascetic Veda Vyasa, using the mental bond they shared. Following the ancient, sanctioned custom of *Niyoga*, she asked Vyasa to bless the widows with offspring. Vyasa agreed but warned that the queens must accept him in his unkempt, ascetic appearance. 

When Vyasa approached Princess Ambika, she was so terrified by his matted hair and rugged look that she squeezed her eyes tightly shut. Vyasa told Satyavati, "Because she closed her eyes, her son will be born blind." This son was Dhritarashtra.

Next, Vyasa visited Princess Ambalika. Terrified by the sage's intense aura, she turned completely pale. "Her son will be pale," Vyasa predicted. This son was Pandu.

Desperate for a perfectly healthy heir, Satyavati ordered Ambika to visit the sage again. But the frightened princess secretly sent her devoted maid in her place. The maid served Vyasa with deep reverence and humility. Pleased by her devotion, Vyasa blessed her, and she gave birth to Vidura—an incarnation of Dharma (Justice) himself, born to be supremely intelligent and wise.

Bheeshma raised the three boys with utmost love and care, training them in all princely arts. When they came of age, the blind Dhritarashtra was deemed unfit to rule, so the pale Pandu was crowned King of Hastinapura. 

Bheeshma then arranged their marriages to secure the kingdom's alliances. He matched Dhritarashtra with Princess Gandhari of Gandhara. Upon learning her husband was blind, the fiercely devoted Gandhari permanently blindfolded herself, refusing to enjoy the light her husband could not see. 

For King Pandu, Bheeshma secured Princess Kunti, the adopted daughter of King Kuntibhoja, and Princess Madri, the sister of King Shalya of Madra, ensuring the Kuru dynasty was fortified for the future.
