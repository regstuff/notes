# Jasma of the Odes

Jasma, a celestial nymph (apsara) in her previous birth, was cursed by the enraged Sage Nala to be born into a poor nomadic community and to marry an ugly man. In fulfillment of this curse, she was born to Nayak Dalo Dhand, the chief of the nomadic Ode tribe in Gujarat. 

"She reminds me of a jasmine, fair and pretty as she is. Let us call her Jasma," her loving father said. 

Tragically, Jasma's mother died when she was just a toddler. Dalo married a woman named Dali, hoping she would be a mother to Jasma. But from the day she set eyes on her, Dali disliked Jasma and often ill-treated her. 

As Jasma grew up, Dali's dislike turned into jealous hatred, for Jasma was easily the most beautiful girl in the Ode tribe. Dali deliberately gave her hard, menial tasks. When the time came for Jasma to marry, the evil Dali saw her opportunity to strike a cruel blow. She arranged for Jasma to marry her cousin Bhalo Bhand's son, Rupa—who was not only poor, but also a cripple. 

Dalo was deeply pained but helpless as he had no dowry to offer for a better match. The wedding took place. When Jasma saw her husband's face and his crippled body, she broke down in tears, lamenting her cruel fate. 
But Rupa's hideous form hid a heart of gold. "Jasma, forgive me," he said gently. "I know how you must feel. I will try to make you as happy as I can. Please don't weep."
Moved by his kindness, Jasma felt ashamed of her vanity. She fell at his feet and promised, "I will be a loyal wife to you and will look after all your needs." 

A few months later, Jivaji Barot, a court poet of King Siddharaj Jaisingh of Gujarat, happened to pass by the Ode camp. He was captivated by Jasma's dazzling beauty and composed several verses about her. He sang these verses at the court in Patan. 
"A mere tribal girl! You call her a queen!" King Siddharaj scoffed. "If it is an exaggeration, Jivaji, I'll behead you!"
"If you see her, your majesty, you will call her a goddess," Jivaji replied. 

Intrigued, the king wanted to summon her, but Jivaji explained that the proud, nomadic Odes would never come to the palace. 
Soon after, a terrible famine struck Gujarat, and the Odes were starving. Jivaji advised the king to revive the construction of the Sahasra-Linga lake and offer the Odes work. Desperate for survival, the proud Odes agreed to go to Patan, but insisted on camping on the outskirts of the capital to maintain their independence. 

The next day, King Siddharaj rode to the construction site. When he finally saw Jasma, he was stunned. "She must be a goddess," he thought. He approached her and offered to make her his queen. 
"Say you will become mine and I will take you to my palace! You will never have to work again," he tempted her. 
"I'd rather live in my hut," Jasma replied in disdain. "What would I do with a kingdom?"

Her mocking laughter maddened the king. "If you will not come willingly, I'll take you away by force."
"Don't you dare touch me. I am a married woman."
"Then I will kill your husband!" the king threatened. 
"Kill Rupa and our tribe will rise as one to avenge the murder."
"Then I will slaughter the entire tribe!" the spurned king roared and rode away. 

He summoned the chief of his guards and ordered him to kill Rupa and capture the rest of the tribe. The guards rode to the site and immediately beheaded the crippled Rupa. 
Aghast at this cold-blooded murder, the Odes took up arms. "Revenge! We must fight to a finish!" Nayak Dalo cried. 

The righteous Odes fought with a vengeance, initially routing the king's soldiers. But the royal commander changed his strategy and attacked from the rear. The Odes were taken off guard. The guards killed Bhalo Bhand, Rupa's father. Nayak Dalo refused to surrender, declaring, "Surrender is death for an Ode!" and was beheaded by the commander. 

When a fellow Ode brought the news of her father's death to Jasma and urged her to run, she refused. "I am an Ode. An Ode never runs away! I will join my husband," she declared. She prepared to commit Sati by jumping into a funeral pyre. 

Just then, King Siddharaj arrived, shocked by the massacre of the innocent tribals. As the flames enveloped Jasma, she turned to the king and laid a terrible curse upon him for lusting after a chaste wife and causing the death of her people. 

As Jasma entered the fire, Laldev, Indra's messenger, appeared to take her to heaven. But Jasma refused to go, saying she could not leave behind all those who had died for her. Struck by her loyalty, Laldev miraculously brought all the dead Odes, including Rupa, Bhalo, and Dalo, back to life. 

When Jasma saw her near and dear ones alive again, she told Laldev, "I would prefer to live and work among my people for a few years more." Laldev smiled, telling her the doors of heaven would be open whenever she was ready. 

Jasma lived out her life with Rupa and her beloved tribe. Many years later, after her death, she was revered. Temples were built in her memory, and folk songs are sung to this day in Gujarat and Rajasthan, remembering Jasma not just for her dazzling beauty, but for her unwavering loyalty, fearlessness, and chaste virtue.
