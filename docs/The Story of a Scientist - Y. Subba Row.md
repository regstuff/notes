# The Story of a Scientist - Yellapragada SubbaRow

Yellapragada SubbaRow was a 'rishi' of a scientist. If he is not as well-known in our country, it is because his pursuit of knowledge was entirely free of ego. The man who discovered Aureomycin, synthesized folic acid, and gave us Hetrazan never pushed himself into the glare of publicity. As the director of research, his name should have been at least at the top of the team. But often it took a self-effacing niche in alphabetical order. 

As a young boy in Madras Presidency, SubbaRow was restless. Once, he and his cousin ran away from home, hoping to go to Kashi to earn money by selling bananas. His anxious mother, Venkamma, tracked them down at a canal and hauled them back. 

"Will you ever understand, son?" his mother scolded him. "No amount of money earned by selling bananas can give you what a sound education can."

SubbaRow tried hard to concentrate on his studies, but he failed his matriculation examination. He was sent to Rajahmundry, where his brother Purushottam, an active nationalist schoolteacher, lived. Even there, SubbaRow struggled and failed again. He went to Madras for a third attempt. Just two months before the exam, his father died, plunging the family into deep poverty. 

Determined to see her son succeed, Venkamma sold her gold ornaments to pay for his fare and examination fee. For the first time in his life, SubbaRow understood his mother's profound sacrifice. Touched and motivated, he finally passed the examination and joined Presidency College, Madras, in 1913.

During college, SubbaRow developed a deep interest in spiritual life and began visiting the Ramakrishna Mission. He decided to become a sanyasi (monk). But a wise Swamiji advised him differently. "I want to realise God," SubbaRow told him. 

"For that, you must first understand man's responsibility to man," the Swamiji replied. "Why don't you become a doctor? You could work in one of our clinics."

SubbaRow took the advice to heart and joined the Madras Medical College, plunging into his studies with renewed enthusiasm. To support his education, the wealthy Kastury family offered financial help. They hoped he would marry one of their daughters. 

"If I marry a rich man's daughter, I will be expected to make myself rich, too, by any means!" SubbaRow initially protested to his mother. But eventually, he was persuaded to marry Seshagiri, one of the daughters, in 1919.

When Mahatma Gandhi launched the non-cooperation movement in 1920, SubbaRow supported it by wearing Khadi surgical gowns to college, which displeased his British professors. Consequently, despite securing excellent marks in his final medical examinations, he was awarded the lesser L.M.S. certificate instead of the full M.B.B.S. degree.

Disheartened, he went to Rajahmundry to consult his brother Purushottam, only to find him bedridden with Tropical Sprue. Purushottam succumbed to the disease, leaving SubbaRow devastated. Resolving to fight such deadly tropical diseases, SubbaRow wrote to Dr. Richard Strong of Harvard University's School of Tropical Medicine in the U.S.A.

Receiving a positive reply, SubbaRow left for America in 1923, armed with a small scholarship and his wife's reluctant consent. He arrived at Dr. Strong's office in Boston with barely a hundred dollars in his pocket. To pay for his fees and support himself, he took the only job available to an Indian doctor in America at the time—washing urinals and bed-pans in a hospital at night.

Despite the hardships, SubbaRow passed his examinations and earned a Diploma in Tropical Medicine. He then joined a bio-chemistry program under Professor Fiske. His hard work paid off when they perfected a method of estimating phosphorus in tissues—the now-famous "Fiske-SubbaRow method." 

They later discovered phosphocreatine and ATP (Adenosine Triphosphate) as the true source of muscular energy. However, another scientist in Germany, Karl Lohmann, published the same discovery just sixteen days before SubbaRow and Fiske were set to present their findings, officially beating them to it. 

"It is the discovery that matters, not the discoverer," SubbaRow said humbly, showing no bitterness. 

Later, SubbaRow met Dr. Gordon Alles, who was working on a cure for pernicious anaemia. Patients struggled to eat the raw broiled liver required for the cure. SubbaRow, remembering that the cure for pernicious anaemia could also cure the Tropical Sprue that killed his brother, was fascinated. He set out to isolate the active substance from the liver so it could be injected.

After long hours in his poorly equipped lab, often dodging suspicious police officers while transporting bloody bags of butcher's liver in his car trunk, SubbaRow and his assistant successfully created an injectable liver extract.

His brilliance caught the attention of Mr. Bell, President of Lederle Laboratories. Bell offered him a massive salary to do research for them. SubbaRow agreed, but with a unique condition: "For half the amount provided... you set up a well-equipped research laboratory where I can work as I want."

In 1940, SubbaRow left Harvard to become the Associate Director of Research at Lederle Laboratories. One of his first major projects was synthesizing folic acid, a vital vitamin extracted expensively from liver. After a year of fruitless experiments by his team of chemists, SubbaRow suggested an unconventional method—mixing all three raw substances together in one bowl. His "gamble" worked, successfully yielding synthetically made folic acid. 

Clinical trials proved that folic acid miraculously cured Tropical Sprue—the very disease that had taken his brother's life. True to his nature, SubbaRow claimed no special credit, listing his name simply as one of the sixteen scientists on the research paper.

Under his leadership, his team went on to discover 'Hetrazan', a drug that effectively controlled the dreaded disease Filaria. Later, after four years of tireless work, they discovered 'Aureomycin', a broad-spectrum golden antibiotic that proved to be a miracle drug against numerous infections.

SubbaRow's attention then turned to the deadliest disease of all: cancer. His team developed 'Aminopterin' (and later the more effective 'Methotrexate'), which was used to treat blood cancer (leukemia) in children. Though it only prolonged life rather than curing it permanently at the time, it was a monumental breakthrough in cancer treatment.

Driven by these successes, SubbaRow finalized plans to build a dedicated, independent cancer research unit. Tragically, he did not live to see it established. Yellapragada SubbaRow, the brilliant, selfless 'rishi' of science, died of heart failure at the young age of fifty-three, leaving behind a legacy of medical miracles that saved countless lives around the world.
