# The Adventures of Baddu and Chhotu

Two friends, Baddu and Chhotu, once set out together to seek their fortune. Reaching a village, they agreed to split up and look for work separately, promising to meet back under a tree that evening. Baddu found a man who would pay him to water his orchard every day; Chhotu found a farmer willing to hire him to take a cow out to graze. "That's wonderful!" they told each other, delighted at their luck, and found a hut to sleep in that night.

The next morning showed just how differently "easy" their two jobs really were. Baddu spent hours hauling buckets from a stream to the orchard, pouring dozens of them onto the saplings, and still found the ground bone dry every time he checked — one particular mango sapling seemed to swallow bucket after bucket without ever looking watered. Chhotu, meanwhile, thought he'd got the better bargain entirely: he simply let the cow loose in a meadow to graze while he settled down for a nap under a tree. The moment he let go of the rope, though, the cow bolted, and Chhotu spent the whole day chasing it across field after field, dodging furious farmers as it trampled through their crops, until he finally caught up with it, exhausted, only at dusk.

That evening, each was too embarrassed to admit how badly his own "easy" job had gone. "How did things go?" Baddu asked. "The cow gave me no trouble at all," Chhotu lied. "I let it loose in the meadow and had a good nap." Baddu, not wanting to be outdone, claimed he'd finished all his watering before half the day was even done. Neither believed the other for a second — each was privately certain the other's job was the truly easy one — until finally, unable to keep up the pretence, they blurted out their frustrations at the same moment. "It's like this, Chhotu. My job is very good, but it's dull... very dull." "What a coincidence! I was about to say the same thing about my own job!" Relieved to discover they were equally miserable, they proposed a simple fix: swap jobs the next day.

It didn't help. Chhotu, now watering the orchard, found the very same mystery sapling drinking up bucket after bucket without ever looking watered, muttering that he must have poured eighty-four buckets on it already. Baddu, now herding the cow, fared no better than Chhotu had — the animal led him on exactly the same wild chase across the countryside, trampling exactly the same neighbours' fields, until he too limped home at nightfall having learned nothing except that the grazing job was every bit as exhausting as the watering one.

That night, aching all over, the two friends finally put their heads together over the real puzzle: why did that one little sapling never seem to get any wetter, no matter how much water either of them poured on it? "Perhaps the ground below it is hollow," Chhotu guessed. "How did it become hollow?" Baddu wondered. "Do you think someone has dug up the earth and buried something there?" "Gold, perhaps!" Forgetting their aching backs entirely, the two grabbed spades and dashed back to the orchard in the dark to dig — and dug, and dug, without finding a thing. "It's no use, Chhotu. We've wasted our time," Baddu finally admitted, and they filled the hole back in and started for home.

But on the way out, digging a little further along almost as an afterthought, Baddu's spade struck something solid: a gold mohur. Quick thinking took over — rather than share the discovery, he palmed the coin and told Chhotu it was "just a pretty stone," while privately vowing to sneak back alone once his friend was asleep. Chhotu, no fool, didn't believe the "pretty stone" story for a second, and the two spent the rest of the walk home each pretending to trust the other completely while secretly plotting to double back for the buried treasure alone.

Chhotu made his move first, waiting until he was sure Baddu was fast asleep before slipping out into the night, digging up an entire pot of gold coins from beneath the sapling, and hiding it in a nearby pool before creeping back to bed — only for Baddu to wake moments later, notice his friend's legs oddly caked in slime up to the knee, and put two and two together. Baddu made straight for the pool himself, waded in, and found the very pot Chhotu had just hidden there.

By morning each was convinced the other had beaten him to the treasure. Chhotu woke to find both the pot and Baddu gone, guessed correctly where his friend must be headed with it, bought himself a fine pair of slippers with an eye toward looking prosperous, and set off after him on a borrowed donkey by a shortcut Baddu didn't know. Along the road he dropped one slipper deliberately in Baddu's path, then rode ahead and dropped the second a little further on. Baddu, coming along behind with the pot slung over his own borrowed mount, found the first slipper, admired it, but reasoned a single slipper was useless — until he spotted the second one up ahead and, thinking he'd have a matching pair if he simply went back for the first, tied up his donkey (and the treasure with it) and doubled back. Chhotu, watching from the bushes, slipped in, grabbed the donkey and the gold, and made off for the village well ahead of his unsuspecting friend.

Safely home with the whole pot to himself, Chhotu bolted his door and began counting his fortune — only for a furious pounding to interrupt him. It was Baddu, who'd worked out the trick the moment he'd returned to find both slippers and his donkey gone. "I got in just before you did," Baddu announced, refusing to be cheated a second time. "Half those coins are mine!" Chhotu, cornered, agreed to split the hoard evenly — right down to one odd coin left over once the piles were divided equally. Rather than fight over it, Chhotu offered to take it to market the next day, exchange it for silver, and split that too, and Baddu, satisfied, agreed to collect his share the following evening.

When Baddu came calling the next day, though, Chhotu's young nephew met him at the door with grim news: "Uncle Chhotu is no more. He's dead." Baddu, utterly unconvinced — certain this was simply Chhotu's latest trick to dodge splitting the last gold coin — marched in to see for himself, found Chhotu lying stiff and silent on a cot, and decided to call his bluff by performing the funeral rites in earnest. He tied his "dead" friend to a wooden bier and dragged him off toward the forest to cremate him, ignoring Chhotu's silent, increasingly desperate hope that his bluff wouldn't actually be tested.

It very nearly was. Baddu, finding a suitable clearing, went off to gather firewood — leaving the bier conveniently in the path of a passing gang of thugs, who mistook it for an abandoned corpse and, in a fit of charitable spirit, decided to cremate it themselves as their good deed for the day. As they piled wood around him and prepared to strike a light, Chhotu's nerve finally broke: he sprang up screaming for help, and the terrified thugs, convinced they'd raised a corpse from the dead, fled shrieking into the night — abandoning, in their panic, a heavy sack they'd been carrying.

Baddu returned to find his "dead" friend very much alive and sitting beside an unclaimed sack of gold coins the thugs had left behind. Rather than quarrel over the windfall, the two of them simply divided the new treasure equally between them too — and when one odd coin turned up left over yet again, Chhotu had a ready answer: "You already have one extra coin from the treasure we found in the orchard. So this one comes to me." "Fair enough," Baddu agreed without argument, and the two friends returned to their village together, considerably richer for all their trouble, and remained good friends for the rest of their lives.
