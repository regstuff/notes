# Vikramaditya's Throne

Long ago, Indra, the King of the Gods, had pleased Lord Shiva with his intense devotion. As a reward, Shiva gifted him a breathtakingly exquisite, magical throne. Indra treasured the throne above all his possessions, declaring, "I shall never part with it, except to someone more worthy of it than I."

In those celestial days, Parvati, Shiva's wife, had thirty-two beautiful apsaras from Indra's court as her close companions. One day, Parvati noticed Shiva casting admiring glances at the dancing apsaras. Consumed by sudden jealousy and anger, she cursed them. "May each one of you become a lifeless statue and adorn the throne of Indra! There you will get all the admiration you crave."

The apsaras were aghast and begged for mercy. Touched by their pleas, Parvati relented slightly. "This throne will reach the earth in the reign of Raja Vikramaditya. Centuries later, it will be found by Raja Bhoja. When he tries to mount it, you will stop him and tell him stories from the life of Vikramaditya. If he is magnanimous enough to hear all of you out, you will be free of this curse."

Meanwhile, on earth, in the city of Ujjaini, reigned King Bhartrihari. He loved his beautiful wife, Anangasena, so dearly that he took a solemn vow never to outlive her. 

One day, a poor, pious Brahman, a devoted follower of Parvati, received a divine fruit from the goddess herself. "Eat it. It will give you everlasting youth and immortality," Parvati blessed him. 

The Brahman, thinking selflessly, decided, "Immortality would be wasted on me, a pauper that I am. An immortal king will benefit many." He presented the magical fruit to King Bhartrihari, who generously rewarded him. 

Bhartrihari immediately gave the fruit to his beloved Anangasena, explaining its magical properties. "Beloved, eat this fruit and preserve your youth and life forever."

But Anangasena did not truly love the King. She was secretly in love with a handsome stable-boy. She gave the precious fruit to him. "Eat this, my love. I will then be sure of having you all my life."

The stable-boy, however, was unfaithful to the Queen. He was in love with a lowly maid who carried cow-dung. He gave the fruit to her. 

The maid, overjoyed, tossed the fruit carelessly on top of her basket of dung and walked merrily down the road. Just then, King Bhartrihari passed by on a hunt and recognized the magical fruit. Stunned, he forced the truth from the maid, the stable-boy, and finally his wife. 

Utterly disgusted with the betrayal and the treacherous nature of worldly attachments, Bhartrihari decided to renounce his kingdom and become an ascetic. "There is no greater enemy than attachment, and no greater happiness than renunciation," he declared. He called his younger brother, Vikramaditya, to the throne. "I have decided to become an ascetic. You shall reign in my place."

Vikramaditya became a wise and beloved King. One dark night, a mysterious sorcerer came to him, asking for his assistance in a dark sacrifice at the cemetery. Fearless, Vikramaditya agreed. 

At the spooky cemetery, the sorcerer commanded, "On that tree there hangs a *vetala* (a ghoul). Bring him to me without uttering a single word! For if you speak, he will fly right back to his tree."

Vikramaditya bravely caught the terrifying vetala and slung it over his shoulder. To provoke the King into breaking his silence, the vetala began telling him a complex moral story. At the end of the tale, the vetala asked a difficult question about justice and virtue. "If you refuse to answer, knowing the truth, I will break your head into a thousand pieces!" the ghoul threatened. 

Vikramaditya, bound by his fierce sense of justice, answered correctly. The moment he spoke, the vetala vanished, flying back to its tree. 

Undaunted, Vikramaditya went back, caught the ghoul again, and the process repeated. The vetala told twenty-four stories, and twenty-four times, Vikramaditya broke his silence to deliver perfectly just answers. But when the twenty-fifth story was told, Vikramaditya truly did not know the answer and kept quiet. 

Defeated, the vetala was forced to stay on his shoulder. "This king is courageous, clever, and compassionate," the vetala thought admiringly. "I must warn him of the sorcerer."

The vetala revealed the truth. "O King, the sorcerer intends to kill you. When you take me to him, he will ask you to bow before the sacrificial fire. When you do so, he will slay you."

"What should I do?" Vikramaditya asked. 

"When he asks you to bow, tell him you don't know how, being a king, and ask him to show you," the vetala advised. 

Everything happened exactly as the vetala had warned. When the sorcerer bent down to demonstrate how to bow, Vikramaditya swiftly drew his sword and slew the evil man. 

Instantly, the ghoul transformed into a radiant, divine Brahman. "You have made the eight-fold prosperity yours. What will you have from me?" he asked. 

"Give the sorcerer back his life, and promise to come to me when I need you," the magnanimous Vikramaditya requested. The sorcerer was revived, completely cleansed of his evil nature. 

Soon, Vikramaditya's fame spread far and wide, even reaching the heavens. Lord Indra, facing a difficult decision between two rival celestial dancers, Urvashi and Rambha, sent his charioteer Matali to fetch King Vikramaditya to act as the ultimate judge. 

Vikramaditya expertly judged the spectacular contest, explaining the nuances of the dance with such profound insight that Indra was astounded. "He is a fit occupant for Shiva's throne!" Indra declared, presenting the magical, thirty-two-statue throne to the mortal King. 

From the day Vikramaditya mounted the celestial throne in Ujjaini, his rule was unimaginably glorious. Poverty and famine were unknown, and evil was vanquished. 

But destiny had other plans. Day after day, terrible omens struck Ujjaini. The court astrologers interpreted them grimly: "They forebode ill, O King. The omens bespeak the birth of your foe."

Vikramaditya remembered a strange boon he had once asked of Lord Shiva. "Let my death be at the hands of none but the son of a two-and-a-half-year-old girl." 

He summoned his friend, the divine vetala. "Roam the whole world till you locate the boy."

The vetala found a young boy named Shalivahana, born under miraculous circumstances in Pratishthana. Vikramaditya immediately marched to Pratishthana to face his destiny. 

During the battle, the young boy, Shalivahana, bravely struck the great King with a simple toy staff. The magical blow caused Vikramaditya such agonizing pain that he was forced to flee back to Ujjaini, where he died a few hours later. 

After his death, his grieving ministers realized, "There is no king worthy enough to sit on this throne." They buried the divine throne deep in a pure, untouched field. 

Hundreds of years passed, and the throne was completely forgotten, fading into legend. The city of Dhara eventually replaced Ujjaini as the capital, ruled by the just and capable Raja Bhoja. 

One day, while hunting near Dhara, Raja Bhoja passed a lush sugarcane field. A poor Brahman, sitting on a raised earthen mound in the center of the field, generously called out, "O King, come with your followers! Quench your thirst on this juicy sugarcane."

But the moment the Brahman stepped down from the mound to greet them, his demeanor changed drastically. "O King, how can you be so wicked! Will you destroy a poor Brahman's crops?" he yelled angrily. 

Puzzled, the King and his men began to leave. But as soon as the Brahman climbed back onto the mound, he called out warmly again, "O King, where are you going? Come back and rest!"

Raja Bhoja realized there was something deeply magical about the mound itself. He climbed onto the platform. Instantly, his mind cleared. "Why! I breathe a purer air. Noble, charitable thoughts fill my being. If anyone at this moment but needs my life, I am ready to give it!" 

Intrigued, the King bought the field and ordered his men to dig up the mound. Buried deep beneath the earth, they uncovered the magnificent, jeweled throne of Vikramaditya. 

After performing the proper sacred rituals, the throne was easily moved to a specially built hall in Dhara. 

On an auspicious day, Raja Bhoja confidently approached the throne to ascend it. Suddenly, one of the thirty-two beautiful statues carved into its base spoke! 

"Stop! O King! Only one possessing all the magnanimity and the other rare virtues of the owner of this throne may mount it." 

Raja Bhoja was astonished. "Tell me of the magnanimity of him to whom this throne belonged," he asked humbly. 

The statue recounted a breathtaking story of Vikramaditya's incredible selflessness and bravery. When she finished, she challenged Bhoja: "Mount the throne if you can equal him." 

Raja Bhoja hung his head. "I cannot," he admitted honestly. 

In this manner, Raja Bhoja made thirty-two separate attempts to mount the throne. Each time, a different statue came to life, stopping him to tell another extraordinary tale of Vikramaditya's unparalleled kingly virtues, forcing Bhoja to admit he was not yet worthy. 

Finally, after the thirty-second statue had told her tale, the statues spoke together. "Do not remain silent. You too are a great, glorious, generous King. Your incredible patience, humility, and magnanimity in listening to our tales have finally released us from Parvati's curse!" 

The statues transformed into radiant apsaras. "Is there any wish of yours that we can fulfil before we return to heaven?" they asked gratefully. 

"Yes," Raja Bhoja replied wisely. "Let the adventures of Vikramaditya become immortal upon earth. Let all who hear them be safe from any danger." 

"So be it," the apsaras blessed him, before vanishing into the sky. 

Recognizing the true divine nature of the artifact, Raja Bhoja did not sit on the throne as a mortal king. Instead, he placed it in a grand shrine inlaid with bright gold and precious gems. Upon the seat, he reverently placed the images of Lord Shiva and Goddess Parvati, worshipping the deities and the magnificent throne that had taught him the true meaning of greatness.
