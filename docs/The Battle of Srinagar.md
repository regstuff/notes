# The Battle for Srinagar

It was October 1947. Srinagar, capital of Jammu and Kashmir, lay calm — but two hundred kilometres to the west, in the border town of Muzaffarabad, uneasy talk filled the market. A large force of Qabailies — armed tribesmen from across the frontier — was said to be massing nearby. No one yet knew why.

They found out soon enough. The raiders swept into Muzaffarabad in trucks, burning and shooting their way through the town, indifferent to the pleas of terrified residents. Hundreds of refugees fled toward Uri, the next town on the road to Srinagar, certain the raiders meant to open that road for themselves as quickly as possible.

When the news reached the Maharaja in Srinagar, the danger was plain. "Only the army can stop this now," he told his Chief of Staff, Brigadier Rajinder Singh. But there was little army to send. "Your Highness, we have no troops on the Uri-Srinagar road," Rajinder admitted, "but I will try to muster some." Back at headquarters he could scrape together barely two hundred men, two mortars, and seven trucks against a raiding force numbering in the thousands. He did not hesitate. "The enemy will advance only over my dead body," he told the Maharaja, and set off to lead the defence himself.

On the rain-soaked night of 22 October, this improvised force began its march — two hundred men against thousands, a march that would end in martyrdom for many of them. Reaching Uri after midnight, Rajinder pushed on toward Garhi at dawn, straight into the advancing raiders. A brutal firefight broke out; within hours forty of his men were down, and Rajinder ordered a fallback to Uri. There, working through the night, his demolition squad blew up the bridge over the Uri Nullah, buying precious time — but the raiders simply waded across at every ford, and Rajinder's machine guns cut them down as they emerged, dripping, on the far bank.

The fighting raged for five relentless days. Each time Rajinder dug in, the raiders probed for a gap; each time they found one, six of their columns would slip past his position while two more kept his men pinned down. Running short of food and ammunition, and with twenty more men wounded, Rajinder finally ordered a withdrawal toward Baramula in the small hours of the morning — but the raiders had guessed his move and laid an ambush along the retreat road. As his trucks rolled into the trap, a hail of bullets and shellfire tore through the convoy. Rajinder was hit in the chest, both legs fractured, yet he dragged himself back into a truck as his men fought to pull him clear.

They carried him to the Diwan Mandir, and from there to Baramula hospital, but the wound was too grave. "Don't worry about me," he told his officers as they prepared to leave him. "Go out and destroy the enemy. Kashmir must be saved. I have done my duty." He died soon after. His men gave him a soldier's salute and turned back to the fight he had bought them time to win — but with the road now clear, the raiders pushed on toward Baramula themselves, burning the power station at Mahura as they went, so that "Srinagar will be in darkness when we get there." At Baramula they looted freely, sparing no one, not even the nuns of a nearby convent — and even as the plunder went on, they posted machine guns on the high ground overlooking the Srinagar road, watching for whatever might come next.

By then Kashmir had signed its Instrument of Accession to India, and help was already on the way. The 1st Sikh Battalion, under Lieutenant Colonel Ranjit Rai, landed at Srinagar airfield and, leaving one company to guard it, pushed straight on toward Baramula. The column ran into the same raiders' guns on the hillside above the road. Rai was hit and fell; Major Bachan Singh took over command and led the companies safely back to Pattan, where they dug in through the night, mounted their machine guns, and waited — under strict orders not to fire until the enemy came well within range. When the raiders finally advanced through the trees, the ambush worked in reverse: the entrenched guns tore into their ranks, and the survivors pulled back to argue over what to do next.

"The Indian troops seem to be here in full strength," one raider leader admitted. "We can't get past them to Srinagar." Someone had an idea: disguise themselves as refugees and slip through in small groups. It nearly worked — small parties of "refugees" filtered into the city, met at first with wary questions from the Indian soldiers posted at the checkpoints, then with real sympathy once they claimed to be fleeing Muzaffarabad. "Don't worry, brothers," the soldiers told them. "We'll throw out the raiders and soon you'll be able to return home" — never suspecting who they were really talking to.

Meanwhile, at 4 Kumaon's headquarters in Delhi, fresh orders came in to move to Srinagar at once. Major Somnath Sharma, lying in hospital with a fractured arm, heard the news and would not be left behind. "You are in no condition to fight," the doctor warned him. "But I must go with my company," Sharma answered — and talked his way onto the very first plane out.

In Srinagar, Brigadier L. P. Sen, worried about the security of Badgam airfield, sent Sharma's company and another under Ronnie Wood to sweep the area from opposite directions. Wood's men found nothing — only refugees, some of them brewing tea by a nullah, seemingly harmless. But when that same "harmless" crowd began setting up a light machine gun in full view, the truth became suddenly, violently clear: these were the disguised raiders who had slipped past the checkpoints, and Sharma's company was now surrounded by hundreds of them.

The fighting that followed was as one-sided as it was desperate — roughly seven hundred raiders against a single company, with orders from the other side to let no Indian soldier escape alive. Sharma radioed for reinforcements and for air support; the Indian Air Force answered within minutes, strafing the raider positions and buying his men a brief respite. But the raiders regrouped, and as casualty after casualty fell at the machine-gun posts, Sharma himself crawled forward under fire to keep the guns fed, loading magazines for gunners who had no one left to relieve them. He was still doing it when a raider shell found his position and killed him.

His sacrifice was not wasted. The two remaining companies of the Kumaon Regiment arrived soon after and drove the raiders back from Badgam for good. Brigadier Rajinder Singh and Major Somnath Sharma had both died at their posts, holding ground with far too few men against far too many — and it was that ground, bought at the cost of their lives, that kept the road to Srinagar closed to the raiders long enough for India's forces to arrive in strength. A grateful nation honoured them with the Mahavir Chakra and the Param Vir Chakra, India's highest wartime gallantry award — both bestowed posthumously, in recognition of a defence that saved Kashmir's capital when almost nothing stood in the way of losing it.
