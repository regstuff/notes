# The Golden Sand

When Sakhwal, a humble sand-merchant of Kathmandu, stepped out of his house one morning, he saw two labourers carrying baskets of sand. "I'll take that sand," Sakhwal offered. "I'll pay you well for it." 

"We'll give it to you, sir," the men agreed. "But what about... it's for..." Sakhwal cut them off, offering them a good price. The men went into Sakhwal's warehouse, emptied their baskets, and happily took their money.

Then, the two labourers hurried back to Lakhu, a sacred pilgrim centre situated at the confluence of the rivers Bhatikhu and Vishnumati. They refilled their baskets with sand and carried them all the way to the palace of the King of Bhadgao.

"Good! Put it down in there," the King's guard ordered. 

A few hours later, the King of Bhadgao examined the contents of the baskets. Plunging his hands into the grains, he frowned. "Humph! Nothing but sand." He turned to his court astrologer, furious. "You claimed that the sand of Lakhu collected at a certain auspicious moment would turn into gold after twelve hours! Is this your gold?"

The astrologer examined the sand, completely baffled. "What could have gone wrong? I was so sure..."

Meanwhile, back at Sakhwal's warehouse, the merchant walked in to inspect his new purchase. He stopped dead in his tracks. The pile of sand the labourers had left was glowing brilliantly. 

"Why is the sand shining so?" he wondered. Picking up a handful, his jaw dropped. "Wh... what's this? Oh! Oh! Wonder of wonders! It's gold dust!"

Sakhwal hurriedly put the gold away in a bin. But that night, he was racked by doubts. As he lay in bed, unable to sleep, his conscience gnawed at him. *It's true I found it in my warehouse... but does that make it mine? Should I keep it or should I... Oh, well, I'll sleep over it and then decide.*

The next morning, Sakhwal set out of his house, walking through the market-place, still struggling with his moral dilemma. Suddenly, a desperate voice broke into his thoughts.

"Mercy! Mercy, Sahuji!" an old man pleaded. Sakhwal turned around to see a poor man on his knees before a ruthless money-lender. "Please don't take away my land and my cattle, Sahuji. I'll be ruined!"

"So had you promised last month, and the month before!" the money-lender scoffed. "No, I can't wait any more! Pay now or else I'll..."

Sakhwal imagined himself in the old man's terrifying plight. And in that moment, he knew exactly what he must do with his newfound wealth. 

"Here! Take this and give him back his bond," Sakhwal declared, stepping between them. 

The money-lender sneered. "You... low-born..."

"I may be low-born, I may be impure..." Sakhwal countered, bringing out a handful of sparkling gold dust, "...but my gold is neither. So take it!" The stunned money-lender accepted the gold and returned the bond to the poor, tearful debtor. 

As Sakhwal walked away, the scene he had just witnessed set him thinking. *There must be hundreds like him... who are caught in the clutches of merciless money-lenders.*

He went straight to the royal court of King Jayadeva Malla. "Maharaj," Sakhwal began, "I bought some sand yesterday. And nearly twelve hours later... it turned to gold!"

When he finished his incredible tale, Sakhwal made his request. "Maharaj, I want to use the gold to free all those who are unfortunate enough to be in the clutches of money-lenders. Do I have your permission, Maharaj? And your blessings?"

The King was astounded. "Blessings? Permission? My good man, men richer and mightier than you would have kept the gold for themselves... and you are ready to part with it so that others may be free of debt!"

The King turned to his courtiers. "A man like Sakhwal is rare. His name should describe his noble nature. From today, he shall be known as Shankhdhar—after Lord Vishnu, the divine protector. Shankhdhar ko jai!"

"Shankhdhar ko jai!" the courtiers cheered.

"And to mark this great occasion," the King announced, "the calendar we follow henceforth shall be the Shankhdhar Samvat, of which this great day shall be the first."

When Shankhdhar's selfless offer was proclaimed, people from far and near approached him. He helped one and all. Soon, there was not a single debt-ridden soul left in the land!

One day, King Jayadeva Malla sent for Shankhdhar. "Will you come with me to the temple of Pashupatinath?" the King asked.

"Gladly, Maharaj," Shankhdhar agreed. 

But when they arrived at the majestic entrance of the sacred shrine, Shankhdhar stopped, hanging his head. "B... but... Maharaj... how can I enter the sacred temple?" he asked softly, aware of his humble caste.

"Please go in, Shankhdhar!" the King said warmly. "I'll bow to the Lord from out here. One's caste is determined by one's character and not by one's birth. Today, there is none of a higher caste than you in the whole land."

Amidst great cheer, the noble Shankhdhar entered the temple. In honor of his legendary generosity, a statue of Shankhdhar was later installed at the southern gate of the temple of Pashupatinath in Kathmandu. And the Shankhdhar calendar is followed in Nepal to this very day.
