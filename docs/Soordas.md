# Soordas: The Blind Bard Who Sang About Lord Krishna

Soordas, the blind bard of Brij, occupies a very eminent position in Hindi literature. He is considered the Valmiki of Brij, not only because he was the first poet to write in that dialect, but also because his works have an epic stature.

Born in 1478 A.D. to a poor Saraswat Brahman couple in the village of Sihi near Delhi, his life began with a tragedy. Soon after his birth, the village nurse made a shocking discovery. "Panditji," she told the father hesitantly, "the child seems to be blind."

"He is blind," the father frowned. "What a nuisance!"

By the time the child was three years old, everyone in the neighborhood seemed to have forgotten his real name. Even members of his own family simply called him 'Soor'—the blind one. His mother had little patience for him. When he complained of hunger, she snapped, "He will have to wait. I've not yet finished cooking. Take him out and see that he does not bother me!"

One year, just a few days before the festival of Diwali, his father declared, "This year I can't afford new clothes for all the boys. Soor can do with his old ones. It couldn't make any difference to the blind fellow, anyway."

The cruel words stung the boy. He ran outside to hide his tears, only to be mocked and tripped by the village boys. Hurt, grimy, and tearful, Soor came back to his mother, hoping for a kind word or a comforting hug. Instead, she scolded him, "You good-for-nothing! How many times have I told you not to go out? I don't have the time to bathe you ten times a day!"

"Sorry, mother. I won't go out again," the miserable boy whispered.

Sitting alone on the verandah, not knowing what to do with himself, he heard a band of street-singers passing by, singing beautiful devotional bhajans. The sound of their music soothed his troubled soul. For a moment, he forgot that he was blind and considered "good-for-nothing." Life took on a new meaning. "One day, I will learn to sing," he promised himself. "But first, I must study."

Hearing his elder brothers chanting Sanskrit shlokas indoors, he approached his father. "Father, please teach me too."

"What! Teach you, a blind brat?" his father scoffed. "Go to your mother, you good-for-nothing." 

His brothers mocked him as well. "You want to learn, don't you? Why don't you learn to cook?" Heartbroken, Soor realized he would have to forget about formal studies.

A few days later, the same street-singers passed by his house. As if in a trance, Soor quietly followed them. It was only when the party camped for the night on the outskirts of the village that they noticed the newcomer. "Hey! Who are you?" they asked.

"I'm Soor. I want to learn to sing," he pleaded. He listened intently to their songs all night. But the following morning, the singers decided, "Why burden ourselves with a blind boy? Let's slip away without him."

When Soor woke up, they were gone. But he could now do without them. He continued to live near a lake, singing devotional songs and eating whatever was offered to him by the simple village folk. He learned a number of things by listening to the conversations of sadhus and pilgrims who camped near the lake on their way to Mathura and Vrindavan. 

Deprived of his sight, the boy was gifted with a remarkable "sixth sense." By the time he was fourteen, he became famous as a diviner. Villagers would come to him to find lost cattle or to ask for auspicious times for journeys, and his predictions were always perfect. Grateful people brought him gifts. 

One day, a wealthy Zamindar visited him. "No, Soordasji! It is I who have come to fall at your feet," the Zamindar said humbly. "I found my son exactly where you had predicted he would be found." In gratitude, the Zamindar built a comfortable hut for Soordas and gifted him an *ektara* (a one-stringed musical instrument).

Soon, disciples began to stay with him to serve him. But one night, Soordas had a vivid dream of Lord Krishna playing his flute, followed by hundreds of devotees swaying to the beat of a devotional song. He woke up suddenly and sat meditating. "I left home so that I could sing of my Lord in peace," he realized. "And I have become entangled in worldly things. I must go away from here."

Early next morning, taking only his ektara, he left his comfortable hut and roamed the jungles, singing in praise of Krishna. He traveled from place to place, eventually falling into an abandoned dry well while wandering through unused paths to escape the crowds. A week later, a divine hand reached down to save him. The touch was unmistakable—he knew it was his Lord Gopala (Krishna) who had saved him.

In his quest for Gopala, he reached Gow-ghat near Mathura. Here, he stayed for many years, composing hundreds of songs. His fame as the "Singing Mahatma" spread far and wide. 

One day, the great scholar-saint Vallabhacharya passed through. Hearing of Soordas, the great Acharya requested a meeting. When they met, Vallabhacharya told him, "You shall spread the glory of Krishna through your songs. But first, you will have to give up this self-denigration." 

"Lord! I am an unread fool. What would I know about Lord Krishna's life?" Soordas asked.

Vallabhacharya stayed for many days, narrating the life and deeds of Krishna to his chosen disciple. Initiated with the sacred mantra *"Shri Krishnah Sharanam Mama"* (I take refuge in Krishna), Soordas accompanied his guru to Vrindavan, where he was appointed the chief singer at Shreenathji's temple at Govardhan.

When Soordas sang of Krishna's childhood pranks, the imagery was so vivid that devotees felt they could actually see Krishna right in front of their eyes. His fame grew so immense that one day, his own estranged brothers came to the temple. "We are your brothers. Do you recognise us? We have sinned against you. Forgive us."

Soordas embraced them warmly. "Dear, dear brothers! Don't put me to shame. I have nothing but love for you." When they begged him to return home, he smiled, "Have no regrets. My life is fuller and richer. All mankind is my family."

Even at the royal court in Agra, the great Emperor Akbar was moved to tears when his famous court musician, Tansen, sang one of Soordas's compositions. Desiring to meet the blind bard, Akbar sent a messenger. But Soordas politely declined, saying, "I sing only at Krishna's court. I will not leave Brij."

Impressed by such devotion, Emperor Akbar personally traveled to meet him. Entranced by Soordas's singing, Akbar offered to gift him the village of Parasoli and the surrounding lands. But the saint gently refused. "The whole world belongs to Krishna. Where is the need to transfer a part of it to me? Please do not ask me to come to your court. That is my only request."

"He is indeed a great fakir," Akbar murmured in deep respect.

Aspiring poets, including the great Tulsidas, would come to seek the blessings of the singing Mahatma. During his stay at Mathura, Soordas composed a new song on Krishna every day, flawlessly describing the exact clothing and ornaments the deity wore, despite being completely blind. 

To test him, the temple priests once left the deity completely unadorned. That morning, Soordas confidently began his song: *"Today I see my Lord unadorned..."* 

The crowd gasped in awe. "He is blind to the world but can see the Lord! He has been blessed with divine vision."

Soordas spent the rest of his days at the temple of Shreenath. His beautiful songs, composed in the local *Brij Boli* dialect, touched the hearts of millions and became so popular that they were sung from Rajasthan and Punjab in the West, all the way to Assam in the East, cementing his legacy as a legendary poet-saint of India.
