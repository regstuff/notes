# The Prophecy

In the ancient kingdom of Varanasi, the beloved King lay on his deathbed. Surrounding him were his grieving queen, who was expecting their second child, his ambitious eldest son, Prince Suryanemi, and his most trusted minister, Goshthila. 

"I have lived well and I'm not afraid of death," the dying King whispered weakly. "And yet... if only I knew that all would be well with my dear queen, who is expecting my child."

"O King, there is nothing to worry about," Minister Goshthila assured him gently. "The queen will be safe. She will bear a son who will one day rule over the kingdom."

Upon hearing this prophecy, Prince Suryanemi's eyes narrowed with dark jealousy. *Rule over the kingdom?* he thought furiously. *My kingdom? No! Never!*

A few days later, the King passed away, and Suryanemi was crowned the new monarch. But the prophecy gnawed at him. Soon after the coronation, he summoned Goshthila in secret. 

"You heard the prophecy, didn't you?" King Suryanemi demanded coldly. "Goshthila, you will have my mother put to death."

Goshthila recoiled in horror. "Maharaj! Let us not be rash. Prophecies are not always fulfilled... and she might have a daughter. Let the queen live."

Suryanemi glared at him. "I am not prepared to take any chances."

Thinking quickly to save the queen's life, Goshthila proposed a chilling compromise. "Then shall I take the queen to my house so I can keep a close watch on her? If she has a son, we'll kill him. And your throne will be safe!"

"An excellent suggestion," the wicked king agreed. 

That very day, Goshthila took the heavily pregnant queen to his own home under the guise of protecting her. A week later, the queen gave birth to a beautiful, healthy boy. 

Goshthila paced the floor nervously, knowing he had to execute the King's cruel order. Just then, he heard a frustrated wail from outside. "O' A-A-OW-A-A! It's a girl! No! Not another girl!" 

It was a poor fisherman complaining about his newborn daughter. Goshthila rushed out. "Give her to me! I'll give you a newborn baby boy and a bag of gold for her," he offered desperately. The poor fisherman readily agreed to the exchange. 

Goshthila took the baby girl and brought her to the weeping queen. "I am taking away your son, but I've left you a daughter. Tell the world she is yours," he instructed kindly. 

He then reported back to King Suryanemi. "Maharaj! Your mother has had a girl." 

Suryanemi laughed in relief. "So the prophecy was false!"

Many years passed peacefully. The queen's foster daughter grew up and was given away in marriage. But the queen's heart ached for her true child. A few days after the wedding, she pleaded with Goshthila, "May I meet my son once? Just once?"

Unable to deny a mother's plea, Goshthila arranged for the young boy, who was now working as a fisherman, to deliver fish to the palace the next day. 

When the boy arrived, Goshthila sneaked him into the queen's chamber. But as the tearful mother embraced her long-lost son, King Suryanemi unexpectedly walked in. 

"Woman! What are you doing here?" he demanded. 

In her panic, the queen blurted out the truth. "My son! They're going to take away my son, Maharaj! I mean the son they exchanged for my daughter!"

Suryanemi's eyes widened as the realization hit him. The prophecy was true! He turned to his guards and roared, "That boy! Kill him!"

The young prince bolted out of the chamber, running for his life. "Chase him! Don't let him get away!" the king commanded his men. 

The boy ducked into the house of an old woman, pleading for help. Hiding him in a large laundry basket, she called out to a passing dhobi (washerman). The dhobi strapped the basket onto his donkey and calmly walked past the searching guards, dropping the boy off safely at the riverbank. 

From there, the boy walked to the next village. To confuse his relentless pursuers, he cleverly asked a cobbler to make him a pair of slippers with the heels facing the front. The trick worked for a while, but soon he found himself trapped against the high city wall. Spotting a drainage outlet, he bravely squeezed through the muck and swam into the river outside the city.

As he swam, a group of powerful underwater Nagas (serpent-men) spotted him and took him to their subterranean king. When the boy explained that Suryanemi's men were after him, the Naga King grew worried that the king's snake-charmers would attack his kingdom with spells. 

Not wanting to endanger his hosts, the brave prince volunteered to leave. "I'll go to the forests of Pingala," he offered. 

"The Yaksha Pingala will eat you up!" the Naga King warned. 

"I'd rather take that chance than be killed by the King's men," the boy replied, swimming away just as Suryanemi's snake-charmers arrived.

Deep in the dark forest of Pingala, the boy heard the ferocious barking of wild dogs. He quickly scrambled up a tall, sturdy tree. Just as he secured his footing, the terrifying, man-eating Yaksha, Pingala, arrived with his pack of vicious dogs. 

"I am he who eats any human who dares enter his forest, and I'm going to eat you," the Yaksha snarled. 

"As long as I live, you won't!" the boy shouted back bravely. 

"Well, I'll wait," Pingala grinned wickedly, settling down at the base of the tree. Soon, the monstrous Yaksha fell fast asleep, and his fierce dogs began dozing around him. 

The boy knew this was his only chance. He carefully took off his upper garment, which carried his strong human scent, and took careful aim. He threw the garment far away from the tree, right onto the sleeping Yaksha. 

The dogs, smelling the strong scent of their prey and thinking the boy had fallen from the tree, instantly pounced in a frenzy... and tore the wicked Yaksha to pieces before realizing their mistake! The dogs then scattered into the forest.

Climbing down, the boy wandered until he met a wizened old sage. "Ha! So wicked Pingala is dead!" the sage declared. "He was destined to be killed by a youth. You are alive, so he must be dead! Here, take this magical turban. It will protect you on your way out."

As the boy emerged from the forest, Suryanemi's soldiers spotted him. He ran desperately until he reached the edge of a steep, plunging canyon. With nowhere else to go, he scrambled down the treacherous cliff face. The soldiers, losing sight of him, assumed he had fallen to his death and happily reported the news to the King. 

"He's dead! At last, I can enjoy my throne in peace!" Suryanemi rejoiced. 

But that very night, as the cruel king was strolling in his private garden, a figure stepped out from the shadows. 

"You!" Suryanemi gasped, drawing his sword. 

"Here I am," the young prince said calmly, drawing his own blade. "Kill me if you can."

In the fierce duel that followed, the wicked King Suryanemi was finally slain. 

The boy then sought out the loyal Goshthila, who tearfully revealed the full truth of the dying King's prophecy. "Come," Goshthila said proudly. "Let us go and do what has to be done. The kingdom is without a king."

The brave young prince was rightfully crowned King of Varanasi. Ruling with wisdom, courage, and compassion, he looked after his subjects even better than his father had done, fulfilling the great prophecy at last.
