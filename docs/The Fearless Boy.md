# The Fearless Boy

Samkicca was a novice who lived with a community of monks. They used to beg for food and eat it together at the monastery. One day, as they were about to eat, Samkicca noticed a stranger watching them. "Revered sir, we have a guest," he told the elder monk. "We will share what we have with him, Samkicca."

The stranger explained he had come from a distant place — he had been staying with his younger daughter, but she had fallen on hard times, and he was now on his way to his elder daughter's house. The monks fed him, and he was struck by how good the simple almsfood was — better, he thought, than anything he could earn even working from dawn to dusk. "Revered sir, I would like to live with you," he said. "I shall perform any task you assign me." The elder agreed, and the stranger stayed on at the monastery.

Several weeks later, though, he grew restless, longing to see his daughter and grandchildren. "Will these monks give me permission to visit her?" he wondered, then caught himself: "Why do I have to ask them at all?" He gathered his belongings and quietly left, his path leading across a forest.

Halfway through the forest, robbers surrounded him. "I don't have anything of value. See for yourselves," he pleaded, but they tied him to a post regardless. "The forest spirit has to be appeased by a human sacrifice," the chief announced. "Have mercy on me, master!" the man begged. "A wretch like me would make a poor sacrifice! Even the spirit would be offended!" Thinking quickly, he told them of a monastery nearby where thirty men of noble birth lived. "Offer one of them instead and you will please the spirit beyond measure." The robbers agreed and let him go.

At the monastery, the robber chief demanded a victim for their sacrifice. One by one, every single monk stepped forward to offer himself in place of the others — "Take me!" "No, me!" "Me!" — each refusing to let another die. Watching this, the man who'd betrayed them muttered bitterly that he too would have been brave, if not for his daughters and grandchildren. Disgusted, the chief turned on him: "Depart from my sight, you wretched man!" Then, to the monks: "I will take the youngest among you." "That is me," several claimed at once. "You are mistaken, brother," they told each other — until the truth came out: it was Samkicca, the young novice, who insisted he was truly the youngest. The elder monks tried to stop him — "You are but a novice" — but Samkicca would not be dissuaded. "I am the youngest person here, revered sir. I shall go. Do not stop me, I entreat you." And Samkicca departed with the robbers.

At their forest shrine, Samkicca sat down and immediately went into a deep trance. "He is saintly and deserves a merciful death," one robber said, raising his sword for a single clean blow. It struck — and shattered. "My hand slipped," the chief said, bewildered, and tried again with a fresh sword, aiming to cleave his head in two. That sword shattered as well. "He is no ordinary mortal," the chief realized, shaken. "I have broken my sword on your head. Yet you remain so calm. Have you no fear?"

Samkicca came out of his trance. "He that is free of desire has no mental suffering," he said. "To one who is free of attachment, death has no terror." Moved, the robber chief knelt before him. "I shall give up my lawless life and follow you." "We too," the others echoed. And Samkicca, who had gone into the forest with a band of robbers, returned to the monastery with a band of disciples.

**The Two Disciples.** An elder had two disciples, one lazy and one hardworking. One morning the hardworking disciple tried to wake his lazy companion to fetch water for their master's bath, but got no response — "Oh, he sleeps like a log!" — and fetched and heated the water himself. When the lazy disciple finally woke and saw the steam rising, he strolled in and told the master, "The water for your bath is ready," taking full credit for his brother's work. This happened again and again, until the hardworking disciple decided, "It's time I exposed him." He heated the water as usual, then quietly poured most of it out before the lazy disciple woke to see the last of the steam and once again claimed the credit. When the master went to bathe and found barely any water, the truth came out. "You must never take credit for the work another has done," the elder scolded. Offended rather than repentant, the lazy disciple stormed off in a huff, never to return — while the diligent disciple continued his studies and himself became a great teacher in time.

**The Great Architect.** Prince Bodhi was delighted with his magnificent new palace and lavished praise on the architect who built it — until, questioning him, he learned that the man had never built anything so fine for anyone else, and never would again. Overhearing this, the prince's friend Sanjikaputta warned the architect privately: talent so rare made him a threat, since the prince meant to have him killed so that no other patron could ever commission a comparable palace. "Your talent is a sword at your throat," Sanjikaputta warned. "Be warned!"

When the prince next asked if the palace was finished, the architect said no — one thing remained, something never yet dreamt of — and asked for seasoned timber, working now in strict privacy, allowing only his wife and son near. In secret, he carved an enormous wooden bird large enough to carry passengers, and readied it for flight. Sensing the prince's soldiers closing in with suspicion, he sent word to his wife: sell everything of value, and bring their son and all their gold to him the next day.

When they arrived, the architect loaded his family into the great wooden bird. "Are we going to fly?" his son asked. "Yes, my son." He pulled a lever, and the mechanical bird lifted into the air just as the prince's soldiers, realizing his escape, ordered archers to shoot it down. Arrows thudded harmlessly into the bird's wooden frame as it soared out of range, and the architect brought his family down safely in another country — where he resumed his career, building palace after palace, each finer than the last, free from any prince's jealousy.
