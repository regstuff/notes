# Mahavira: The Great Conqueror

About 2,500 years ago in Kundagram, near Vaishali in Bihar, ruled King Siddhartha and his queen, Trishala. The royal couple already had a son named Nandivardhana. Soon after his birth, Queen Trishala experienced several strange, vivid dreams featuring auspicious symbols—an elephant, a fish, a bull, and a lion. 

When King Siddhartha consulted the royal astrologers, they rejoiced. "We are fortunate," they declared. "Your queen will bear a son who is destined to become great."

The entire kingdom celebrated for ten days when the new prince was born. King Siddhartha proudly named him Vardhamana, for the kingdom had seen growing peace and prosperity since his birth. 

Prince Vardhamana grew into an exceptionally brilliant and handsome youth, mastering his lessons with astonishing ease. But he was also celebrated for his immense courage. One day, while playing with his friends, a venomous cobra slithered into their path. The other boys screamed and fled in terror, but Vardhamana stood calmly. As the cobra reared up, the prince gently knelt and caressed its hood. Pacified by his touch, the snake quietly slithered away.

"You are the bravest of us all!" his friends cheered. They hoisted him onto their shoulders to parade him through the streets. 

However, a local bully grew jealous of the attention. Challenging Vardhamana, the bully violently threw him to the ground. Unfazed, Vardhamana swiftly overpowered the larger boy, pummelling him until he ran away in defeat. The admiring crowd gave the prince a new title: "Mahavira"—the Great Warrior.

His legendary bravery only grew. When a rogue elephant ran amuck in the city streets, sending terrified citizens fleeing for their lives, Mahavira walked boldly up to the towering beast. With a mere, compassionate touch, he calmed the mad elephant completely. 

Despite the love, glory, and adulation showered upon him, Mahavira felt a deep, profound sadness. "The world is torn by differences of caste, colour, creed, and social status," he reflected from his balcony. "As a result, man is always sorrowful. There must be a way out."

He approached his parents to seek their blessing to renounce his princely life and become an ascetic. Queen Trishala was aghast. "No, Mahavira, never! If I am parted from you, I shall die of grief!"
Moved by his mother's tears, Mahavira promised, "I will not talk of going away as long as both of you are alive."

Years later, when Mahavira was twenty-eight, both his parents passed away. His elder brother, Nandivardhana, ascended the throne. When Mahavira sought his permission to leave, his grieving brother pleaded, "We have just lost our parents. If you too go away, I shall be desolate. Wait for a year or two." 

Mahavira patiently waited. Two years later, with his brother's heavy-hearted blessing, he distributed all his wealth to the poor and needy. After a grand feast, he was carried in a royal palanquin to a nearby park. There, beneath an Ashoka tree, Mahavira removed his magnificent ornaments and princely garments. He plucked out his hair in five handfuls, offered his respects to all liberated souls, and began his life as a wandering ascetic.

For twelve agonizing years, Mahavira roamed the land completely naked. He patiently endured insults, mockery, and brutal physical assaults from villagers who mistook him for a thief or an idle vagabond. Crowds beat him, dogs were set upon him, and mud was flung at him, but Mahavira remained serene. He became indifferent to pleasure and pain, surviving on severe fasts and maintaining strict silence. 

Once, in the Bohad jungle, villagers warned him not to take a specific path because it was infested with a deadly serpent. Fearless, Mahavira walked directly into the serpent's territory. When the furious snake reared up to bite him, Mahavira simply stared at it with profound compassion. Overwhelmed by his peaceful aura, the serpent lowered its head and slithered away, seemingly ashamed of its own anger.

Finally, after twelve years of wandering, Mahavira sat in deep meditation under a Sal tree on the banks of the Rijuwalika River near the Parshvanath hills. It was there that he attained *Kevala Jnana*—complete, infinite, and supreme enlightenment. He had discovered the root cause of sorrow and the path that leads away from it. He was now a *Jina*, a spiritual conqueror. 

The very first and greatest principle of Mahavira's religion was *Ahimsa* (non-violence). "One should not harm or hurt any living being in thought or action," he preached. 

As a Jina, Mahavira traveled across Northern India, delivering profound sermons. King Bimbisara of Magadha, deeply moved by his teachings, asked how a king could strive for *Nirvana* (liberation).
"Simply possess the Three Jewels," Mahavira explained. "Right Belief, Right Knowledge, and Right Conduct. You must vow not to hurt anyone, not to lie, not to steal, to remain celibate, not to own property, and to spend time each day in self-analysis and meditation."

Unlike the rigid society of his time, Mahavira welcomed everyone to his discourses. He broke down barriers of caste, age, class, and gender, happily admitting outcastes into his *Sangha* (monastic order) and appointing his female cousin to head the community of female disciples.

"Master, how can I attain Nirvana?" a commoner once asked.
"Every man has a right to, and can attain Nirvana by his own efforts," Mahavira answered, "without the help of any supreme authority or mediatory priests. You attain it by controlling your passions and conquering your *Karma*."

For thirty years, Mahavira preached his message of peace, non-violence, and self-reliance, succeeding in converting vast regions of Northern India to Jainism. Finally, at the age of seventy-two, in the town of Pavapuri in Bihar, Mahavira attained Nirvana, freeing his soul forever from the cycles of misery and pain.
