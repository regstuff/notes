# Tenali Raman

Long ago, when Krishna Deva Raya ruled over Vijayanagara, there lived in the village of Tenali a poor, lazy lad named Ramalingam. His father was dead, and though his mother needed his help, he did little but idle under the trees all day.

One afternoon a wandering sadhu found him stretched out in the shade and scolded him for wasting his time while everyone else toiled in the fields. "I'd love to work, sir," Ramalingam said, "but my health is poor." It was a lie, but the sadhu believed him and, out of pity, offered to teach him a mantra that would call forth the goddess Mahakali herself, so that he could ask her to bless him with good health.

"I'll learn the mantra all right," Ramalingam thought privately, "but it's plenty of food I'll ask for. That's the blessing I need." That night he ran to the temple, sat before the idol, and repeated the chant a hundred thousand times. At last the goddess appeared before him — eight skull-garlanded heads, blazing with light. The moment he saw her, a funny thought struck him, and instead of falling in terror at her feet, he burst out laughing.

Furious at the impertinence, Mahakali demanded he explain himself. "Forgive me, Goddess," he said between fits of laughter, "but I could not stop this one nose when I had a cold. What would you do, I wondered, if your thousand noses ran?" The goddess could not help herself — she was amused despite her anger, and admitted it would indeed be funny to watch her two hands battle uselessly against a thousand runny noses. Delighted by his fearlessness, she blessed him: "May you always succeed in making people laugh. Vikata Kavi shall be your name" — a name, she pointed out with pleasure, that read the same backwards as forwards.

Then she offered him a boon. From one golden cup she poured the sweet milk of learning; from another, the sour curds of wealth (offered as curds, she explained, because wealth is rarely won by pleasant means). "Choose one," she said. Ramalingam asked to taste both before deciding — and the moment she held the cups out, he drank them clean before she could stop him. Too late, the goddess realised she'd been tricked. Ramalingam bowed low. "The one without the other would have been useless to me. I had to have both." She forgave him, though not without a warning: wit and wealth would bring him prosperity — and prosperity would bring him jealous enemies.

Pleased with himself, Ramalingam went home and lay awake turning over a new ambition. "This tiny village is no place for a man of my attainments," he told himself. "I must enter the court of Vijayanagara." His friends laughed at him — the palace guards, they said, would throw a rustic like him straight back out. He wasn't deterred; he simply needed to find a patron first.

His chance came when the Rajguru, the king's own guru, arrived in Tenali to worship at a local shrine. Raman offered to serve him, and when the old priest — assuming this was just some ignorant village bumpkin — set off to bathe in the river, Raman trailed after, carrying his towel. As soon as the Rajguru waded in, Raman snatched his clothes off the bank and hid them behind a rock. "Something tells me I'll soon enter the court," he thought, "guards or no guards."

The shivering priest had no choice but to bargain. Raman's price: the Rajguru must let himself be carried into the city on Raman's shoulders, past the king's own palace. Grumbling but freezing, the old man agreed. Riding into Vijayanagara astride a stranger's back, the Rajguru was spotted by the king himself, who was taking the morning air on his terrace. "Which self-respecting man would carry another on his back?" the king wondered — then recognised his own guru and, alarmed, sent guards to investigate. Raman slid the Rajguru down at the king's feet and bowed. "Respected sir, I have tried you enough. You are truly a holy one. I shall serve you for the rest of my life," the mortified Rajguru declared, more to save face than out of sincerity — and hoisted Raman onto his own back to carry him home, just to be rid of him quickly.

Before the king, Raman told the whole story without shame. Krishna Deva Raya, delighted by his boldness, appointed him court jester on the spot — with one condition. "If you ever go too far with your wit," the king warned, "you shall be hanged." He handed over a bag of gold besides, so Raman could set up a household in the city with his mother and wife.

Once settled, eager to keep the king's favour, Raman began leaving home early and returning late. One moonlit night, walking back from court, he noticed two shadowy figures lurking near his house — thieves, clearly, waiting for the household to sleep. Instead of raising an alarm, he strode in and loudly complained to his wife about a supposed wave of robberies plaguing the city, warning her to lock away every valuable. His wife caught on at once. Together they filled an old trunk with a heavy grinding-stone, hauled it to the well in the yard, and dropped it in with a tremendous splash — "so no thief will ever find it" — before blowing out the lamps.

The thieves, who had heard every word, crept to the well the moment the house went dark, certain the trunk of treasure lay just beneath the surface. All night they cranked the well-wheel, draining it bucket by bucket, groaning about their aching backs but refusing to give up. Just before dawn, with his fields at last well watered from the emptied well, Raman strolled out to thank them for their labour. The startled thieves dropped their ropes and fled without looking back. At court the next day, the king laughed until he wept at the tale, and rewarded Raman with another bag of gold — for the joke alone.

Not everyone found Raman so amusing. As his influence grew, courtiers muttered that a man so free with his jokes at the king's expense would one day go too far and lose his head. Their chance to see him fall seemed to arrive when the king ordered a private Krishnaleela performance for himself and the queen, with guards posted at the palace gate and again at the hall door, under strict orders to let no one in — not even, though he didn't say so, his own jester.

Raman, turned away at the gate and burning with curiosity, talked his way past the first guard by promising to split "the reward" he claimed the king had summoned him to collect. He struck the same bargain with the second guard at the hall door. Once inside, though, he realized there was no reward waiting for him at all — only a play in progress. Thinking fast, he snatched up a stick and began thrashing the startled lead actor, who howled for mercy loudly enough to bring the performance to a screeching halt.

The furious king ordered a hundred lashes for the outrage — until Raman, dragged before him, begged that two friends waiting outside be allowed to share the punishment, since he was "honour bound" to split anything he received with them. Baffled, the king had the two guards fetched in, and Raman promptly explained how each had let him pass in exchange for half his "reward." Realizing he'd been played twice over — first by his own bribed guards, then by his own jester's wit — the king couldn't decide whether to be angrier or more amused. In the end amusement won: the dishonest guards were given fifty lashes each and dismissed, and Raman himself walked away with a bag of gold for exposing them.

Thus, again and again, did Raman of Tenali turn the king's anger to laughter and his own mischief into reward — the cleverest fool the court of Vijayanagara ever kept.
