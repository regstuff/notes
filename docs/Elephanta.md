# Elephanta

Beyond Bombay harbour, across the blue waters, lies the green isle of Elephanta. A short walk uphill takes you to the cave temples, cut out of solid rock by the hand of man. Inside the main cave may be seen some of the finest sculptures of Indian art carved about 1300 years ago. 

These caves were carved during the reign of the Mauryas of the Konkan, who were defeated in a great naval battle off Elephanta by the Chalukyan King, Pulakesin II. After being ruled by several Hindu dynasties, the island fell to the Sultans of Gujarat and passed from them to the Portuguese whose soldiers damaged many of the sculptures by using them for target practice. The island was then taken by the Marathas. 

The early names of this island were Gharapuri and Sripuri. When the Portuguese first landed on the island, they saw a massive stone elephant. "This is unique! A stone elephant to receive us!" they marvelled. "The island should be called Elephanta!" 
The stone elephant was later removed by the British to the Victoria Gardens at Bombay. 

Of the seven caves at Elephanta, the main cave is the most important. It is dedicated to Lord Shiva, the Great God of the Hindu Trinity. This Amar Chitra Katha tells the legends depicted in some of the sculptures found on the island. 

## Shiva Nataraja
In the main cave, the first figure at the right is that of Shiva as Nataraja. His dance symbolises the eternal motion of the universe. 

Long, long ago, in the forest of Taragam, there lived ten thousand rishis who by virtue of their penances had become very powerful. "We are greater even than the Devas! We are invincible!" they boasted. 
At Kailasa, Shiva turned to Vishnu. "You and I must subdue these arrogant sages." Shiva took the form of a handsome, young yogi and Vishnu that of the yogi's charming wife. 

As soon as they appeared before the rishis, the sages were captivated by the beautiful wife. "I cannot concentrate on the sacrifice," they muttered, leaving their rituals unfinished. Meanwhile, the handsome yogi had the same disastrous effect on their wives. 
Suddenly the rishis came to their senses. They realised that they were being deluded by some supernatural power. "May you and your magical powers be cursed!" they yelled. "And cursed be your wife, the serpent in a woman's guise!" Their curses, however, were of no avail. 

So they prepared a sacrificial fire, chanting mantras. Suddenly, a ferocious tiger sprang from the fire. Unperturbed, Shiva caught it in an iron grip, flayed it, and draped its skin over his body. 
The next moment, a poisonous snake emerged from the fire and darted at Shiva. He calmly picked it up and wore it as a garland. 
Hardly had he done so when an evil dwarf goblin emerged. In reply, Shiva dashed the evil creature to the ground, assumed his own form, and began to dance on the goblin's back. 
Good had triumphed over evil! The rishis were humbled. "He has taught us a lesson in humility! Glory to the Great Shiva!"

## The Killing of Andhaka
The next panel depicts the killing of the demon Andhaka. 
The asura Andhaka was the son of Sage Kashyapa and his wife Diti. By severe penances, he pleased Brahma and won a powerful boon. He then began harassing the Devas, who fled to Shiva for help. 
"I will teach Andhaka a lesson," Shiva promised. 

When Andhaka learnt of Shiva's intentions, he sent the evil Nila to destroy him, but Nila was slain. Meanwhile, Andhaka had sneaked into Kailasa to carry away Parvati. 
"Come away with me. I am greater than Shiva," he boasted. 
At that moment, Shiva returned. "I am ready to receive your arrows!" Andhaka roared. 
Shiva's arrows struck the asura, but due to his boon, each drop of his blood that touched the earth became another asura. "Andhaka is immortal! Andhaka can never die!" his clones cheered. 

Vishnu, who had come to watch the combat, sent his Sudarshana Chakra flying to destroy the newly spawned asuras. Meanwhile, as Shiva attacked and killed Andhaka, he held a bowl to catch the asura's blood, making sure that not a drop fell to the ground, so that no new asuras would spring up. Shiva thus vanquished the forces of darkness. 

## The Fiery Lingam
In one end of the main cave is the shrine dedicated to Shiva in the form of a lingam. It is guarded on all sides by dwarapalas (gate-keepers). 

One day, Brahma, the Creator, and Vishnu, the Preserver, were arguing about which of them was the greater. Suddenly, there appeared an endless fiery pillar, a lingam, without beginning or end. 
"I will find the origin of this lingam," Vishnu said, taking the form of a boar and burrowing into the earth. 
"And I shall find the end!" Brahma declared, taking the form of a swan and flying into the heavens. 

After countless years, the exhausted boar returned. "I have failed. I could not find its beginning." 
Neither did Brahma succeed. But he thought, "I must not admit that I could not see the end." Suddenly he saw a Ketaki flower falling from the heavens. "You must come with me to Vishnu and say that I picked you up from Shiva's head," he plotted. 
Brahma assumed his real form and told Vishnu, "I saw the end of the fiery lingam. And I was a witness to it," presenting the flower. 
"I bow to you, Lord Brahma. You are the greatest of us all," Vishnu conceded. 

Suddenly, Shiva emerged from the centre of the fiery lingam. "Brahma, you have been dishonest! From now on, none shall honour you. Nor will you have your own temple or festival. And you, Ketaki, shall no longer be used for worship in my temple. 
"Dear Hari (Vishnu)," Shiva turned to Vishnu, "you are a lover of truth! I am pleased with you. You will always be honoured as I am." 
And to this day there are temples and festivals connected with the worship of Shiva and Vishnu but few worship the Creator. 

## The Marriage of Shiva and Parvati
One of the most exquisite panels at Elephanta is that of the marriage of Shiva and Parvati. 
Uma (Parvati) was the daughter of Himavan and Mena. To woo and win Shiva, the ascetic god, Uma took to meditation and practised the severest of austerities. 
One day, an old Brahmana appeared before her. "You are young and beautiful. Why have you renounced the world?"
"To win the love of Shiva."
"That mendicant? He has matted hair and decks himself with serpents! He is uncouth..."
"How dare you speak of my loved one so! Go away!" Parvati flared up. 

Suddenly, the old man changed his form. It was the Great Lord himself! "It is you, my Lord!" she gasped. 
And Parvati was married to Shiva. Himavan gave the bride away. Brahma acted as priest, while Vishnu stood by watching the beautiful ceremony. 

## The Descent of the Ganga
The next panel is of Shiva as Gangadhara, showing the descent of the Ganga to the earth. 
Sagara, a great ruler, performed the Ashwamedha Yagna and let loose a sacrificial horse. Indra, afraid of Sagara's growing power, stole the horse and hid it in the hermitage of Rishi Kapila. Sagara's sixty thousand wicked sons found the horse and attacked the sage. 
The powerful sage opened his eyes in anger and reduced them to ashes. 

Generations later, Sagara's descendant Bhagiratha undertook severe penances to bring the celestial river Ganga down to earth, as only her purifying touch could redeem his ancestors' ashes. 
Brahma agreed but warned, "Shiva must break her fall, or the earth will be washed away."
Bhagiratha prayed to Shiva, who agreed. As the proud Ganga hurtled down from the heavens, Shiva caught the turbulent river in his matted locks. Amused by Ganga's attempts to escape, Parvati smiled with pride at Shiva's strength. 
The force of Ganga's fall broken, she flowed gently onto the earth and over the ashes of Bhagiratha's ancestors, bringing them back to life. 

## Mahesha
In the centre of the northern wall is the gigantic figure of Shiva as Mahesha, the Supreme God, combining in himself the qualities of Creator, Preserver and Destroyer. In the centre is Shiva, the Creator, holding a Matulunga, the symbol of creation. On our right is Shiva, the Preserver, holding a lotus. On our left is Shiva as Rudra, the Destroyer, holding a cobra. Yet out of destruction comes creation and the cycle of life. 

## Ardhanarishwara
The next panel is that of Ardhanarishwara Shiva, when Shiva became one with his wife, Parvati, symbolising the unity of the male and the female elements in the universe. 

## Mount Kailasa
The panel beyond shows a domestic scene with Shiva and Parvati on Mount Kailasa. Parvati, pretending to be angry, turns her face away from Shiva. 
The next panel shows the demon-king, Ravana, overcome with pride in his own power, attempting to uproot and lift Mount Kailasa. As the mountain shook, Parvati trembled with fear, but Shiva smiled. Pressing the mountain down with his foot, Shiva imprisoned the arrogant Ravana beneath it. 

This sculpture also shows Shiva with his three eyes, the third eye being the eye of anger. Once, Parvati playfully went behind him and closed both his eyes. She thereby shut out the light in the three worlds and all living things began to perish. The earth was plunged in darkness! 
But a third eye instantly appeared on Shiva's forehead, above Parvati's hands, and the universe was saved. 

The last panel in the main cave shows Shiva as the Great Yogi, sitting on a lotus in meditation. Beyond, facing the lingam shrine, is a pedestal where once stood Nandi, Shiva's vehicle, the bull.
