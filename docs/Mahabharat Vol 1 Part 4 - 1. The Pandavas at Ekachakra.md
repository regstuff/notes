# The Pandavas at Ekachakra

After escaping the burning mansion of Varanavata, the Pandavas and their mother, Kunti, journeyed through dense forests until they arrived at the peaceful town of Ekachakra. Guided by Sage Vyasa, they adopted the guise of humble Brahmana mendicants and took up residence in the modest home of a local Brahmana family. 

For some time, they lived quietly, sustaining themselves on the alms they collected from the townsfolk. Every evening, they would pool their collected food and divide it into two halves—one half for the perpetually ravenous Bheema, and the other half shared among the rest of the family.

One day, while the four brothers were out seeking alms, Bheema stayed behind with Kunti. Suddenly, the quiet afternoon was pierced by the sound of agonizing wails coming from the inner chambers of their host's house. 

Deeply concerned, Kunti approached the Brahmana and his family. "You have provided us safe shelter," she said gently. "I wish to know the cause of your woe to decide if it can be removed."

The weeping Brahmana explained their dire situation. "A mighty, cannibalistic Rakshasa named Baka rules over the nearby forest," he sighed heavily. "He protects our town from enemies, but exacts a terrible tribute. Every week, a different household must send him a cartload of rice, two buffaloes, and the man who drives the cart. Today, it is my family's turn. I cannot abandon my wife or my innocent children, so we must all perish together."

Kunti smiled reassuringly. "Do not fear for me or my sons," she told the astonished Brahmana. "I have five sons. Let one of them go to the Rakshasa with the quittance."
The Brahmana strongly protested, refusing to send a guest to his death, but Kunti insisted. She knew Bheema's immense strength and was confident he could easily slay the demon. 

When Bheema was informed of the plan, he readily consented. He loaded the cart with the massive feast of rice and drove the two buffaloes into the dark forest. 

Upon reaching the clearing where the demon resided, Bheema decided to thoroughly infuriate the monster. Instead of presenting the food, he calmly sat down on the cart and began to eat the enormous feast himself! 

Smelling the food, the monstrous Baka emerged from the trees. Seeing a mere human devouring his meal, the cannibal let out a terrifying roar. "Who is this villain eating up my meal?" he bellowed, rushing forward with upraised arms.

Bheema completely ignored him. Without so much as a glance, he continued eating the rice cheerfully. Blinded by rage, the Rakshasa brought his massive fists down on Bheema's back. Bheema barely flinched and kept eating. 

Furious, Baka uprooted a massive tree and hurled it at Bheema. Having finally finished his meal, Bheema wiped his hands, caught the tree effortlessly in his left hand, and hurled it right back. 

A brutal fight erupted. The two giants grappled and struggled, tearing apart the surrounding forest as they fought. But the mighty Bheema quickly gained the upper hand. He pressed the demon down with his knees, seized his neck, and bent him double with terrifying force. Baka expelled a horrifying roar of pain and fell dead. 

Terrified by the roar, Baka's monstrous kinsmen emerged from the woods. But when they saw their invincible leader dead on the ground, they immediately surrendered to Bheema, promising never to prey on human flesh again. 

Under the cover of darkness, Bheema carried the lifeless cannibal to the town gates and deposited him there unobserved. The next morning, the citizens of Ekachakra awoke to find their oppressor dead, glutted in gore. Thousands thronged the spot, marveling at the superhuman deed.

When the townsfolk rushed to the Brahmana's house to inquire about his survival, the wise host kept the Pandavas' secret. "A noble Brahmana, a master of occult lore, came to me and offered to deliver the food," he told the crowd. "He must have slain the brute."

The town celebrated their newfound freedom with a grand festival. However, recognizing that the extraordinary deed would soon draw unwanted attention and potentially reveal their true identities to Duryodhana's spies, Yudhishthira gathered his family. 
"We have lived here comfortably," he told Kunti and his brothers, "but it is time for us to move on before we are discovered."

Leaving Ekachakra behind, the Pandavas continued their journey in disguise, silently awaiting the right moment to reclaim their destiny.
