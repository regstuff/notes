# The Green Demon and Other Sufi Tales

A young man wandered into a field where a group of men were harvesting wheat, and fled screaming. "Run, sir! There is a demon in the field!" "Where?" "Right there in the middle of the field!" The man ran to the spot and found only a watermelon. "Don't be afraid. I'll kill your demon for you," he told the terrified peasants, and cut it off its stalk, sliced it, and bit into it as he walked toward them.

"He eats demons! He'll kill and eat us next!" the peasants cried, and drove him away with sticks, convinced he'd gone mad. He fled, baffled by their terror.

The following harvest season, another melon appeared, and the peasants fled again — until a wise man passing by stopped to ask what they were running from. "A demon, sir." "Can you describe it to me?" "It has a large, round, green head... and long, long arms, sir." The wise man guessed correctly that their demon was only a melon, but wanted to be sure, and told the peasants he would go and look at it himself, pretending fear "to win their confidence." For several days he did nothing but watch it from a distance, until he judged it safe to touch. When it didn't move, he encouraged the peasants, one by one, to touch it themselves — "It's harmless. Absolutely harmless!" Puzzled about where it had come from, they eventually connected it to a bag of wheat seeds they'd sown, cut the melon open to find its own seeds, and planted a few. In time the seeds grew into more melons, which the wise man tasted and declared delicious and safe to eat. Soon the whole village was eating them happily — "how clever we are! We have discovered something new to eat!" Had the first man used the wise man's patient approach instead of forcing the fruit on them, he would have been the one to receive the credit for the discovery.

**The Perfectionist.** A carpenter who prided himself on the quality of his work was asked by a new father to build the finest cradle he had ever made. "You have come to the right man," the carpenter promised, envisioning a perfect creation. When the father returned a week later, the cradle wasn't ready — "it has to be a dream, don't forget, sir" — and week after week, the same answer came: "I am not satisfied with my work. Whatever I do must be perfect." By the time the child had outgrown the need for a cradle altogether, the father had simply given up asking. Years later, that same child grew up, married, and had a son of his own — and sent his own father back to the very same carpenter to finally collect the cradle that had been ordered for him at birth. The old carpenter refused to be rushed even now: "I take pride in my work, and I will not permit you or your father to rush me." Realizing it would be futile to argue with the perfectionist, the young man quietly left.

**The Thief, the Old Woman and the Priest.** A thief broke into the house of an old woman to see what he could steal, but was kept awake all night by her moaning — "Oh, this Dib-Dib is worse than the plague!" she groaned, over and over, apparently in the grip of some terrible disease. Terrified he'd caught it simply by breathing the air near her room, he fled home in a panic, certain he too had "the merciless Dib-Dib," and collapsed, feverish with fright, in front of his startled wife.

She rushed to a local priest for help, insisting he come at once. The priest, imagining claw marks from some wild beast, examined the thief and found nothing — no wound at all — and was baffled: the wife spoke of a spirit, the thief spoke of a disease. Deciding to investigate properly rather than guess, he went first to the old woman's house himself. Peering in her window, hearing her cries of "Dib-Dib," he began to shiver himself from sheer suggestion — and when the rattling window frame startled the old woman, she caught him spying and accused him furiously. "Your Dib-Dib has me too, in its grip," the priest wailed — until she scoffed and showed him the truth: a leaking tap, dripping *"dib... dib... dib"* into a puddle all night, nothing more.

Relieved and a little embarrassed, the priest asked her to keep his own folly quiet, and hurried on to the thief's house — where the man's wife scolded him for having "left him to die alone." The priest, unwilling to admit the truth, instead pretended he'd been off finding the cure, mumbled some invented words over a cup of water, and made the "afflicted" thief drink it — extracting, in exchange for the cure, a solemn promise never to steal again. The thief, thoroughly convinced he'd been saved from a deadly curse, kept his word from then on. The priest's reputation as a holy man remained intact, and the old woman, despite everything, never did find a way to make use of what she now knew about him.

**The Immature Critic.** A venerable old man walking down a street, lost in thought, was interrupted by a young man eager to share his opinion of Sufi masters. "I have no respect for them. I think they act mysterious and speak in riddles because they want to confuse us. I personally have no faith whatsoever in their wisdom," he declared confidently. The old man, unbothered, produced a ring and asked the young man to go sell it in the market for whatever he could get. Visiting stall after stall, the best offer the young man could get was a single silver coin — "are you a cheat or a fool?" one trader scoffed when he tried naming his own price instead. Returning empty-handed, he reported his failure. The old man then took the ring to a jeweller himself, who valued it at a thousand pieces of gold and offered to buy it on the spot. "What you know of Sufis," the old man told the young critic, "is about as much as those trinket-sellers know of precious stones. If you wish to value gems, my son, first become a jeweller." The venerable old man was none other than the Sufi sage Dhun-Nun.

**Sweet or Sour.** An orchard owner appointed a man to tend his pomegranate trees. Months later, walking through the orchard, the owner asked for a pomegranate to quench his thirst. The keeper plucked one and handed it over — sour. He tried another; even more sour. After several more equally bad fruits, the owner lost his temper: "You've worked here for months. Can't you tell which of the pomegranates is sweet?" The keeper answered simply: "I am sorry, sir. I was appointed to tend the trees, sir — not taste the fruit."
