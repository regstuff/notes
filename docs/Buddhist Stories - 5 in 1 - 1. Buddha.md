# Buddha

In the Himalayan foothills, Kapilavastu was a small but prosperous kingdom. The Sakyas ruled over it, and Suddhodana was their king. 

One day, his queen, Mayadevi, dreamt that a white elephant with six tusks pierced her womb. Ten months later, the queen was on her way to her father's house. As she was passing through a beautiful grove on the way to Lumbini, she commanded, "Stop! I would like to spend some time here."
The queen got down and started walking towards a majestic *sala* tree in the middle of the garden. Suddenly, she stopped. "I am in great pain. I must rest."
There, a baby was born to her. It was the full moon night of Vaisakha. There was profound silence all around. 

On hearing the joyous news, the king rushed to Lumbini and brought the mother and the child to the palace. The revered sage Asita came to the palace to see the baby. 
As the sage gazed upon the infant, tears filled his eyes. 
"I see tears in your eyes, sage Asita. Why are you unhappy?" King Suddhodana asked anxiously. 
"This boy will be a king of kings... or a great saint," Asita prophesied. "I am crying because I will not live to hear his great words."

Both the king and the queen were happy. On the fifth day, the king said, "Let us name him Siddhartha."
"And we will see that he becomes a king of kings and not a saint," the queen added. 
Immediately afterwards, the queen became seriously ill. On the seventh day after the birth of Siddhartha, she called for her sister. "Sister Prajapati, I shall soon leave this world. When I am gone, please be a kind mother to Siddhartha. Promise me."
"I promise," Prajapati wept. 

After a year had passed, the king said, "You have been a good mother to him, Prajapati."
"I love him dearly. I am proud to be his mother."

As Siddhartha grew, the king became increasingly anxious about the prophecy. "He spends so much time alone, meditating under that jambu tree. I don't like that."
"He says his playmates play cruel games," Prajapati explained. 

One day, as Siddhartha was walking along in the garden, a wounded swan fell from the sky. "Poor bird! I wonder whose arrow has hurt him," Siddhartha cried softly. He gently removed the arrow and tended to the bird's wound. 
Some time later, Devadatta, Siddhartha's cousin, arrived with his bow. "This is my bird. I shot it! Give it to me."
"Devadatta! It is such a lovely bird. Why do you wish to harm it?" Siddhartha asked. 
"The bird belongs to me. Give it back."
"I won't do that."

The matter was taken to the court. "Prince! What right have you to keep the bird?" Devadatta demanded. 
"Sir," Siddhartha addressed the judges, "if I had not removed the arrow, the bird would have died. The bird owes its life to me. Should the bird belong to the one who took its life or to the one who gave it life?"
"What the boy says is right," the head judge proclaimed. "The one who saved the life of the bird has a greater right. Devadatta, the bird belongs to the prince."
"Thank you, noble judges," Siddhartha smiled. 

Years rolled by. Dandapani, a Sakyan noble, arranged the *swayamvara* of his daughter, Yashodhara. Princes from far and near came to the event. Princess Yashodhara chose Siddhartha as her husband and placed the garland around his neck. 

The assembled princes were deeply hurt by Yashodhara's choice. They approached Dandapani. "Sir, the princess should not be married to Siddhartha. He is not a good fighter," one complained. 
"Sir, you should hold a test in archery. The princess should be given in marriage to the winner."

Dandapani tried to persuade his daughter. "Yashodhara! Choose someone else as your husband."
"Father, I have made my choice. Please agree to it," she replied firmly. 

When the news reached King Suddhodana, he felt sad. 
"Father, why are you sad?" Siddhartha asked. 
"The people don't think you are a good warrior."
"Father, let Dandapani hold a test. I will take part in it."
"I am happy to hear that. Your ancestors were great warriors, Siddhartha," the king beamed. 

Many gathered to witness the test. To everyone's astonishment, Siddhartha excelled in everything. "It is Siddhartha who has won in horse-riding!" the crowds cheered. "No one could equal him in archery too!"
Siddhartha and Yashodhara were married with great pomp. The king tried his best to provide all the comforts of life to Siddhartha, isolating him in a luxurious palace. 

Soon, a son was born to them. King Suddhodana was immensely pleased when he heard the news. "Lord! It is a boy!" the messenger announced. 
"Good! Now Siddhartha is tied to worldly life and will never think of becoming a saint," the king thought. 

One day, Siddhartha approached his father. "Father, I would like to go out of the palace and see more of the world."
"I will order a chariot for you. After it is ready, you can go out in it," the king agreed cautiously. He ordered all sights of unhappiness to be cleared from the streets. 

A few days later, in a beautiful chariot with four horses driven by his trusted charioteer Channa, Siddhartha drove through the streets of the city. "How handsome the prince is," the people whispered. "I have heard he is brave and fearless."

But as he drove further, Siddhartha saw an unexpected sight. "Channa, who is this? His head is white. He seems very weak. His skin is wrinkled."
"He is an old man, master! He is bent with age."
"Does everyone get old, Channa?"
"Yes, my lord! Everyone has to grow old."
Siddhartha became deeply troubled. "Will my Yashodhara also grow old? Will my strength go away with years?"

On another day's ride, he saw a man wailing by the roadside. "What is the matter with this man?"
"He is ill, my lord! He is crying with pain," Channa replied. 
"Is disease peculiar to him?"
"No, my lord! Anyone may fall ill in his lifetime."
"Even I?"
"Yes, master, even you."

Still another day, he saw a procession carrying a body wrapped in white cloth. "Why are they carrying that man, Channa?"
"He is dead, my lord!"
"Is he the only dead man? Or do others also die?"
"Everyone who is born has to die some day."
"I feel sick, Channa," Siddhartha whispered. "Let us return to the palace."

On the way back, he saw a man sitting peacefully under a tree. "Stop, Channa! Who is that? He looks calm and different from all the other men I have seen."
"He is a saint. He has given up a life of pleasure and pain in search of truth."

At the palace, Yashodhara noticed his distress. "Siddhartha! Please tell me the cause of your unhappiness!"
"Mother," Siddhartha later told Prajapati, "I have learnt that all things alive and beautiful keep changing. Men grow old. Men fall ill and die. I feel unhappy when I think of these things."

In the middle of the night, he finally made his decision. "I must find a way to end sorrow. I will go in search of truth, like that saint."
He woke his charioteer. "Channa, get my horse ready! I wish to ride out."
"Yes, master!"

Before leaving, the prince returned to the bedroom of his wife. Yashodhara and their infant child, Rahula, were fast asleep. "I can't kiss Rahula goodbye; that will awaken Yashodhara. I must go."
Siddhartha mounted his horse and rode out into the night, accompanied only by Channa. 

Once they were safely outside the city, Siddhartha got down from the horse. "Channa, take all my jewels and return to Kapilavastu."
He then cut off his long royal hair and walked off alone. 

Later, he saw a beggar on the road. "Halt, my good man! Take my rich clothes and give me yours."
"With pleasure, master!" the beggar agreed happily. 

Soon he reached Rajagriha, the capital of Magadha. He went from house to house silently waiting till the people offered him food. King Bimbisara observed him from his palace. "Look at that saint. He looks so handsome. Find out who he is."

As soon as the messengers brought news about Siddhartha's true identity, King Bimbisara personally went to meet him. "You seem to belong to a noble family. Your hand should not hold a begging bowl, but the reins of an empire. I will give you a high position in my kingdom. Come."
"O king, you are kind," Siddhartha replied gently. "But I cannot accept your invitation. I feel that life is full of sorrow. I wish to find a way to end all sorrow."
"If that is your wish, I pray that you find it. Please come and teach me when you have found the solution," Bimbisara requested respectfully. 

From Rajagriha, Siddhartha went in search of the great sages of those days. Not satisfied with their teachings, he entered the thick jungles of Urubilva, near modern-day Gaya. 

There were five hermits meditating in the jungles. When they saw him, they thought, "This man is keen on his goal. He is sure to succeed. Let us wait on him."
Siddhartha practiced extreme asceticism. He drank only water and ate only fruits and herbs. He slept on the hard ground. After some time, he started eating only one hemp grain every day. This made him dangerously weak. One day, when he had gone to bathe in the river, he realized, "I feel weak. I don't have strength to get up." He caught hold of a low branch of a tree and barely raised himself with its support. But as he began to walk away from the bank, he felt faint and collapsed. Slowly, he managed to get up. 

"I have come to believe that starving does not help in reaching the truth," he realized. 
The next day, as he sat beneath a banyan tree, Sujata, the daughter of a herdsman, came to that spot. She respectfully offered a bowl of nourishing food to Siddhartha. "Thank you for feeding me," he said. 

Later, the hermits confronted him. "How is it that you have started eating food again?"
"Starving the body is not going to help," Siddhartha explained. 
The five ascetics were deeply disappointed. "He does not deserve our respect. Yes, he wants the pleasures of life," they muttered, and abandoned him. 

Siddhartha now lived a lonely life. "They have left me. Let them go. But starving the body is not going to help. I am now sure of that."
He moved towards a great Bodhi tree and sat beneath it. "Come what may, I shall not move from this seat till I find a way to end sorrow."
Heat and cold, hunger and thirst troubled him. Rain lashed on him. Visions of the life of pleasure he had abandoned floated before his eyes. But nothing could tempt him. 

And then, in deep meditation, he finally saw the light. 
"I know. I know the truth now. The way to end sorrow is found."
Siddhartha became the Buddha, the Enlightened One. 

He spent seven weeks under the tree enjoying his state of perfect happiness and enlightenment. Then he went out into the world to teach others. First, he went to Banaras, in search of the five ascetics who had abandoned him in Urubilva. He found them in Deer Park. 
"Here comes Siddhartha," the ascetics scoffed. "He returned to a life of ease. Let us not talk to him."
But as soon as he came near, his radiant presence overwhelmed them. They got up and received him with deep respect. 
"I have come to tell you what I have found. Listen!" the Buddha said. "There is great sorrow in this world. This sorrow is because of desire. If you can free yourself from desire, you will be free from sorrow. I will show you the way to remove sorrow from the mind."
When they heard the Buddha, they became his first disciples. The *Sangha* (community of monks) thus came into being. 

After that, he returned to Urubilva and went to the house of Kasshyapa, a great Brahman who was a worshipper of Agni, the God of Fire. 
"What do you want?" Kasshyapa asked. 
"I want to spend a night here," Buddha replied. 
"You are welcome. Please come in."
"May I stay in the room where you keep the sacred fire?"
"Haven't you heard that the sacred fire is guarded by a fierce serpent at night? The serpent will bite you if you go near the fire."
"I am not afraid. Please allow me to spend the night there."

At last, Kasshyapa agreed. Buddha sat silently meditating before the fire. Kasshyapa went to sleep outside. 
Early next morning, Kasshyapa thought, "Let me go and look for him. He must be dead. Poor man!"
But when he went inside the room, he saw Buddha sitting perfectly peacefully. The light from the fire shone beautifully on his serene face. 
Awed by his spiritual power, Kasshyapa bowed. "I accept you as my master. Teach me."
Buddha taught Kasshyapa and many others in Urubilva. 

One day, a disciple informed him, "Master, I have heard that King Bimbisara will be celebrating a great animal yagna."
"Let us go to Rajagriha," Buddha decided. 

On their way to Rajagriha, they saw a large herd of sheep being driven along. There was a lame lamb struggling to keep up. Buddha gently lifted it in his arms. "Poor thing, it must have been suffering a lot of pain. Good man, where are you going?" he asked the shepherd. 
"I am going to Rajagriha. These sheep belong to the king. They are going to be sacrificed in the yagna—in the sacred fire."

King Bimbisara heard that Buddha was on his way to Rajagriha. He went forward with his ministers to meet him. Seeing him with Kasshyapa, Bimbisara thought, "He is in the company of Urubilva Kasshyapa. Does that mean Buddha is his disciple?"

When they came near, Buddha gently placed the lamb down. "You had expressed the wish to see me after I had found the way. Here I am," Buddha said to the king. 
"Holy one! I am about to begin a yagna. Great Kasshyapa and you are also welcome to take part in it."
"O king, killing of innocent animals cannot be a good deed. The way to happiness does not lie in yagna," Buddha advised softly. 

The king turned to Kasshyapa. "What has the great Kasshyapa to say? You are known as a fierce worshipper of fire."
Kasshyapa stepped forward and bowed deeply to Buddha. "I am a disciple of Buddha."
Bimbisara was astounded. "I see Buddha is the teacher! Don't you also believe in yagna?"
"Buddha has shown me the right way. Neither the worship of fire nor the sacrifice of dumb animals can make us free from sorrow."

The king turned to Buddha respectfully. "Master, let us hear your words of wisdom."
And Buddha taught them the path of peace and compassion. 
"Lord, I have given up the idea of performing the yagna. I take refuge in Buddha," Bimbisara declared. 
The next day, the king invited Buddha and his disciples for a royal meal at his palace. "Lord, I give Venuvana, my garden, as a gift to the Sangha. Please accept it."

One day, a grief-stricken woman named Krisha Gotami brought her dead child before Buddha. "Master, please bring it back to life. It is my only child."
"Control your grief," Buddha said gently. "Do as I say. Get me a few mustard seeds from any house where no death has taken place. And I will bring back to life your child."
Full of hope, Krisha Gotami went from house to house. 
"I lost my husband last year!" one woman wept. 
"I lost my two children," another sighed. 
"My mother died yesterday."
Disappointed, Krisha Gotami came back to Buddha. "Lord! I could not find a single place where no death has occurred."
"My child, all that is born, must die one day. There is ultimately nothing but sorrow in life. It is freedom from desire that frees us from sorrow." Realizing the universal truth, she found peace. 

After a few years, at Suddhodana's court in Kapilavastu, a noble informed the aging king, "Lord! Our prince has become famous. Many have become his disciples. They are spreading his teachings everywhere."
"Go to Siddhartha and tell him that I am growing old and wish to see him before I die," Suddhodana commanded. 

When Buddha reached Kapilavastu, the king greeted him emotionally. "Welcome my son! I wish you would become king."
"I am sorry. I have chosen the path of peace."
The king arranged for Buddha's stay in a grove nearby. The next morning, the king was horrified to hear a report. "Lord, the prince is going from house to house to receive alms in the city!!"
Suddhodana rushed out to confront him. "My son, must you beg?"
"But it is our custom."
"What do you mean? You are descended from a proud line of kings!"
"O great king, you claim descent from kings. My descent is from the Buddhas of old. They always begged for their food and lived on alms."

Once they reached the palace, Buddha spoke softly to all those that had gathered. His words brought peace to their hearts. Buddha noticed that his wife, Yashodhara, was not present. 
"Where is Yashodhara?" he asked. 
"She has refused to come," Prajapati explained. "She has been very unhappy ever since you left. She has cut her hair, wears simple clothes, and eats sparingly just as you did."
Accompanied by two disciples, Buddha went to Yashodhara's chamber. "I must try to heal the sorrow in her heart."
When Yashodhara saw Buddha, she broke down, fell at his feet, and wept. Then, remembering that others were present, she got up and sat at a little distance. Buddha spoke gentle words of comfort, easing her long pain. 

A week later, Yashodhara pointed out a monk to her young son. "Rahula, do you see the man sitting there in the centre? He is your father. Go to him and ask for your share of his property."
Rahula walked up to Buddha. "Father! My mother sent me to ask you for my share of your property."
Buddha turned to Sariputra, his chief disciple. "My son asks for his inheritance. Well then, take him in the Sangha."

After Rahula joined the Sangha, many young men of the royal Sakyan family also joined. Amongst them was Buddha's jealous cousin, Devadatta. He also moved about with Buddha. One day, Devadatta ambitiously suggested, "Master, you should rest. I shall lead the Sangha."
"No, Devadatta. The Sangha still needs my guidance," Buddha refused. 

Devadatta felt incredibly jealous. He went to Rajagriha and met Prince Ajatasatru, son of King Bimbisara. "Prince Ajatasatru! How long can you wait to become a king? Put your father in prison and be a king yourself."
Ajatasatru agreed. After imprisoning his father, he said, "It is nice to be a king. Thank you, Devadatta! If you want my help in anything, ask for it."
"I need your help, King Ajatasatru. Help me kill Buddha. I hate him."

Devadatta tried many ways to assassinate the Buddha. Once, as Buddha was meditating, a disciple yelled, "Look out! A big stone is rolling down towards Buddha."
"Sit where you are. No harm will come to me," Buddha said calmly. The heavy rock magically split into two, and the pieces fell harmlessly on either side of Buddha. 
Many of Devadatta's own followers witnessed this miracle and came to join the Sangha of Buddha. "The rock did not kill him. He looks frail, but he is great. Let us go to him," they said. 

Ajatasatru was furious. "I hear many of my followers have joined his Sangha. Why not set an elephant on him?"
He ordered an elephant keeper, "Intoxicate the massive elephant Nalagiri, and then let him loose in the path of Buddha."
The drunken beast caused havoc. "Run, run, Nalagiri is free! He has ruined half the town already. Twenty men have been killed!"
As Nalagiri charged down the street where Buddha was walking, the disciples panicked. "Master, let us hide. A wild elephant is coming this way."
"No, he won't harm us," Buddha said firmly. 
Nalagiri came thumping along and rushed towards Buddha in a mad fury. Buddha simply smiled and raised his hand peacefully. The raging elephant instantly calmed down and knelt reverently at his feet. 

Years later, Ajatasatru became an unhappy, guilt-ridden man and fell ill often. He asked his wise physician, Jivaka, "Jivaka, why do I suffer?"
"Your ailment is not physical but spiritual. Only Buddha can help you."
"Let us go to him, then. Where will he be now?"
"In the Amravana, at Vaishali... which belongs to Amrapali, the courtesan. Amrapali has gifted it to Buddha."

When Ajatasatru reached Amravana in the dead of night, he grew paranoid. "I heard no sound. Where is Buddha?"
"He is here with over twelve hundred disciples," Jivaka assured him. 
Ajatasatru's mind troubled him deeply. "Twelve hundred people! Not a sound of human habitation here!! Have you brought me here to be killed? Is it a plot of yours?"
"My lord! Trust me. Let us go ahead."
Ajatasatru was spellbound when he finally saw the massive assembly of men meditating quietly around the great teacher. 
"All suffering and pain, fear and hatred, come from desire. The man who is free from all desire, need not worry. Whom has he to fear?" Buddha preached. 
"How soothing!" Ajatasatru thought, his paranoia melting away. He repented for his sins and became a disciple of Buddha. 

Not much later, the wicked Devadatta also repented after falling severely ill. He told his followers, "Children, take me to Buddha. He alone can bring peace to my troubled mind."
His men carried him on a litter, but stopped to rest just outside the monastery. "Where have my men gone? I am in a hurry to meet Buddha," Devadatta gasped, trying to walk himself. But before he could reach Buddha, he tottered and fell to the ground. 
"I am dying," Devadatta whispered. "But now I know that Buddha is indeed the enlightened one—the teacher of all. I take refuge in him."

Millions took refuge in him and in his teachings, chanting:
*Buddham Saranam Gacchami* (I take refuge in the Buddha)
*Sangham Saranam Gacchami* (I take refuge in the Sangha)
*Dhammam Saranam Gacchami* (I take refuge in the Dharma)

Buddha lived to a ripe old age, spreading peace. People who spoke different languages and belonged to distant lands became his devoted followers.
