# Tales of Avvaiyaar - 1. Avvai and Paari

One day, a visitor was announced to the court of King Paari of Parambu Naad.

"The revered poet Avvaiyaar is here to see you, sir," a guard announced.

King Paari's face lit up with joy. He stepped down from his throne to welcome her. "Avvai! Dear Avvai! You have come at last to thread your sweet verses like pearls on the bare string of my life!"

"I am so happy to see you again, Paari," the old poetess smiled, leaning on her walking stick. 

Paari looked at her closely. "But you look tired. Any king would gladly provide you with a royal carriage. But, no, you will insist on wearing out your feet."

"What am I to do?" Avvai shrugged gently. "I would be extremely uncomfortable in a carriage."

"My simple Avvai! Come, refresh yourself with a wash and a meal," Paari insisted.

After Avvai had a wash, Paari led her to the grand dining-chamber of the palace. 

"How delicious this payasam is!" Avvai exclaimed, tasting the sweet dish of milk and rice.

"Serve her some more," Paari immediately ordered his attendants.

"No, my friend, don't," Avvai stopped him, raising a hand. "This stomach will accept only what it needs for a day, not for a week!"

After her meal, Avvai spent the rest of the day with Paari, delighting him with her beautiful verses. 

"Superb! Superb!" Paari clapped, listening to her poetry.

In this joyful manner, many days flew by in the palace.

Then, one day, Avvai came to him with her small bundle of belongings. "Paari, I am restless. I must go now."

Paari's heart sank. *She is the only solace in my troubled life as a king. How can I let her go?* he thought sadly. But he respected her freedom and did not protest. 

"All right go, Avvai. I cannot stop you," Paari said quietly.

"Goodbye, my friend. You will always be in my thoughts," Avvai promised.

While she was walking through the lonely Parambu jungles on her journey, a mysterious figure on a horse rode aggressively towards her. His face was covered with a cloth mask.

"Hand over all you have, if you value your life!" the robber demanded, pointing a knife at her.

"What! A robber in Paari's land?" Avvai asked, shocked. 

She handed over her small bundle. "All right, here you are! But your king will hear of this, believe me."

When the robber had ridden away, an indignant Avvai turned right around and made her way back to the palace.

"Avvai! You are back!" Paari cried out in surprise when he saw her.

"Yes, but only to tell you how disturbed I am!" she fumed. "I have been robbed, my friend. I am not upset at losing my belongings... But to think that there are robbers in this land... the land of good King Paari!"

"What! Am I hearing right? Who dared do such a thing! He shall be severely punished!" Paari yelled, looking absolutely furious. He paced the room. "Now what would the proper punishment be? Let me see... Yes... yes... his hands must be cut off!"

"But, my friend..." Avvai started, taken aback by the severe punishment.

"No, Avvai. Show him no mercy. Here is a sword. Do it yourself," Paari demanded, drawing his sword and holding it out to her. "Here! Cut them off."

Avvai stared at the sword in confusion. "Paari, I... I don't understand."

"Don't hesitate. Cut them off!" Paari urged. Then, smiling warmly, he reached into a box and pulled out her stolen bundle. "Here are your belongings."

He removed the cloth mask from his pocket. "I am the robber, Avvai. I could not think of any other way of bringing you back to the palace."

Avvai's eyes filled with tears. "How great is your affection for me, Paari!"

"Avvai, will you stay on in this robber's kingdom?" Paari asked hopefully.

Deeply touched by his love, Avvai agreed at once and stayed there for a few more happy months.

Then, finally leaving Paari's kingdom, Avvai continued on her endless wanderings. One day, her path led her through a vast, harsh stretch of barren country.

As she walked on, severe hunger and thirst began to torment her. *Not a single fruit-bearing tree! Not a stream of water in sight!* she thought, wiping sweat from her brow.

Somehow she staggered on till, at last, she saw a large tree in the distance. 

*A jamun tree! Is it real? Or am I seeing things?* she wondered. 

Avvai approached the tree and sat down, completely exhausted, under its shade. *For the strength to shake the fruit down, I would first have to eat a few of them!* she thought miserably.

Even there, trapped in such a difficult situation, Avvai did not lose her famous sense of humour. She patted her empty stomach. 

"O my bag of troubles, if I ask you to manage without food for one day, you cannot. Yet, when you could have taken in enough for days to come, you did not agree. Never will you understand my troubles. You are a difficult one to live with!" she scolded her belly. 

"Now, when this stomach cannot get a thing to eat, how keenly it hungers for your payasam, Paari!" she sighed. 

She composed a verse in her head: *We bow to the undeserving, we cross the seas, we put on many acts, we enslave people and rule over them, we sing the praises of all kinds of men—all for a measure of rice for this tyrant. For a measure of rice and for four cubits of cloth to cover his body, man must think and think of his eighty crore devices.*

Avvai laughed aloud at her own verses, composed on a completely empty stomach.

Then she began to muse quietly. Suddenly a young voice broke into her thoughts from above.

"Good evening, Paati," the voice called out.

Avvai looked up into the branches. *A boy? What is he doing in this lonely place?* she wondered. It was a young cowherd, sitting high in the jamun tree.

"My child, I am tired and hungry. Will you throw me some fruit?" Avvai asked.

"Certainly, Paati! Do you want hot fruit or cold?" the boy asked playfully.

*Hot fruit or cold? He's teasing me,* Avvai thought. *I will humour him.* "Mmm—throw me some hot fruit," she replied.

Plucking some ripe jamuns, the boy threw them down into the dust. Avvai picked them up. But before putting them into her mouth, she had to blow hard on them to clear away the sand and dust.

"Ha, ha, ha!" the boy laughed heartily from the branches.

"Why are you laughing, child?" Avvai asked, slightly annoyed.

"Are the fruits too hot, Paati?" the boy giggled. "Be careful. Blow hard and cool them or you might scald your tongue."

"What do you mean?" Avvai asked, puzzled.

Suddenly, Avvai understood. The boy had made a clever pun on the word "hot." Because she was blowing on the fruit as if it were hot food, he called the dusty fruit "hot"!

Avvai smiled, deeply humbled by the young boy's sharp wit. "I, an old axe who could withstand the hardest ebony, must acknowledge defeat before this young plantain stem," she praised him. "I cannot sleep for two nights now, having admitted defeat at the hands of this young cowherd grazing his black buffaloes."


# Tales of Avvaiyaar - 2. The Sole Consideration

The famous poetess Avvai often visited the grand courts of various kings across the land, where she was always welcomed with great warmth and heard with deep respect.

During one such visit, a king approached her with a troubled look. 

"Ah, Avvai. I've been thinking of you," the king said warmly. "I have to appoint a new minister for my court, and I desperately need your wise advice."

Avvai nodded, leaning on her walking stick. "How can I help, O King?"

"Why, there go the two men I have in mind!" the king pointed out the window into the royal gardens. Two men were walking and talking together.

"One is known to be a highly capable, just, and intelligent man," the king explained, pointing to the man on the left.

"The other is my own cousin," the king said, gesturing to the second man. 

The king turned back to Avvai. "Don't you think it would be wiser to appoint my royal relative rather than someone who is in no way connected with me?"

Avvai looked thoughtful. "I am not so sure," she replied slowly.

"The disease we are born with could kill us," Avvai explained gently, sharing her profound wisdom. "On the other hand, the unknown herb that grows in the distant, unconnected jungle cures us."

The king listened intently. 

"Merit should be the sole consideration in appointing a person to a position of high responsibility," Avvai advised firmly. "Find out which of them is more capable, just, and trustworthy, and appoint him. Blood ties mean nothing when it comes to the welfare of your kingdom."

The king bowed deeply. "I am truly grateful to you, Avvai. When I seek advice, no one else speaks out freely... That honesty is exactly the quality I must look for in the minister I appoint."

A week later, the king sought out Avvai again.

"Avvai, I have officially appointed the new minister..." the king smiled broadly. "...And merit was the sole consideration in my choice."

"I am very happy to hear that," Avvai smiled, pleased that her honest advice had served the kingdom well.


# Tales of Avvaiyaar - 3. Varappuyara

Once, the renowned poetess Avvai was invited to a distant kingdom where a grand assembly of poets was to be held. As usual, the humble ascetic refused any form of royal transport and walked all the way there.

As she passed through the countryside, she saw worried villagers gathering near dry fields.

"This spring, too, is drying up," an old farmer sighed, looking at the cracked earth.

"Will the drought this time mean the death of us all?" a woman cried, holding an empty pot.

"The crops are completely ruined. What shall we do for food?" another villager wept.

Avvai's heart broke for the suffering people. 

When Avvai finally reached the grand palace, the assembly had already begun. The local poets were taking turns reading out their long, flattering compositions to the king.

One after another, they falsely praised the king and loudly blessed him with a long life and a prosperous reign, completely ignoring the desperate famine outside the palace walls.

Finally, Avvai rose to her feet. Everyone in the court waited eagerly to hear the legendary poetess's composition.

"O king," Avvai began, her voice ringing clear across the hall, "a parrot is fond of the words it has learnt, and is never ashamed or tired of repeating them. But it becomes speechless and gives but one terrified cry when it sees a big cat approaching its cage."

She glared at the sycophantic poets who had just spoken. "So, O king, the poem I present before you is simply *Varappuyara*."

And with that single word, Avvai took her seat again.

The court erupted in shocked murmurs. 

"What? Varappuyara?" the other poets gasped.

"Is that all?"

"What kind of poem is that?" they scoffed.

But the king knew there was profound meaning behind her single word. "Avvai, I would be deeply grateful if you explained your poem to us," the king requested respectfully.

Avvai stood up again. "O king, I am a poet; words come easily to me. But even *I* am struck dumb when I see the real horrors of drought and famine in your land."

She pointed her walking stick towards the window. "By *Varappuyara*, I make my earnest request that you raise bunds in your land. These bunds will dramatically increase the level of the water in your reservoirs. More water will enable you to irrigate your land better, and your starving people will finally prosper."

The king was very pleased by her selfless request. "Avvai, you have spoken like the great poet and humanitarian that you truly are."

Thereafter, the poets and ministers all left the assembly. But the king, who till then had been so enthusiastic, suddenly had second thoughts.

"Hmm... bunds! Bunds everywhere will heavily deplete our royal treasury," he muttered, pacing his private chambers. 

"O king, what you say reminds me of another verse," Avvai said softly, having stayed behind.

The king looked at her.

"Giving away alms may diminish a man's material wealth," Avvai explained gently, "but it inevitably adds to his spiritual wealth. A sculptor carves away stone only to create an exquisite piece of art. These actions that seem to *take away* only add to mankind's real wealth."

She pointed to the king. "The money in your treasury is now like dead matter. It will gain true life only if you use it for a good cause."

The king smiled, his doubts completely washed away by her profound wisdom. "You are right, Avvai. I will do exactly as you advise."

And the very next day, the massive public work on the bunds was begun, saving the kingdom from famine.


# Tales of Avvaiyaar - 4. Avvai to the Rescue

One day, as the old poetess Avvai was passing through a village, she heard a commotion.

"Kick him! Thrash him!" a group of angry men yelled, chasing a young, frightened boy down the street. 

"Keep out of our street, you filthy urchin!" one of the men roared, raising a heavy wooden stick.

"I'm going... please... don't hurt me," the poor boy cried, cowering in fear.

"Then go at once! Or I'll..." the man raised his stick to strike the child.

"STOP!" a sharp voice commanded.

The man froze as Avvai quickly stepped in and blocked his heavy blow with her simple wooden walking staff.

"Leave the child alone!" Avvai scolded them, her eyes flashing with anger. "Cruel, heartless men! What are you doing?"

"What are you doing here, old woman?" the man demanded, pointing his stick at her. "We are keeping our street clear of pollution. Who are you to stop us?"

"Let me just lay my hands..." another man threatened, stepping towards the boy.

"Don't move another step!" Avvai warned fiercely, shielding the trembling child behind her back. "I will not let you touch a single hair on this child."

The men scoffed at the old woman. "Oh, ho! Breaking caste rules at your age, are you?" one mocked.

Avvai stood tall. "There are no castes, but two," she declared clearly. "One: the good who help the poor in distress; the other: the low-born that will not help."

"Indeed! And who says this?" a man sneered.

"All religions say this," Avvai replied calmly. "Do good and refrain from evil."

"Ha, ha!" the leader laughed arrogantly. "Look at this hag teaching us religion. Us—the Brahmanas, in whom God vested all knowledge!"

"Who is this senile, old...?" another man began to insult her.

"Brother, wait!" a third man suddenly gasped, grabbing his arm. "I have recognised her."

"Who is she?" the others asked, confused.

"The great poet Avvaiyaar!"

The leader crossed his arms, unimpressed. "Humph! So she's a poet! But what can she know of the deep shastras and the puranas...?"

Avvai looked at him with profound pity. "Friend, how vain you are of your knowledge of the shastras and the Puranas!" 

She pointed to the ground. "Look... look at the ant's wonderful corridors in the earth... look at the honey and the hive the little bee makes, or the spider's delicate web... such magnificent things are entirely beyond mortal men."

The angry men fell completely silent, staring at the ground.

"Let no one therefore boast of knowing this or that..." Avvai concluded softly. "...Every creature on this earth excels in something. You are not the centre of the earth, O Brahmana."

The Brahmana looked as if he would explode with rage, but he had no argument against her simple, undeniable truth. Then slowly... his eyes fell in shame. He turned around, and followed by the others, he quietly left the scene, leaving the young boy safe in Avvai's care.


# Tales of Avvaiyaar - 5. Avvai and Adaiyaman

The great poetess Avvai often spent time in the kingdom of the mighty king Adaiyaman, whom she held as one of her dearest friends. 

One day, she found him sitting on his throne, looking incredibly gloomy.

"O Adaiyaman, you look depressed today. What is the matter?" Avvai asked, leaning on her walking stick.

"Avvai, the neighbouring king, Thondaiman, has challenged me to battle," Adaiyaman sighed heavily. "Oh, what a terrible waste is war; of lives... of energy... of resources."

Avvai's heart ached for her peace-loving friend. *I must do something about this,* she thought with determination.

Without telling Adaiyaman her plan, Avvai immediately made for Thondaiman's kingdom. Her fame preceded her, and upon arriving, she was received cordially by the rival king.

"Avvai, I am glad to see you," King Thondaiman smiled warmly. "I am on my way to the royal armoury to inspect my weapons for the coming war. Come with me."

And at the armoury, Thondaiman proudly showed Avvai his massive, gleaming array of pristine weapons. 

*She will know my incredible strength now and will probably report it back to Adaiyaman,* Thondaiman thought smugly.

Avvai looked carefully at the spotless swords and unused shields. 

"O Thondaiman," she said, her voice dripping with careful sarcasm, "your weapons are perfectly clean, well-oiled and shiny. But Adaiyaman's weapons are horribly soiled with blood, and constantly under repair."

Thondaiman looked thoughtfully at his gleaming weapons that had clearly not known many wars. At once, he understood the deep implication of the words of the wise poet.

"Wise Avvai," Thondaiman said slowly, his boastful smile fading. "You have politely complimented me on my keeping my weapons in perfect trim, but at the same time, you have sharply forewarned me of Adaiyaman's immense valour and vast experience in actual battle."

He realized that fighting a battle-hardened veteran like Adaiyaman would be a devastating mistake.

"Go and tell Adaiyaman that the war is off!" Thondaiman declared, turning to his generals.

"Your decision is a worthy one, O king. War is self-destruction," Avvai praised him. "It is like plunging into a deep river with a heavy grinding-stone for help."

Thondaiman smiled, grateful for her clever intervention. "For sparing our women the tragic loss of husbands and sons, the very rain shall fall seasonally to bless you, and therefore your people." He paused, looking at the old ascetic. "The rain will fall for my sake, Avvai?"

"Yes!" Avvai nodded with a bright smile. "If on this ancient earth, there is even one truly good man, the glad clouds will pour their rain for him. But all may have the rain that pours for his sake."
