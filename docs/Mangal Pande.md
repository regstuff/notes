# Mangal Pande: The Spark of 1857

In the winter of 1857, exactly a century after the fateful Battle of Plassey, discontent simmered among the native sepoys of the British East India Company. In the important military station of Barrackpore, near Calcutta, the tall, handsome Indian sepoys of the 34th Native Infantry presented an impressive picture on the parade ground. Yet, beneath their shining uniforms, deep resentment was brewing. 

The sepoys were miserably underpaid compared to their European counterparts, struggling to survive on a mere seven rupees a month. However, the final, explosive spark was ignited not by wages, but by a horrifying rumor surrounding the new Enfield rifles. 

Word spread rapidly from the ammunition depot in Dum Dum that the new cartridges, which had to be bitten open before loading, were greased with cow and pig fat. To the Hindu and Muslim sepoys, this was the ultimate sacrilege. They believed it was a deliberate British plot to defile them, break their caste, and forcibly convert them to Christianity.

"Pig's fat! Those infidels! Do they want our sepoys to be thrown out of the community?" angry villagers shouted in the bazaars.
"Better to die than let our young men be subjected to such humiliation!" others agreed. 

The British officers, led by Major-General J.B. Hearsey, tried to assure the men that their religion was not under attack. But the sepoys’ trust in the British had eroded. Mysterious nocturnal fires began to rage in public buildings, signaling the people's anger. Secret letters circulated among the Bengal Army regiments: *"We serve for honour and religion; if we lose our honour, the Hindu and Mohammedan religions will be destroyed."*

The unrest first erupted at the nearby station of Berhampore. On February 27, the 19th Native Infantry flatly refused to accept the new percussion caps and cartridges. 
"No, sir, we can't accept them," they declared to their commanding officer. "Even if we just touch them, we will be defiled!"

Col. Mitchell, an ill-tempered British officer, reacted furiously, threatening to send them to Burma to die. That night, fearing an attack from British artillery, the frantic sepoys seized their muskets from the armory. Disaster was narrowly averted when native officers convinced Col. Mitchell to withdraw the artillery. Though a massacre was prevented, the British authorities in Calcutta immediately ordered the 19th Regiment to be disarmed and disbanded. Furthermore, a ship full of European troops from Rangoon was ordered to Calcutta. 

When news of the European troop ship reached Barrackpore, panic swept through the 34th Native Infantry. The sepoys believed the British soldiers were coming to slaughter them all.

On the afternoon of Sunday, March 29, the tension finally boiled over. A mild-mannered sepoy named Mangal Pande suddenly appeared in front of the quarter-guard, waving a loaded musket. 

"Rise and take up arms, you lazy blackguards!" Mangal Pande roared to his fellow sepoys. "Make haste before the English make outcastes of us all!" 

He paced the parade ground like a caged tiger, shouting and gesticulating wildly, urging the men to strike a blow for their religion. The sepoys watched in excited fascination.

Hearing the commotion, the Adjutant, Lt. Baugh, arrived at a full gallop. Mangal Pande instantly raised his musket and fired. The shot brought down Lt. Baugh’s horse, sending the British officer tumbling into the dust. Baugh quickly drew his pistol and fired back, but missed. 

Drawing his heavy talwar (sword), Mangal Pande engaged in fierce hand-to-hand combat with the officer. With a powerful swing, Pande struck down Lt. Baugh. Another British officer, Sgt. Hewson, rushed in to help, but the enraged sepoy easily threw him to the ground as well.

"Shabash!" a muffled cry of encouragement rang out from the watching sepoys. 

Desperate, the wounded British officers ordered Ishwari Prasad, the head of the native guard, to arrest Pande. "Order your men forward to get this son of a fool!" Hewson yelled.
But the native guard stood stock-still. They made no move to help the British officers. For a brief moment, the mob instinct took over, and some sepoys even struck at the fallen officers. 

More British officers arrived, including Col. Whelan and Brigadier Charles Grant, but they looked on helplessly as the native guards flatly refused to obey their commands to arrest the mutineer. 

"Join me, you dogs! Come on!" Mangal Pande continued to swagger, taunting the British.

Finally, General Hearsey rode onto the parade ground, flanked by his two sons. Apprised of the situation, the General rode directly up to the rebellious native guard. Drawing his revolver, Hearsey pointed it squarely at the head guard’s head. 

"The first man who refuses to march, when I give the order, will be shot dead!" Hearsey roared. "Quick march!"

Faced with a loaded revolver, the native guard finally obeyed, marching forward like well-oiled clockwork to surround the lone rebel.

Seeing the guards advancing upon him, Mangal Pande realized his brief hour of glory was over. Resolving to die rather than face capture and the ignominy of a British trial, he placed the muzzle of his musket firmly against his own breast. Using his toe, he pressed the trigger.

The shot rang out, and Mangal Pande collapsed in a pool of blood. However, the blast did not kill him; he was severely wounded and taken into custody. 

Despite his injuries, Mangal Pande was hastily tried. When asked who had incited him to mutiny, he refused to implicate any of his comrades, declaring proudly, "I acted of my own will."

On April 8, 1857, Mangal Pande was hanged in the presence of his entire regiment. Retribution continued swiftly; on April 21, Ishwari Prasad, the head guard who had refused to arrest him, was also executed. The British then disarmed and disbanded the rest of the 34th Native Infantry. The sepoys were stripped of their uniforms and marched out of the cantonment under heavy European escort. 

But instead of feeling humiliated, the disbanded sepoys felt a profound sense of liberation. "Punishment? You call this punishment?" they laughed as they walked away. "Brothers, we are free!" 

When these men returned to their villages, they were treated as heroes. The spark ignited by Mangal Pande that Sunday afternoon quickly spread across the country like wildfire. The disaffection grew into a blazing inferno, and soon the surrounding regions were in open rebellion. 

The Great Mutiny of 1857—India's First War of Independence—had officially begun, and history would forever remember that it was Mangal Pande who bravely struck the very first blow.
