# Zarathushtra: The Prophet of Ahura Mazda

In ancient Persia, during an era plagued by strife and wickedness, the people suffered greatly under the reign of evil. Desperate for salvation, a wise elder prayed fervently, "O Ahura Mazda, send us a saviour! Let past glory return to this earth."

At that time, in the city of Rae, lived a man named Frahimurva and his daughter, Dughdhowa. Dughdhowa was bathed in an unearthly, divine radiance, but the fearful townspeople misjudged it. "Send your daughter away, Frahimurva!" they demanded. "She portends evil. This radiance is an evil omen!"

Torn between his love for his daughter and the demands of the elders, Frahimurva wept. "My child, we must listen to the wise men, but I cannot bear to see you go." 

"Oh, father, please let me stay," Dughdhowa pleaded. "Who will look after you when you are old?"

"No, child," Frahimurva decided heavily. "We mustn't think only of ourselves. Go. My good friend Paitarasp awaits you."

After a sorrowful farewell, Dughdhowa journeyed to Paitarasp's home, where she was welcomed warmly. Paitarasp soon introduced her to a handsome young man named Pourushasp. "This is Pourushasp. It is your father's wish that you marry him," he said. The two instantly fell in love and were soon married.

Months later, while pregnant, Dughdhowa woke up terrified from a nightmare. "I had a terrible dream," she told Pourushasp, describing a vicious tiger that was driven away by a radiant man wielding a magical wand.

Pourushasp sought out the wise men for an explanation. The sage smiled reassuringly, "O Pourushasp, you are indeed blessed. Your son will be a great man. He will be the messenger of God and will teach the world a religion of love and justice. But he will suffer. Jealous men will try to destroy him."

On a beautiful spring day, Dughdhowa gave birth to a baby boy. Strangely, instead of crying, the newborn smiled. "Let us call him Zarathushtra," Pourushasp said proudly.

However, the dark prophecy soon unfolded. Durasrob, the region's head priest who was secretly a dark sorcerer, recognized the threat the child posed to the forces of evil. "The child must not live. I shall kill him," he plotted. 

Durasrob stole the baby and placed him in the path of a stampeding herd of cattle. Miraculously, a cow stood protectively over the infant, shielding him from the hooves. Furious, Durasrob tried again with a herd of horses, but a majestic stallion protected the child. 

Refusing to accept defeat, the sorcerer carried the baby into a dark forest. "You will not escape this time. The wolves will tear you to pieces!" he cackled. But the wolves took one look at the holy child and slunk away in reverence. Seething with rage, Durasrob threw the infant into a blazing fire, declaring, "I shall burn you to death!" To his sheer disbelief, the raging flames transformed into a soft bed of fragrant roses, where Dughdhowa soon found her unharmed baby.

A year later, Zarathushtra fell gravely ill. Anxious, Pourushasp called for Durasrob, unaware of his true nature. The evil sorcerer handed them a vial. "Don't worry," he smirked inwardly, "my poison never fails." As Dughdhowa tried to feed the baby the medicine, Zarathushtra spat it out, spilling it entirely. "Oh, you naughty boy!" she scolded gently, before realizing, "But what's this? Your fever has come down!"

Zarathushtra grew into a sturdy, highly intelligent boy. Sent to study under the learned Burzin Kurus, he quickly became the master's most brilliant pupil. As Zarathushtra matured, he embraced a life of simplicity, dedicating his days to helping the poor, the sick, and being exceptionally kind to dumb animals. 

One day, Pourushasp offered his son any of his possessions. Zarathushtra chose only his father's simple girdle. "When I wear it, I feel so confident... so sure of winning God's battle against the forces of evil," he explained.

Eventually, Zarathushtra decided to leave home in search of ultimate truth. During his journey, he reached the river Abahi, which had no bridge or boat. Stepping boldly into the water, he found that the river miraculously only rose to his chest, allowing him to cross safely. As he emerged on the other side, he glowed with a divine light.

In the wilderness, a majestic, winged figure appeared before him. "Zarathushtra, what are you looking for?" 

"For the will of God. You, Vohu Mano, can help me," Zarathushtra replied, recognizing the Angel of the Good Mind. 

Vohu Mano instructed him to close and open his eyes. When Zarathushtra did, he beheld a vision of the Supreme Creator. "I am Ahura Mazda," the divine voice echoed. "The righteous man is the one who is truthful, just, charitable. He looks after all my creations, respects them, protects them. Go forth Zarathushtra and spread my message."

"I will, my Lord. But give me a symbol," Zarathushtra asked. 

"Every shining object is the symbol of God. Fire burns away all evil and it can never be made impure," Ahura Mazda declared. 

Armed with this divine revelation, Zarathushtra returned to his people. "Listen to me, my brothers," he preached. "It is up to you to decide whether you want to tread the path of good or the path of evil. Banish evil from your heart and mind, and make room for God. God does not sit in heaven on a golden throne. He lives in your heart." He taught them the three pillars of his new faith: *Humata* (good thoughts), *Hukhta* (good words), and *Hvareshta* (good deeds).

Many years later, King Gushtasp of Balkh had a vivid dream where Zarathushtra descended from the sky, handing him a ball of sacred fire that did not burn, alongside a holy book, the Avesta. 

The very next morning, Zarathushtra arrived at the palace gates. Astonished, King Gushtasp invited him in and challenged him to answer the profound questions of his court's wise men. Zarathushtra's wisdom left the scholars speechless. "No ordinary man can have such knowledge," they admitted. "It could only have been given to him by God."

Impressed, Gushtasp gave Zarathushtra a place of honor. But the king's courtiers grew violently jealous. Plotting his downfall, they sneaked into Zarathushtra's quarters and planted dark artifacts—skulls, bones, and sinister relics. The next day, they accused the prophet of being a dark sorcerer. 

When the king's guards discovered the planted evidence, a heartbroken Gushtasp ordered Zarathushtra thrown into a dungeon. The courtiers gloated, "Where is his Ahura Mazda now?"

A week passed, and tragedy struck the king. His beloved, magnificent steed, Asp-i-Siyah, mysteriously fell ill, its legs drawn tightly into its stomach, unable to stand. The king refused to eat out of grief. Hearing of this, Zarathushtra sent word from his cell that he could cure the horse.

A desperate Gushtasp freed him. "Cure my horse, and you will prove the greatness of your God," he pleaded. 

"Your horse can be cured only if you have complete faith in me and fulfil four conditions," Zarathushtra stated. 

First, Gushtasp had to publicly proclaim his absolute belief that Zarathushtra was the messenger of the one true God. The king agreed, and the horse's first leg was miraculously freed. 
Second, the king's brave son, Aspandiar, had to pledge to use his strength to defend the true religion. He did, and the second leg snapped into place. 
Third, the Queen herself had to acknowledge God and follow his path. She devoutly accepted, freeing the third leg. 
Finally, Zarathushtra demanded that the keeper of his apartment be questioned again under threat of death. Terrified, the keeper confessed that the jealous courtiers had planted the bones. Furious, King Gushtasp banished the treacherous men forever. Instantly, Asp-i-Siyah's fourth leg was released, and the horse stood up, fully healed!

"Forgive me, Zarathushtra, for having doubted you," Gushtasp wept, embracing the prophet. 

Years later, four dazzling angels rode into the king's courtyard. They were Vohu Mano (Good Mind), Asha Vahishta (Righteous Law), Haurvatat (Perfection), and Adar Gushasp (Sacred Fire). They commanded the king to accept and protect Zarathushtra's religion. In devotion, Gushtasp and Zarathushtra built great temples across the land, dedicated to the Sacred Fire.

When Zarathushtra was seventy-seven years old, a Turanian assassin infiltrated his temple and struck him down with a sword as he prayed. But before Zarathushtra succumbed, he hurled his prayer beads at the murderer. In a flash of divine justice, the assassin was struck dead by a bolt of holy light. 

Though the prophet passed away, the great religion he preached lived on, burning as brightly and eternally as the sacred fires he kindled in the hearts of his followers.
