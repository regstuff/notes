# Paurava and Alexander: Battle of the Giants

More than two thousand years ago, the valiant King Paurava (known to the Greeks as Porus) stood on the banks of the Jhelum river, proudly watching his soldiers defeat the army of his bitter rival, King Ambhi of Takshashila. 

When the fleeing Ambhi was captured and brought before him, Paurava displayed his characteristic Rajput-like pride. "Go back to Takshashila and don't come to this side of the Jhelum again," Paurava commanded with disgust. "I can't stand the sight of cowards! Begone!"

Humiliated and burning with a desire for revenge, King Ambhi committed the ultimate act of treason. He sent his minister to Alexander the Great, the mighty Greek conqueror who was camping at Nicaea, offering him the entire kingdom of Takshashila if Alexander would help him destroy King Paurava. 

Alexander eagerly accepted the invitation. To secure his path to the Indus river, he divided his massive army into two divisions. While the first division marched ahead, Alexander led the second division to secure their rear, facing fierce and unexpected resistance at the heavily fortified city of Mashakavati (Massaga). 

During the brutal siege, Alexander himself was wounded in the ankle by an arrow. Enraged, the Greeks battered down the city walls using massive military engines. When the brave chieftain of Mashakavati fell in battle, the defending mercenaries agreed to a truce, promising to vacate the fort and join Alexander's army. 

However, the proud Indian mercenaries secretly planned to slip away into the night rather than fight against their own countrymen. Learning of their plan through his spies, Alexander ordered a ruthless, surprise night attack. The Greeks mercilessly slaughtered the sleeping mercenaries, along with their women and children, leaving a dark stain on Alexander's legacy. 

Continuing his relentless march, Alexander conquered the supposedly impregnable rock fortress of Aornos. Finally, he crossed the mighty river Sindhu (Indus) on a magnificent bridge of boats and triumphantly entered Takshashila, where the traitorous King Ambhi welcomed him with open arms and showered him with gold and gifts. 

While resting in Takshashila, Alexander, a student of the great philosopher Aristotle, was fascinated by the ascetic Indian sages. He sent an envoy to the revered Sage Dandamis, ordering him to come to the royal court. 
The fearless sage flatly refused. "God is the creator of light, of peace, and of life," Dandamis replied calmly. "Alexander is not God, for he abhors peace and slaughters men. If he wishes to meet me, he may come here. He can cut off my head, but he cannot destroy my soul." Deeply impressed by such fearless wisdom, Alexander himself visited the sage for profound philosophical discussions. 

Soon, it was time for war. Alexander sent an envoy to Paurava's court, demanding tribute and submission. 
Paurava smiled fearlessly. "Tell Alexander that he has nothing to offer me. But if he wants anything from me, he may come to me. I will certainly meet him, but I will come armed for battle."

True to his word, Paurava marched his massive army, complete with terrifying war elephants, to the banks of the Jhelum. When Alexander arrived on the opposite bank, he realized the river was in heavy spate and impossible to cross directly in the face of Paurava's formidable elephants and archers. 

For days, Alexander employed brilliant psychological warfare. Every night, he would march his cavalry up and down his side of the river, making a terrific noise, pretending he was about to cross. Paurava would rush his troops to match them, only to find the Greeks doing nothing. Eventually, assuming it was a bluff, Paurava stopped responding to the nightly alarms. 

This was exactly what Alexander had been waiting for. On a pitch-black night, under the cover of a massive, roaring thunderstorm that drowned out the noise of his movements, Alexander secretly marched 12,000 of his best men sixteen miles upstream and successfully crossed the swollen river using rafts covered with animal hides. 

Realizing the Greeks had crossed, Paurava immediately dispatched his son with a cavalry unit to stop them. But in the muddy, treacherous terrain, the Indian chariots became bogged down. Paurava's brave son was killed in a fierce cavalry clash. 

Grieving but undeterred, King Paurava personally led his entire army to face the Greeks on the plains of Hydaspes. As the two massive armies clashed, Alexander launched a brilliant, sweeping cavalry attack against Paurava's left wing. When Paurava shifted his right wing to support the left, the Greek general Koinos swept around the rear, completely encircling the Indian army. 

The battle turned into a chaotic bloodbath. Paurava's archers slipped in the thick mud, unable to properly anchor their massive longbows. Worse still, the Greek infantry mercilessly targeted the Indian war elephants, stabbing them with long spears. Blinded by pain and panic, the enraged elephants turned and stampeded backward, mercilessly trampling Paurava's own soldiers. 

Despite his army collapsing around him, King Paurava fought like a towering giant. Mounted on his colossal lead elephant, he hurled javelin after javelin at the Greeks, refusing to retreat even as he suffered multiple severe wounds. 

Seeing his rival bleeding and surrounded, the traitor Ambhi cowardly rode up to him. "Paurava! Surrender!" Ambhi yelled. 
Furious at the sight of the traitor, the wounded Paurava summoned his remaining strength and hurled a heavy javelin straight at Ambhi's head. "You coward!" Paurava roared, narrowly missing as Ambhi galloped away in sheer terror. 

Watching the incredibly heroic, desperate stand of the Indian king from a distance, Alexander was deeply moved. "He is one of the bravest men I have ever known," Alexander told his generals. "He must be saved."

Alexander sent Meroes, an old friend of Paurava, to plead with him to stop fighting. Exhausted, bleeding heavily, and having fought to the absolute limits of human endurance, Paurava finally agreed to dismount and meet the Greek Emperor. 

As the towering, majestic Indian king walked proudly toward the Greek camp, Alexander rode forward to meet him with profound respect. 
"O Porus," Alexander asked gently, "Tell me, how would you like to be treated?"
Paurava looked the greatest conqueror in the world squarely in the eye. **"Treat me, Alexander, as befits a king,"** he replied proudly. 

"I will, but don't you need anything else?" Alexander asked, amazed by his unbroken spirit. 
"Everything is included in what I have asked for, Alexander," Paurava smiled. 

Deeply struck by his incredible courage, dignity, and royal bearing, Alexander did not treat Paurava as a defeated prisoner. Instead, he not only restored Paurava's entire kingdom to him but also added vast new territories to his rule. The two great giants of history—one from the West and one from the East—who had fought each other so fiercely on the battlefield, embraced each other as true allies and lifelong friends.
