# The March to Freedom - 3 (The Saga of Indian Revolutionaries) - 1. The Saga of Indian Revolutionaries

While the mainstream Indian National Congress pursued a path of constitutional agitation and non-violent protest against British rule, a parallel movement of fiery, young revolutionaries emerged. Driven by an intense, burning love for their motherland, these revolutionaries believed that freedom could only be won through armed struggle, sacrifice, and martyrdom. 

The saga began with Vasudeo Balwant Phadke in Maharashtra, who was driven "almost mad with the idea" of freeing his starving countrymen. In 1879, he organized an armed rebellion, attempting to cut telegraph wires and raid government treasuries to establish an Indian Republic. He was captured and sent to the infamous prison in Aden, where he died in 1883, becoming an early inspiration for future patriots. 

Following him were the Chapekar brothers (Damodar, Balkrishna, and Vasudeo) of Pune. Outraged by the brutal and humiliating methods used by the British Plague Commissioner, Rand, during an epidemic, they assassinated him in 1897. All three brothers bravely mounted the scaffold and were hanged. 

The movement spread to Bengal after the British ruthlessly suppressed protests against the partition of Bengal in 1905. Young teenagers like Sushil Kumar openly fought back against police brutality. Khudiram Bose and Prafulla Chaki threw a bomb intended for a cruel British magistrate, Kingsford. Though the attempt failed, Khudiram's cheerful martyrdom on the gallows awakened the nation from its slumber. 

Revolutionary activities were not confined to India. In London, Vinayak Damodar Savarkar organized students at India House, sent bomb manuals to India, and boldly declared the 1857 revolt as the "First War of Independence." His associate, Madanlal Dhingra, assassinated Sir Curzon Wyllie in London to avenge the brutal oppression in India. Savarkar himself was later arrested, subjected to a spectacular but failed escape attempt at sea, and sentenced to fifty years of harsh imprisonment in the Andaman cellular jail. 

In America, Lala Hardayal launched the "Ghadar" (Rebellion) party, rallying Indian expatriates, particularly Sikhs, to send arms and money back home. When a ship of Sikh immigrants, the *Komagatamaru*, was turned away from Canada and fired upon by British police upon returning to Calcutta, the outrage fueled plans for a massive armed uprising in India. 

Led by Rash Behari Bose and Jatin Mukherji (Bagha Jatin), the revolutionaries planned a nationwide mutiny within the British Indian army for February 21, 1915. Tragically, a traitor revealed the plot. Rash Behari Bose escaped to Japan, while Bagha Jatin died a hero's death in a fierce, bloody gun battle with the police in Balasore, Orissa. 

To crush the growing revolution, the British passed the draconian Rowlatt Acts in 1919, allowing arrest without trial. This led to widespread protests, culminating in the horrific Jallianwalla Bagh massacre in Amritsar, where General Dyer ordered his troops to gun down hundreds of unarmed, peaceful protesters. 

From the blood-soaked earth of Jallianwalla Bagh emerged a new generation of fierce revolutionaries, including Bhagat Singh and Chandrashekhar Azad. They formed the Hindustan Socialist Republican Association. 

To fund their movement, Ramprasad Bismil, Ashfaqulla Khan, and others executed the daring Kakori Train Robbery in 1925, looting the state treasury. Though most were caught and hanged, Chandrashekhar Azad eluded capture. 

When the veteran leader Lala Lajpatrai died from injuries sustained during a brutal police lathi charge, Bhagat Singh and his comrades assassinated the British officer Saunders in revenge. 

To "make the deaf hear," Bhagat Singh and Batukeshwar Dutta threw a non-lethal bomb in the Central Assembly in Delhi in 1929. They purposefully courted arrest to use their trial as a platform to broadcast their revolutionary ideals to the nation. Bhagat Singh, along with Raj Guru and Sukhdev, kissed the hangman's noose on March 23, 1931, becoming eternal symbols of youth and sacrifice. 

Chandrashekhar Azad continued to fight until he was surrounded by police in Alfred Park, Allahabad in 1931. True to his vow never to be captured alive, he fought fiercely until his last bullet, which he used to shoot himself. 

In Bengal, Surya Sen (Masterda) led the spectacular Chittagong Armoury Raid in 1930, successfully liberating the town for a brief period before retreating to the hills to fight a guerrilla war. Masterda was eventually caught and hanged, but his bravery inspired many, including young women like Bina Das, who boldly attempted to shoot the British Governor of Bengal during her university convocation. 

The final chapter of this armed saga was written far from India's shores. Rash Behari Bose, living in exile in Japan, had built the foundation of the Indian National Army (INA). In 1943, he handed leadership over to the charismatic Netaji Subhas Chandra Bose. 

Netaji allied with the Japanese during World War II, swelling the ranks of his "Azad Hind Fauj" with Indian prisoners of war and expatriates. "Delhi Chalo!" (Onward to Delhi!) was their battle cry. In 1944, the INA crossed the Burma border, and its soldiers kissed the sacred soil of India. 

Though the INA was ultimately defeated when the tide of the war turned against Japan, their incredible bravery and the subsequent highly publicized trials of INA officers at the Red Fort in Delhi united the entire country in a wave of patriotic fervor. 

The immense pressure from these decades of relentless revolutionary struggle, combined with the mass upsurge of Mahatma Gandhi's Quit India Movement and the crippling economic effects of World War II, finally broke the back of the British Empire. 

On August 15, 1947, India finally awoke to freedom, and the Indian tricolor was hoisted atop the Red Fort, fulfilling the ultimate dream of countless martyrs who had gladly sacrificed their lives for their beloved motherland.
