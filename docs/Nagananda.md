# Nagananda: The Joy of the Serpents

Long ago, Jimutavahana, a noble Vidyadhara prince, entrusted his kingdom to his ministers so he could dedicate his life to serving his aged parents. Along with his loyal friend Atreya, he traveled to the Malaya mountains to find a peaceful hermitage for them. 

"Ah! The fragrance of the sandal tree!" Jimutavahana sighed happily. "What are the pleasures of a kingdom compared to the beauty of nature in all its glory!"

Suddenly, the clear, flawless notes of a melodious song floated through the air. Following the enchanting music, the two friends reached a forest temple. Inside, a beautiful maiden named Malayavati, Princess of the Siddhas, had just finished her prayers. Her maid, Chaturika, was skeptical of her devotion. "O Princess Malayavati, why do you waste your time worshipping this merciless deity? Your devotion seems to have no effect on her."
"You are wrong, Chaturika," Malayavati replied, her eyes shining. "Early this morning, Goddess Gowri appeared in my dream. She promised that a Vidyadhara prince would soon marry me."

Just then, Jimutavahana and Atreya entered the temple. Malayavati was instantly struck by the handsome prince, wondering if he was the husband promised by the Goddess. However, an ascetic soon arrived and called Malayavati away for midday worship. 

Though she left physically, Malayavati's heart remained with Jimutavahana, and the prince was equally captivated by her. The next day, desperate to see him again, Malayavati and Chaturika hid in the gardens to overhear the prince.
They listened as Atreya asked Jimutavahana to draw a portrait of the woman he loved. "How could you draw her in her absence?" Atreya asked.
"My heart filled with love makes my eyes behold her even when she is not there," Jimutavahana replied dreamily, sketching Malayavati's exact likeness on a stone slab.
Overjoyed, Malayavati whispered, "Enough! I have heard enough. Let's go."

Before they could reveal themselves, Malayavati's brother, Prince Mitravasu, arrived to speak with Jimutavahana. "I bring you a proposal from my father," Mitravasu announced. "He wants you to marry my sister, the Princess Malayavati."
Not realizing that his beloved and the Princess were the same person, Jimutavahana politely declined. "Though I am honoured by the proposal, I cannot accept it. I love another."

Heartbroken, Malayavati believed she had lost her only love. Sending Chaturika away, she wept, "O Goddess Gowri, your words have proved false. Life has no meaning for me now." In despair, she tied a vine around her neck and hanged herself from a tree.
Chaturika, who had hidden nearby, screamed in horror. "Help! Help! Save the princess!"

Hearing the screams, Jimutavahana rushed out, cut the vine, and saved her. "How could you have placed this rough noose around a neck where only the most delicate of necklaces had a right to rest?" he asked. 
"You are the cause," Chaturika scolded him. "She was desperate because you refused to marry her, because your heart was given to the one whose portrait you drew!"
Realizing his mistake, Jimutavahana joyfully led Malayavati to the portrait. "This is the girl to whom I have given my heart!" he smiled. 
Mitravasu soon returned, and with the blessings of all their parents, Jimutavahana and Malayavati were happily married. 

The day after the wedding, Queen of the Siddhas instructed Malayavati that both she and her groom must wear customary red robes for ten days. Since Jimutavahana was out walking near the seashore, a servant was sent to deliver his red robe.

Meanwhile, at the seashore, Mitravasu brought troubling news. "Jimutavahana, the wicked Matanga has occupied your kingdom. We shall declare war on him. We only await your orders."
But the prince of peace shook his head. "I will not permit you to declare war. How can I consent to the slaughter of hundreds of men for the sake of a kingdom?" He sent his friends back to the capital to urge peace. 

Left alone, Jimutavahana noticed a massive white peak nearby. "Look! That mountain looks like a peak of the Himalayas," he observed earlier.
"That's no mountain," Mitravasu had explained. "It's a heap of bones. Garuda, the giant divine bird, used to massacre countless Nagas (serpents). To stop the extinction of his race, King Vasuki of the Nagas agreed to send up one Naga a day for Garuda to devour. Those are the gleaming bones of Garuda's ill-fated victims."

Looking at the tragic mountain of bones, Jimutavahana's heart swelled with compassion. "Ah! If I could protect but one Naga by offering my own body!" he thought.

Suddenly, a piteous cry pierced the silence. An old Naga mother was weeping over her son, Shankhachooda, who had been chosen as that day's victim. "Alas! My beloved son! How can I give you up?"
Jimutavahana approached them gently. "Mother, take heart. I have come to save your son. I will die in his place. Give me the red victim's robe."
Shankhachooda fiercely refused, insisting a great soul like Jimutavahana should live. The Naga youth then left momentarily to offer his final prayers at a nearby temple. 

As they left, the Queen's servant arrived, handing Jimutavahana the customary red wedding robe. Seeing it as a sign from the heavens, Jimutavahana quickly wrapped himself in the red cloth and sat on the sacrificial rock. 
With a deafening roar of wings, the giant Garuda swooped down from the sky, gripped the red-robed prince in his massive talons, and flew him to the top of the Malaya mountains.

Back at the hermitage, Jimutavahana's parents and Malayavati were filled with dread when a blood-smeared jewel fell from the sky. Recognizing it as Jimutavahana's, they feared the worst. 

Meanwhile, Shankhachooda returned to the sacrificial rock and found it empty, except for a trail of blood. "Blood! Oh God! Garuda must have taken away that good soul!" Filled with guilt, the Naga followed the bloody trail up the mountain, desperately hoping to save the prince. 

On the mountain peak, Garuda paused his feast. He was puzzled. "Hitherto all my victims were panic-stricken. This one is calm and peaceful. You do not seem to be a Naga. Who are you?" he asked the bleeding prince.

Before Jimutavahana could answer, Shankhachooda rushed forward. "Garuda, stop! He is Jimutavahana, a Vidyadhara prince. I am the Naga sent up from Patala!"
Garuda froze in horror. "What! The virtuous Jimutavahana! Alas, with this one act I have sunk into a mire of sin. To save one life he has sacrificed his! And wretch that I am, I have almost devoured him."

Jimutavahana's distraught parents and Malayavati arrived just in time to see the dying prince. With his final breaths, Jimutavahana looked at the grief-stricken Garuda. "Garuda... I have no right to live," the giant bird wept. "I shall atone for my sins by killing myself."
"No," Jimutavahana whispered weakly. "By repentance alone can you atone for your sins. Cease forever from destroying life." Advising Garuda thus, the noble prince breathed his last. 

Malayavati, unwilling to live without her husband, prepared to step into a funeral fire. "Gowri, be my witness. Here and now I follow my husband," she prayed. 
Suddenly, a shower of golden liquid fell from the sky, and Goddess Gowri appeared in a burst of light. "Wait, my child. How can my words prove false? Jimutavahana will live!"

Garuda, realizing how to truly atone, had flown to the heavens and begged Lord Indra for Amrit, the nectar of immortality. He showered the Amrit over Jimutavahana, instantly bringing the prince back to life, his wounds fully healed. 
"Rise, Jimutavahana, and become the emperor of the Vidyadharas," Goddess Gowri smiled.

Garuda didn't stop there. He showered the remaining Amrit over the colossal mountain of bones. Miraculously, all the thousands of dead Nagas were revived, returning to life and joyous celebration. King Vasuki of the Nagas bowed to the prince. "You have brought happiness to the Nagas. You will therefore be known forever as Nagananda—the joy of the serpents."

As the revived prince and his family walked down the mountain, they were met by a royal chariot. It was Matanga, the usurper. Overwhelmed by stories of the prince's incredible sacrifice and divinity, Matanga fell to his knees. "Jimutavahana, pardon me and come back to your kingdom. The people want you."

Jimutavahana, the apostle of love and nonviolence, returned to his kingdom with Malayavati and ruled peacefully as the legendary Emperor of the Vidyadharas.
