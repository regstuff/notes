# Valmiki: The Adikavi and Author of the Ramayana

To Valmiki, Indians owe a deep debt of gratitude, for he gave us the Ramayana, one of the most fascinating stories of all time. Valmiki was the Adikavi, the first poet, and his Ramayana the Adikavya, the first poem. But the story of how he became a poet is an absorbing tale in itself.

Strangely, in his early life, Valmiki was a hunter and a robber. Roaming the jungles, he would shoot down harmless birds and gentle animals to bring carcasses home for food. He would also waylay and rob lone travellers passing through the forest. 

"Hand over that bundle or else I will kill you!" he would threaten, brandishing his knife at terrified merchants.

One evening, he returned to his hut with a small sack. "Here is today's loot," he told his wife. 

"Is that all?" she asked, disappointed. 

"Be patient. A group of rich merchants will be passing through the jungle tomorrow," he assured her. 

Thus, he lived a life of violence and crime, until one day, he saw from a distance the Saptarishis, the seven great sages. Gripping his knife, he leaped into their path. "Stop, if you love your lives!" he demanded.

Unfazed, one of the sages calmly asked, "What do you want?"

"All the wealth that you have on you."

"We are men of God and own no wealth," the sage smiled gently. "But why do you want to rob us?"

"I have to maintain my wife and children. Robbery is my means of livelihood," the robber replied impatiently. 

"But first, will you answer my question?" the sage asked. "Your family lives on the fruits of your sin. Will they partake of your sins as well?"

"Of course, they will," the robber scoffed. 

"Go and ask them," the sage challenged him. "Come back with their answer."

"All right. But if you run away, I will make you pay for it!" the robber warned before turning towards his hut.

Inside his home, he asked his wife, "Dear wife, you benefit from my sinful life, don't you?"

"Of course, I do. What of that?"

"You will share my sins, won't you?"

His wife looked at him in disbelief. "Certainly not! You are the sinner. Why should I share your sin?"

Desperate, the robber turned to his young son. "Won't you at least share my sin, my darling?"

"No, father, I will not."

The robber stood stunned and dazed. Bending under the weight of sorrow, he went out with faltering steps, realizing the bitter truth of his actions. He returned to the waiting Rishis and burst into tears, throwing himself at their feet. 

"My soul is lost," he wept bitterly. "Redeem it, O compassionate ones."

The Rishis took pity on him. "Sit down and recite 'Ma-Ra'," the sage instructed. "Put your heart and soul into it. Don't stop for a moment till we return. God bless you."

The robber squatted on the ground, closed his eyes, and forgetting himself, began reciting "Ma-Ra", which continuously chanted, became "Rama, Rama". Months passed into years, and still, he sat unmoving. Time rolled on, and an anthill slowly formed around him, eventually covering him completely.

At last, one day, the seven Rishis returned. "Now come out into the open," they called.

The anthill burst open, and out stepped a person radiant and completely transformed. 

"Among the greatest Rishis you are one," the sage proclaimed. "And greater glory awaits you. You came out of a *valmik* (anthill), and so we shall call you by the new name of Valmiki."

Thereafter, Valmiki, because of his spiritual power and knowledge, earned the respect of holy men everywhere. Long afterwards, the great sage Narada paid him a visit. 

"Who among men is the most perfect one, O Narada?" Valmiki asked. 

"Rama, who is the present King of Ayodhya," Narada replied. At Valmiki's eager request, Narada narrated the wonderful story of Rama. The story remained fresh in Valmiki's mind, just as he had heard it.

Sometime later, Valmiki went to the Tamasa river for a bath with a pupil. Admiring the beauty of nature, he noticed two birds sitting on a tree branch. "See how blissful those two birds are, and how innocent and pure is their love for each other," he smiled. 

Suddenly, an arrow pierced the male bird, killing it instantly. 

"Oh God, what cruelty!" Valmiki cried out, watching the female bird mourn. In his grief and anger, he cursed the hunter. "You have slain a harmless creature. May you never prosper, O cruel man!"

To Valmiki's own surprise, his spontaneous utterance came out in Sanskrit, perfectly structured in verse with melody and rhythm. "The words! The form! Something quite new! Amazing indeed!" he realized. As it arose from his *shoka* (grief), he called the stanza a *shloka*.

That day, he had a divine visitor—Brahma, the Creator himself. 

"Everything has happened as decreed by me," Brahma smiled. "You have heard Rama's story from Narada. Write what you know, and also what is unknown to you."

"But Lord, how can I write about things I don't know?" Valmiki asked humbly.

"Whatever knowledge you do not possess shall be revealed to you," Brahma blessed him. "As long as mountains and seas exist on earth, so long will your Ramayana live."

Valmiki sat in yogic meditation and saw every incident of the Ramayana as clearly as if it were happening before him. Inspired, he wrote the glorious epic, the Ramayana, in beautiful verse. When he recited it, the shlokas held his listeners spellbound.

Years later, some disciples went deep into the woods and found a beautiful woman weeping uncontrollably. Realizing it was Sita, who had been abandoned in the forest by Rama, Valmiki hurried to her. 

"O Sita, chastest among women, do not grieve," the Rishi comforted her. "Come with me to my hermitage. Consider it your own home."

Valmiki entrusted Sita to the care of the female ascetics. In due course, she was blessed with twin boys, Luv and Kush. They were brought up and educated by Valmiki, who also taught them to recite the Ramayana to the accompaniment of the veena.

One day, news arrived that Rama was performing the Ashwamedha (horse sacrifice). Valmiki instructed the boys, "Meet Rama and sing the Ramayana before him. If Rama asks about your father, merely say you are Valmiki's disciples."

At the venue of the sacrifice in Naimisha, Rama and his assembly were entranced by the singing of the boys. "Wonderful poetry and music," Rama praised. But when he offered them a reward of eighteen thousand gold coins, the boys refused, saying they did not accept money. 

Soon, the truth dawned on Rama that the boys were his own sons and Sita was their mother. He immediately sent a messenger to Valmiki, asking him to bring Sita back, provided she would swear her purity before the assembly.

Sita agreed, saying, "I will do anything my husband commands."

The inmates of the hermitage bade her a tearful farewell as she and Valmiki arrived at the sacrifice. 

"I am honoured by your visit, O great sage," Rama greeted him. 

"O Rama, I take full responsibility on behalf of Sita. I do solemnly swear that she is pure in mind and body," Valmiki declared. 

"I will be delighted if Sita herself swears," Rama requested. 

With quiet dignity, Sita prayed, "With Earth, my mother, as witness, I swear that I have always been faithful to my husband."

Instantly, a miracle took place. The earth opened up, and Bhoomi Devi (Mother Earth) rose on a magnificent throne. "Sita, my dear daughter, I have come to take you," the goddess said gently. She lifted Sita onto the throne, and together they disappeared into the earth, leaving a heartbroken Rama calling out her name. 

Having fulfilled his duty, Valmiki left the scene and quietly returned to his hermitage to continue his ascetic life. Through the centuries, his work has lived on, continuing to inspire generations, just as Brahma had promised.
