# Pareekshit

Pareekshit was the posthumous son of the valiant Abhimanyu and the grandson of the legendary Pandava prince, Arjuna. Miraculously saved by Lord Krishna while still in his mother's womb from a fatal celestial weapon, Pareekshit grew up to be a strong and noble king. Two generations after the great Mahabharata war, he ruled the kingdom of Hastinapura with justice and valor. 

King Pareekshit was incredibly fond of hunting. One hot afternoon, while enthusiastically pursuing a deer deep in the forest, the tired and thirsty king stumbled upon a small, quiet hermitage. An old sage sat outside, his eyes closed in deep meditation. 

"Revered sir, I am thirsty. May I have some milk?" the king requested respectfully. 

The sage did not reply. Pareekshit did not know that the holy man was observing a strict *mouna*—a vow of absolute silence. Feeling deeply slighted and insulted by the apparent lack of courtesy, the king's temper flared. Looking around, he spotted a dead serpent lying in the grass. Using the tip of his bow, the arrogant king picked up the dead snake and deliberately draped it around the meditating sage's neck before storming off. 

A young Brahmana boy witnessed this disrespectful act and rushed to inform the sage's hot-tempered son, Shringi. 

Furious that a king would dare insult his pious father in such a manner, Shringi immediately drew water from the river and pronounced a terrible curse: "Within seven days, the mighty serpent king Takshaka shall bite and kill the wretch who dared place a dead snake on my father's shoulders!"

When the sage finally broke his vow of silence and learned what his son had done, he was deeply saddened. He immediately sent a disciple to Hastinapura to warn the king. King Pareekshit was overwhelmed with profound remorse for his foolish, impulsive act, but a curse once uttered could not be taken back. 

Determined to protect the king, his councillors devised a brilliant defensive plan. They rapidly constructed a magnificent, heavily fortified palace at the very top of a massive, smooth marble pillar. King Pareekshit moved into this inaccessible tower, surrounded by hundreds of chanting priests and expert physicians. Every single article of food or water was meticulously tasted and inspected before reaching the king. 

Meanwhile, a poor but highly skilled Brahmana named Kashyapa, who possessed the ultimate magical antidote for the most deadly snake venom, heard of the king's plight. Hoping to earn a handsome reward, he began the long journey to Hastinapura. 

On the way, he was stopped by a stranger. It was the serpent king, Takshaka, traveling in human disguise. 
"Where are you going, my friend?" Takshaka asked. 
When Kashyapa revealed his mission, Takshaka laughed. "I am Takshaka! You can never revive the king if I bite him."

"If you don't believe me, I'll demonstrate my powers," Kashyapa challenged. 
Assuming his true, terrifying serpentine form, Takshaka violently bit into a massive banyan tree, injecting it with his most potent venom. Within seconds, the enormous tree withered and burned entirely to ashes. 

Unperturbed, Kashyapa calmly collected the ashes, sprinkled a special liquid over them, and chanted powerful mantras. Before Takshaka's astonished eyes, the ashes miraculously reformed into wood, branches, and leaves, perfectly reviving the entire tree!

Worried that this Brahmana would foil his curse, Takshaka resorted to bribery. "O pious Brahman, if it is wealth that takes you to the king, I can give you more wealth than you could ever hope to get from him," Takshaka offered smoothly, producing a massive pile of magical gold. "Accept it and go in peace."
Tempted by the immediate, unimaginable wealth, the poor Kashyapa happily accepted the bribe and turned back. 

With the only man who could stop him gone, Takshaka proceeded to Hastinapura. On the fateful seventh day, knowing the tower was heavily guarded against any snakes, Takshaka used his dark magic to shrink himself to the size of a tiny worm. He bored his way deep into a luscious fruit hidden within a basket of offerings being carried to the king by a group of seemingly harmless Brahmanas. 

The guards thoroughly checked the men but found only fresh fruits, allowing them to enter the king's chamber. 
King Pareekshit happily accepted the basket. Picking up a particularly beautiful fruit, he noticed a tiny worm crawling on the skin. 

"It is said that worms infest only the tastiest of fruit," the king smiled, feeling a false sense of security as the sun began to set on the seventh day. "I'll remove it and eat the fruit."

He casually flicked the worm onto his shoulder. Instantly, the tiny worm rapidly expanded, transforming into the massive, terrifying form of Takshaka! With a lightning-fast strike, the serpent king violently strangled Pareekshit, delivered a fatal bite, and vanished through the high window with a mighty swish. The great king fell dead on the floor. 

Pareekshit’s young son, Janamejaya, was immediately crowned king. He grew up to be a wise and just ruler, but when he finally learned the true, treacherous details of his father's assassination from his ministers, his grief turned into a blinding, obsessive rage. 

"Takshaka must die!" King Janamejaya roared. "I will not rest till every Naga (serpent) in the world is utterly destroyed!"

The king ordered his priests to immediately commence the *Sarpasatra*—a terrifying, apocalyptic sacrifice designed to entirely annihilate the Naga race. A massive sacrificial fire was lit, and the powerful priests began chanting ancient, irresistible mantras. 

As the priests called out the name of each Naga, the dark magic dragged the screaming serpents through the air, helplessly casting them into the blazing fire. Thousands upon thousands of snakes were mercilessly consumed by the flames. 

But Takshaka was nowhere to be seen. Terrified, the serpent king had fled to the heavens and sought the absolute protection of Lord Indra, wrapping himself tightly around the god's throne. 

Realizing where Takshaka was hiding, the head priest dangerously intensified the mantras. "O Nagas, I charge you! Let Indra come with Takshaka!"

High in the heavens, the irresistible pull of the magic grabbed hold of both Takshaka and Lord Indra. They were violently dragged down through the clouds toward the roaring sacrificial fire. Looking down at the terrifying flames, the panicked Indra cowardly abandoned his protection, violently shaking Takshaka off and fleeing back to the heavens. 

As the screaming Takshaka plummeted toward his fiery death, a young, brilliant ascetic named Astika suddenly arrived at the outer gates of the sacrifice. Astika was the son of a great sage and a Naga princess, making him uniquely sympathetic to both sides. 

"Tishtha! Tishtha!" (Stay! Stay!) Astika commanded in a voice ringing with immense spiritual authority. The sheer power of his command instantly froze Takshaka in mid-air, suspended directly above the roaring flames. 

Impressed by the young man's radiant aura and profound wisdom, King Janamejaya warmly welcomed him into the enclosure. "Pray sir, what do you seek?" the king offered generously. "Ask, and it shall be given."

"O King, I have a single request," Astika replied firmly. "Stop this sacrifice. Let all those Nagas who are still alive, live in peace. I personally promise that they will never harm you."

Janamejaya was utterly taken aback and deeply conflicted, his desire for vengeance warring with his solemn promise to grant the young sage's wish. However, guided by his wise ministers who reminded him of the importance of keeping his royal word, Janamejaya finally relented and ordered the terrifying sacrifice to be stopped. 

The surviving Nagas joyously hailed Astika as their ultimate savior, and King Janamejaya finally found a profound, lasting peace that vengeance could never have provided him.
