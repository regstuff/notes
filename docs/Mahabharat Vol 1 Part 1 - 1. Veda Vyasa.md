# Veda Vyasa

Rishi Krishna-Dwaipayana was profoundly revered as 'Veda Vyasa' because he had painstakingly analyzed, divided, and interpreted the ancient Vedas. A timeless seer of immense wisdom, Vyasa spent three long years deep in meditation, contemplating the lives, philosophies, and destinies of his ancestors and descendants. 

He dwelt extensively on the circumstances that would inevitably lead to a great, cataclysmic war amongst his grandsons—the Kauravas and the Pandavas. In his infinite wisdom, he saw their actions not as stark black or white, but in shades of grey, recognizing a profound universality in the human condition. 

As Vyasa pondered how he could possibly record this massive, complex history for the benefit of all mankind, Lord Brahma, the Creator, appeared before him. "O divine Brahma," Vyasa bowed, "I have composed a great poem in my mind, but I cannot think of any scribe on earth capable of taking it down."
"You call your work a poem, O Muni," Brahma replied warmly, "so it shall be a poem. There shall be no poet whose work will surpass this one. Therefore, let Lord Ganesha be your scribe."

Following Brahma's advice, Vyasa invoked Lord Ganesha, the remover of obstacles. Ganesha agreed to the monumental task but laid down a strict condition: "I will be the scribe of your work only if you can dictate it to me without a single pause."
Vyasa, matching wits with the deity, replied, "And you will not inscribe anything if the meaning is not perfectly clear to you." Ganesha smiled and nodded his assent. 

With the invocation of Narayana and Saraswati, Vyasa began dictating the epic tale of *Jaya* (Victory), which came to be known as the *Mahabharata*. Whenever Vyasa needed a moment to compose the next sequence, he would deliberately recite complex, profoundly intricate verses, forcing even the omniscient Ganesha to pause and grasp their deep meaning. 

When the epic was finally complete, Vyasa taught it to his disciples—Sumanta, Jaimini, Paila, Vaishampayana—and his own son, Shuka. However, it was only after the main characters of his work had departed from the earth that Vyasa permitted the story to be recited in public. 

The opportunity arose during the *Sarpa Satra* (a massive, twelve-year-long snake sacrifice) conducted by King Janamejaya, the great-grandson of Arjuna. Seeing Vyasa and his disciples at the pavilion, King Janamejaya bowed respectfully. "O Sage, you have been a witness to the terrible feud between the Kauravas and the Pandavas," the king requested. "I wish to hear from you the true history of those events."

Vyasa instructed his disciple, Vaishampayana, to narrate the epic to the king. Vaishampayana began by tracing the illustrious history of the Bharata race, starting from the ancient times when kings ruled with pure virtue.

He told the story of King Dushyanta, who while hunting in the forest, discovered a beautiful maiden named Shakuntala in the ashrama of Sage Kanwa. Shakuntala was the daughter of the celestial apsara Menaka and the sage Vishwamitra, but was raised by Sage Kanwa. Captivated by her grace, King Dushyanta married her in a secret *Gandharva* wedding. Before returning to his capital, he promised to send for her. 

In due time, Shakuntala gave birth to a son of extraordinary strength, who fearlessly played with wild lions and tigers. Recognizing the boy's destiny, Sage Kanwa sent Shakuntala and her son to Dushyanta's court. However, fearing public scandal because the marriage was secret, Dushyanta publicly denied knowing her. As Shakuntala turned to leave in righteous anger, a celestial voice thundered from the heavens, commanding the king to accept his lawful wife and son. Dushyanta happily complied, revealing he had only feigned ignorance to clear her name before his subjects. The boy, Bharata, grew up to be a legendary emperor whose name defined the entire race. 

Vaishampayana then traced the lineage down to King Uparichara (Vasu), whose seed was miraculously swallowed by an apsara cursed to live as a fish in the Yamuna river. When caught by fishermen, the fish was cut open, revealing two human infants. The boy became King Matsya, but the girl, who carried a strong odor of fish, was raised by the fisherman chief and named Satyavati. She grew up ferrying passengers across the river.

One misty morning, the great Sage Parashara boarded her ferry. Struck by her beauty, the sage created a dense, miraculous fog to hide them and asked for her hand. Touched by his divine grace, Satyavati consented, but asked that her fishy odor be replaced with a sweet fragrance. Parashara granted the boon, transforming her into *Gandhavati* (the sweet-scented one). 

On an island in the river, Satyavati gave birth to a son of immense spiritual energy. Because he was dark-skinned and born on an island (*dweep*), he was named Krishna Dwaipayana. Leaving his mother with a promise to appear whenever she needed him, the boy departed to live a life of asceticism. 

This boy grew up to become the legendary sage who arranged the Vedas and composed the Mahabharata—the great Veda Vyasa himself.
