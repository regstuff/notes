# The Clever Dancer

Muladeva was a young Brahmin of Ujjaini, a man of great knowledge and ingenuity who could defeat any pundit or scholar in debates upon the scriptures — an acknowledged authority on the Vedas. One day his close friend Shashi came to him with a suggestion. "You are a master in the art of oratory, and your fame has travelled far and wide. Why not go to Pataliputra and earn greater glory?" "A fine suggestion, friend," Muladeva said. "I can test my mettle in that home of polished wit and refinement."

On the outskirts of the great city, they found a woman washing clothes and asked her where travellers found lodging. "I know that ducks nest on the river banks, fish live in the water, bees nest in lotuses — but I haven't the faintest idea where travellers stay!" she said, sending them off dumbfounded by her deliberate riddling. It only got stranger. A little further on they found a small boy weeping loudly over a plate of warm rice pudding. "Only a foolish child weeps when such delicious food is before him," Shashi said. The boy laughed at them. "Only fools fail to understand why I cry. I have to wait for the pudding to cool before I can eat it — crying clears my throat and exercises my lungs. But country bumpkins like you cannot understand all this!" Shashi and Muladeva could only marvel, half-offended, at such precocious cheek.

Then, crossing a forest, they came upon a young woman gathering mangoes in a tree. "Give us some mangoes, fair one," Muladeva called up. "Hot or cold?" she shot back. "Some warm ones first," he answered, thinking himself clever — and she threw some down, which he and Shashi dutifully blew the dust from before eating. "Were they too warm?" she called from the branches, laughing. "I noticed you blew on them. They are quite cool — you can save your breath." Muladeva, the great wit of Ujjaini, felt genuinely humbled for the first time. "She is the wittiest creature I've met," he told Shashi. "I'll get this clever girl for a wife, and teach her a lesson or two."

They soon learned she was the daughter of a rich Brahmin named Yajnaswami, and lost no time acting on it — disguising themselves as humble travelling Brahmins the very next day and presenting themselves at his door, confident that "no one will recognize us." Bold enough now to bargain, they told Yajnaswami they would only stay as his guests on one condition: that after four months, he grant them whatever they asked for. He agreed readily, and for four months the two friends lived under his roof, crossing paths often with his daughter — who never once recognised the "country bumpkins" from the forest, though Muladeva privately fretted that he still hadn't gotten the better of her.

When their four months were up and Yajnaswami, delighted with such stimulating company, told them to name their reward, Muladeva asked for his daughter's hand in marriage. "Have they tricked me?" Yajnaswami wondered — but the young man was learned and eligible besides, and he consented readily enough. Alone with his new bride in the bridal chamber that night, Muladeva finally revealed himself. "Do you recognise me? Those warm and cool mangoes..." Far from alarmed, she only smiled. "Country bumpkins are too slow for city wits." "I, the country bumpkin," he told her, "am about to desert you and go far away. And you, the city wit, will stay here and fret!" Unshaken, she threw the challenge back at him: "You will come back to me — as father of my son." In the dead of night, while she slept, Muladeva slipped a ring engraved with his own name onto her finger before slipping away to meet Shashi outside, and the two friends left for Ujjaini.

Waking to find her husband gone and a ring on her finger identifying him as "the renowned Muladeva of Ujjaini," she went to her father. "My husband has deserted me. I wish to leave on a pilgrimage," she told him — a fiction to cover her true purpose. "My child, I understand," he said, and let her go, equipped with money and attendants. On the road she bought herself a fine costume, and arrived outside Ujjaini with much fanfare, dressed as a dancer and travelling under a new name: the Lady Sumangala, from the city of Kamarupa. Word of the beautiful newcomer spread fast — a distinguished local dancer named Devadatta sought her out first, impressed enough by her reputation to gift her a whole mansion to live in.

News of Sumangala's charm soon reached Shashi, who sent a messenger with gifts and greetings. "Tell your master I do not set much store by presents," she replied. "As for his greetings, I wish he had come with them in person." That night Shashi went himself, dressed in his finest — and found her palace guarded by four successive doors, each with its own toll: a bath at the first, fine clothes at the second, a leisurely meal at the third, each delay swallowing an entire watch of the night, until at the fourth and final door — the one leading to her private apartment — the doorkeeper turned him away outright. "A fine gentleman you are, to visit a lady at this unearthly hour! Go away, good sir, or you'll drag misfortune upon your head!" Crestfallen, Shashi went home and admitted to Muladeva what had happened. "Outdone by a mere dancer! Friend, I swear to get the better of this woman," Muladeva vowed.

That night he tried the same four gates himself, but bribed his way through each one with jewels — a gold-and-pearl casket, a diamond necklace — until he stood before Sumangala herself, unrecognized by his own deserted wife. "Words are too poor to describe your loveliness," he told her. "Indeed, you speak prettily," she answered, playing the part, while thinking to herself, "Now I have you in my power, you learned fool!" She entertained him royally, and he returned again and again, until one day she told him plainly: "Muladeva, I came to this city not only to taste its refinements, but to find a suitable husband. You are the man I want to marry." "That would be my heart's desire," he answered — and so, without ever recognising his own bride, Muladeva married Sumangala a second time and moved into her mansion.

A few days later, she announced she had to return to Pataliputra — a summons from her king, she claimed — and left Ujjaini heartbroken but sure they would meet again. In due course, back home, Sumangala gave birth to a son.

Twelve years passed. The boy grew up skilled in many accomplishments, taught by his mother, and one day, playing with other boys, was taunted for having no known father: "Weaklings! Children of weaklings!" he shot back, only for the other boys to retort, "Son of a strong father — but do you know who your father is?" He came home upset and demanded the truth. His mother finally told him everything: "Your father's name is Muladeva. He deserted me and went away to Ujjaini." Undaunted, the boy declared, "I will bring back my father — a captive!" — and armed with everything his mother could tell him about how to identify the man, he set out for Ujjaini alone.

There he tracked Muladeva to a gambling den, joined the dice game uninvited, and won money from everyone at the table with startling skill — "You play exceedingly well! You play like a man!" — before giving all his winnings away to beggars on his way out. That night, while Muladeva slept, the boy crept into his chamber, gently eased him off his own bed onto a pile of soft cotton on the floor, and made off with the entire bedstead. Muladeva woke the next morning thoroughly confused to find himself on the ground with his bed gone.

He spotted the boy selling the very same bedstead in the market square days later. "What is the price of this bedstead?" he asked. "I will exchange it for a strange and wonderful tale," the boy answered — and issued a challenge: if Muladeva could truly understand the riddle he was about to pose, the bedstead was his; if not, he would become the boy's servant. Muladeva agreed, and the boy told an elaborate riddling tale of a boar's beloved covered in showers from "chariots of snakes," ending a famine. Baffled, Muladeva could only offer a wrong interpretation, and the boy laughed and explained it plainly: the clouds were the chariots, the earth was the boar's beloved from Vishnu's Varaha incarnation, and the whole tale simply meant that rain from the clouds had made grain grow. Undaunted, Muladeva tried a second riddle of his own — about a wonderful child whose birth made the earth tremble, who grew bigger and stepped into another world — only to be beaten again: the boy recognised it at once as Vishnu's own Vamana avatar, growing from dwarf to giant to reclaim the heavens.

Humbled and bound now by his own wager, Muladeva owned himself defeated and agreed to follow the boy wherever he led — straight to Pataliputra, and straight to the boy's mother. "What! Sumangala?" Muladeva said, stunned. "Yes — but also your deserted wife," she told him. "Today, as I vowed, you have been brought here by our son." The trick she had played on him years before, and the promise she'd made as he slipped that ring onto her finger — that he would return to her one day as the father of her son — had come true after all, delivered by the very child neither of them had known existed until he came looking, and won, at every game his father set before him.
