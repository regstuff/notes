# Swami Chinmayananda: Modern Life Meets Ancient Wisdom

It was the end of a warm summer's day in 1916. The large rambling family house cooled down as the pleasant breeze wafted in through the wide verandahs of sun-dried mudbricks in Ernakulam, Kerala. To the soft murmur of swaying palms and whispering waves was added a new sound—the hearty cry of a new-born baby.

The birth of the first-born was an occasion for much joy and celebration for Kuttan Menon and Manku.

"Kuttan! It's a boy! As predicted!" a relative rejoiced.

"But of course! Just as our family priest foretold," Kuttan smiled.

An astrologer was immediately sent for to cast the baby's horoscope. "Let me see the position of the stars," he muttered, studying his charts. "26th mesham 1091 of the Kollum era. At 7:30 the star Pushya ascending in the Raja Yoga. Hm! Auspicious birth indeed!"

The new father's chest swelled with pride. Vadakke Kurupath Kuttan Menon was a judge at the local court in Ernakulam. "Perhaps he will follow in my footsteps and become a lawyer," he said.

"He is destined to be a King!" replied Manku, gazing at her newborn with affection. 

The namakarana samskara, or naming ceremony, was performed on the fourth day by Chattambi Swamigal, a learned saint and frequent visitor to the house. "O child! Thou art the devotee of the family deity. Thy name is Balakrishnan," the saint declared.

Balakrishnan, or Balan for short, received all the rituals prescribed by the scriptures. Whenever Chattambi Swamigal visited, he would place Balan on his chest and rock him gently. Both of them would prattle on endlessly.

"What on earth do you say to him?" Manku asked, mystified.

"It's between him and me. I've taught him everything!" Swamigal would laugh. He always repeated the same phrase after each visit: "Take care of the child! But initially he will have to suffer much. Yes, we remember your prediction that he will become great."

Tragically, just after the birth of his second sister, Balan's mother died. His aunt, Kochu Amma, lovingly took over his upbringing. "A plane! A plane! Amma, look! I'm also going to fly a plane when I grow up," young Balan would shout. "I'll lean out and yell, 'Hello, Amma,' from the sky!"

The family was deeply devout. Balan would line up in the family prayer room every evening. It was not out of choice but compulsion—no prayer, no dinner. To keep himself alert during the two hours of prayers, Balan began to day-dream and invent private games about the gods lined up on the wall. 

*Lord Shiva is my favourite. Let me see how long I can concentrate on Him,* Balan thought. *I'll stare and stare at the picture.... Now when I close my eyes tight, I'll try to remember it in complete detail.* Accidentally, young Balan had discovered a powerful technique of meditation.

At school, Balan was brilliant. His father would marvel, "Well, well! Balan has once again topped the score in Malayalam and English." 

"When on earth does he study? I always find him playing," his aunt wondered. 

Balan's childhood habit of questioning everything grew with age. "Why should there be a God?" he challenged his uncle. 

"God is the sum of all knowledge and goodness. We are each of us a ray of that sun," his uncle explained.

But the arrogance of adolescence warped his views. "If I am a ray of God, it is my presence that makes the existence of God visible. So Balan is the source of God, not God the source of Balan," he argued.

In college, Balan grew into a flamboyant young man. He dressed in silk suits, played tennis, and excelled in literature. He was deeply moved by the freedom struggle, and when Mahatma Gandhi launched the Quit India Movement in 1942, Balan joined the cause. "Our first priority is our country!" he told his classmates.

Soon, Balan was deeply involved—writing pamphlets and giving speeches. When the British began cracking down on student leaders, an arrest warrant was issued for him. "The British are after you," a friend warned.

Balan went underground, fleeing to Kashmir. To survive, he took a job at a British Military Intelligence Communication Centre in Abbottabad, relaying coded messages. His boss was impressed: "Young man! You are good at your job. Too good! Why did you settle for this job?" Balan eventually confided in him, and the supportive boss gave him a safe place to stay.

After saving some money, Balan left the job and rejoined the Quit India movement. But this time, he was recognized and thrown into a dark, disease-ridden prison. He soon fell ill with typhus.

"Is he in a stupor or is he dead?" a guard asked.

"If he isn't, he soon will be. Throw him out at night," another replied.

The next morning, a feeble Balan lay immobile on the roadside. By a stroke of incredible luck, a Christian lady passing by spotted him. "Stop! Driver! That boy! His profile is just like my son's," she cried. She took him into her home and nursed him back to health.

After recovering, Balan moved to Baroda, staying with his cousin. There, using an old typewriter, he began writing articles for national newspapers. "When I write, I pretend I'm a mochi, a cobbler, so that I can write from a common man's angle," he explained.

Despite his success as a journalist in Delhi—writing for the National Herald under the pen name 'Mr Tramp'—Balan felt a deep emptiness. *What a hollow life! So unsatisfactory,* he thought. He began studying philosophy in the evenings, turning back to his childhood mantra, *Om Namah Shivaya*.

Seeking answers, he traveled to Rishikesh to expose the 'bluff' of holy men. "I'm going to find out how those holy men are keeping up the bluff!" he declared.

But when he met Swami Sivananda, he was astonished. *He gives such elevating spiritual discourses, yet humbly serves fruits and tea to visitors! He exudes such dynamic peace,* Balan realized. 

"God gave you such intelligence! Why don't you use it for Him? You can join us and become a sannyasi," Swami Sivananda suggested.

After returning to Delhi, the sudden death of his close friend Shroff shook Balan to the core. Realizing the impermanence of life, he returned to Rishikesh. "When I left for Delhi, I came with a pile of books. Now, I am ready," he thought.

On 25th February 1949, on Shivaratri day, Balakrishna Menon was initiated into sannyasa. Plunging into the icy Ganga, he chanted, "I renounce all desire for wealth, spouse, name or fame."

"You will be called Chinmayananda Saraswati," Swami Sivananda declared.

Swami Chinmayananda's questioning mind hungered for deeper knowledge. "I feel I have not caught the true importance of what our wise sages wrote," he admitted. Sivananda sent him to study under the strict and austere Swami Tapovanam in the freezing heights of Gangotri.

After rigorous study, Chinmayananda felt a calling. "Is this what they are doling out as Hinduism? The real truth needs to be taken to the masses," he told his guru. "I want to share all the knowledge with people from all walks of life."

Swami Tapovanam blessed the mission. "It's a mammoth task. Make sure you have at least four people in the audience," he smiled.

In 1951, Swami Chinmayananda held his first 'Jnana Yajna' in Pune. Armed with only 25 paise and a trunk of books, he began speaking in English, breaking the traditional barrier of Sanskrit to make the ancient Vedic texts accessible to the modern Indian. 

"In the old days, Yajnas were held to honour the gods. Our Jnana Yajna is a different form of sacrifice. We burn our ignorance in the fire of knowledge," he taught.

The movement exploded. Devotees flocked to hear his logical, witty, and persuasive discourses. "He is reading my mind and speaking only to me!" listeners would marvel. He established study groups, Balavihar classes for children, and Yuva Kendras for the youth. "Children are lamps to be lit, not vessels to be filled," he emphasized.

In 1953, devotees organized the 'Chinmaya Mission' to spread true knowledge. Swami Chinmayananda traveled the globe, lecturing at prestigious universities like MIT, Stanford, and Harvard. "Happiness equals the number of desires fulfilled divided by the number of desires entertained," he would explain. "Our Indian rishis focused on reducing the denominator. The modern world tries to increase the numerator."

Despite suffering multiple heart attacks, Swamiji never slowed down. "The further I go, the nearer I shall be to each one of you!" he promised his devotees.

With words as his tools, Swami Chinmayananda worked for 42 years to rebuild India, heart by heart. By the end of his life, he had conducted 576 Jnana Yajnas across the world. On August 3, 1993, at the age of 77, the great sage left his mortal frame and attained Mahasamadhi in San Diego, leaving behind a global legacy of spiritual awakening.
