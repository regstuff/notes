# Padmini of Chittor

King Ratnasen, the valiant Rajput ruler of Chittor, was a great lover of beauty and the fine arts. One day, a traveling merchant presented him with an exquisite, lifelike statue of a woman. Spellbound by the statue's perfection, the king asked who it depicted. 

"This is Princess Padmini of Sinhala Island, Maharaj," the merchant replied. "She is renowned as the most beautiful woman in the world."

Intrigued and completely captivated, King Ratnasen embarked on a long, arduous journey across the seas to Sinhala Island. There, he won the heart of Princess Padmini and married her. Accompanied by her fiercely loyal relatives, the brave warriors Gora and Badal, Padmini traveled to Chittor and was joyously crowned the Maharani. 

Life in Chittor was blissful until a dark shadow fell over the court. King Ratnasen had a highly learned but arrogant courtier named Pandit Raghav Chetan. One day, during a scholarly debate, Raghav Chetan made a deliberate, deceptive miscalculation about the phases of the moon to win an argument. The king, discovering the deceit, was furious. Despite Padmini's gentle pleas for mercy, King Ratnasen banished the dishonest Pandit from his kingdom. 

Humiliated and burning with a desire for revenge, Raghav Chetan traveled to Delhi. There, he sought an audience with the ruthless Sultan, Ala-ud-din Khilji. Knowing the Sultan's insatiable lust for wealth and beauty, Raghav Chetan played upon his greed. 
"You conquer kingdoms, O Sultan," Raghav sneered smoothly, "but you do not possess the greatest treasure in the world—Queen Padmini of Chittor, whose beauty puts the celestial apsaras to shame."

Inflamed by the Pandit's vivid descriptions, Ala-ud-din Khilji immediately marched his massive army to Chittor. However, upon reaching the impregnable fortress, he realized that a direct assault would be futile. Resorting to cunning deceit, the Sultan sent a messenger to King Ratnasen. 
"Our Sultan comes in peace," the messenger lied. "He merely wishes to look upon Queen Padmini, whom he considers his sister, before returning to Delhi."

Ratnasen was outraged, but Padmini, wishing to avoid a bloody war, offered a compromise. "I will not show him my face directly," she told her husband. "He may look at my reflection in a mirror."

Ala-ud-din entered the fort with a small, heavily armed retinue. When he was led into the palace, he looked into a mirror and saw Padmini's reflection. Her beauty far exceeded even Raghav Chetan's descriptions, and the Sultan was instantly consumed by an obsessive desire to possess her. 

Concealing his dark intentions, the Sultan cordially thanked the king and began to take his leave. As customary for a gracious host, King Ratnasen escorted his royal guest to the outer gates of the fort. But the moment they stepped outside the secure inner walls, Ala-ud-din's hidden soldiers ambushed the unsuspecting Rajput king, captured him, and carried him away to the Sultan's camp. 

Ala-ud-din immediately sent a devastating ultimatum to Chittor: *Submit Queen Padmini to my harem, or King Ratnasen shall be executed.*

The fort was plunged into despair. But the brilliant Queen Padmini did not break. She summoned her valiant relatives, Gora and Badal. Together, they devised a daring rescue plan. 

A message was sent to the Sultan's camp: *Queen Padmini agrees to come to Delhi, but she must be accompanied by her retinue of five hundred maids to maintain her royal dignity.*

The Sultan, blinded by his impending triumph, eagerly agreed. The next morning, a procession of five hundred closely guarded palanquins departed from Chittor and entered the Sultan's camp. 

But when the palanquin doors opened, out poured not weeping maids, but five hundred of Chittor's deadliest, heavily armed Rajput warriors! 
Taking the enemy completely by surprise, the Rajputs slashed through the guards and freed King Ratnasen. In the fierce, bloody skirmish that followed, the heroic Gora sacrificed his own life to hold off the Sultan's forces, allowing Ratnasen, Padmini, and Badal to safely escape back into the impenetrable fort of Chittor. 

Furious at being so thoroughly humiliated, Ala-ud-din ordered a merciless siege of Chittor. For months, the massive army surrounded the fort, cutting off all supplies. Eventually, the food and water within the walls began to dwindle. The situation became completely hopeless. 

Recognizing that surrender to the ruthless invader was a fate worse than death, King Ratnasen and his warriors made a grim, solemn decision. They would ride out to fight to the death. 

To protect their honor from the invading army, Queen Padmini led the Rajput women in the ultimate, terrifying sacrifice—*Jauhar*. A massive funeral pyre was lit deep within the fort's inner chambers. Chanting holy prayers, the brave queen and all the women of Chittor walked willingly into the roaring flames, choosing to be consumed by fire rather than fall into enemy hands. 

The next morning, King Ratnasen, Badal, and the remaining Rajput warriors donned the saffron robes of martyrs. Flinging the fort gates wide open, they charged roaring into the vast ocean of the Sultan's army. They fought with the terrifying fury of men who had nothing left to lose, taking thousands of enemies down with them before finally falling on the battlefield. 

When the victorious Ala-ud-din Khilji finally breached the gates and stormed into the fort, eager to claim his prize, he was met with a haunting silence. The mighty fortress was entirely empty. Reaching the inner chambers, the Sultan found nothing but the smoldering, silent ashes of the great pyre. 

"But why did they kill themselves?" the frustrated, utterly bewildered Sultan asked his minister as he stared at the ashes. 
The minister sighed deeply. "Your Majesty... you will never understand."
