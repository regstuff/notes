# Tales from the Upanishads - 1. Shvetaketu

In a hermitage deep in the forest lived the highly learned sage, Uddalaka Aruni, with his young son, Shvetaketu.

One day, looking at his son, the sage thought, *Shvetaketu has turned twelve... He must now pursue the study of the scriptures.*

"Shvetaketu, my child, please come to me," Uddalaka called out.

"What is it, father?" Shvetaketu asked politely.

"Dear child, everyone in our family has, without exception, studied the scriptures and imbibed their deepest meaning," the sage explained. "It is time for you too, to tread the path of learning. Go forth son, and come back bright and resplendent, with the knowledge of the Brahman."

Following his father's command, Shvetaketu left the hermitage to study under another learned rishi. Under his able guru, Shvetaketu spent the next twelve years reading the scriptures, learning the sciences, studying complex philosophy, and mastering the arts.

At the end of twelve long years, Shvetaketu returned home. He was incredibly proud of his accomplishments. *I have now mastered nearly everything that a man can study!* he thought smugly.

"Pranam, father!" Shvetaketu said, bowing before Uddalaka.

"O, Shvetaketu, how you have grown!" the sage smiled, blessing him. "Shvetaketu, how did you fare in your studies at the gurukula?"

Shvetaketu puffed out his chest. "Very well, O father! I have mastered all the arts and sciences and I know everything that can be known..."

Uddalaka sighed inwardly. *Oh! What pride! Such conceit is born only out of ignorance,* he thought. *This boy has not grasped the true essence of the supreme knowledge—Brahman.*

Shvetaketu noticed his father's sudden quietness. "Why have you fallen silent, father?" he asked.

"Dear child, you say you know everything that can be known..." Uddalaka said carefully. "Then do you possess that knowledge or wisdom by which what is *unknown* becomes known; what is *unseen* becomes seen?"

Shvetaketu was completely stumped. "Pray, father, do tell me what that knowledge is..." he asked humbly, his pride deflating.

"Well son, look at those pots and toys—all of them are made of clay," Uddalaka pointed. "It is the lump of clay that, when fashioned out in a certain shape, becomes a vessel, and when given another shape, becomes a toy. So the true essence of all such things is clay. The toys and vessels are merely different names given to it."

Uddalaka continued, "So by knowing a single lump of clay, all that is made of clay can be known; by knowing a nugget of gold, all that is made of gold can be known; and by knowing a piece of iron, all that is made of iron can be known! Therefore, my child, you must get to know the essence of all things—the one thing that exists in everything in this universe."

"I still do not understand, father," Shvetaketu admitted.

"These rivers, my child, proceed from the east towards the west, then from the ocean they rise in the form of vapour and dropping again as rain, merge back into the ocean," Uddalaka explained. "Just as the rivers do not remember what they were, so also all created beings do not know that they have issued from truth."

The sage pointed outside. "Look at the tree over there. If you were to strike at that tree, it would discharge a little sap. Pervaded by life, it would continue to draw sustenance from the earth. But when life forsakes one of its branches, it dries up! Each branch forsaken by life, dries up. When the entire tree is forsaken by life, the whole tree dries up..."

"Similarly, my son, when life forsakes the body, it dies. But life itself does not die," Uddalaka smiled. "That which does not die is *Atman*. You are that Atman, which is all-pervasive."

"Why can't I see this being which is all-pervasive?" Shvetaketu challenged.

"Well Shvetaketu, bring me a fruit from that banyan tree."

Shvetaketu picked a banyan fruit and took it to his father. "Here, father."

"Break it, son," Uddalaka said. "What do you see, Shvetaketu?"

"It is broken, father. I see many tiny seeds," Shvetaketu replied.

"Break open a seed, son," the sage instructed. "What do you see now?"

Shvetaketu squinted at the broken seed. "Nothing father."

"Nothing?"

"Yes, nothing!"

Uddalaka smiled gently. "If there is truly nothing in the seed, how can that 'nothing' give rise to a mighty banyan tree? That invisible power, Shvetaketu, is the essence of all things—the Atman—that pervades the universe. You too are that, O Shvetaketu!"

"Then what is that 'something' which we cannot see, father?" Shvetaketu asked.

"If we cannot see the essence, how do we know that it exists? I shall explain to you. Put this salt in water," Uddalaka instructed.

Shvetaketu did as he was told. "Here is the pot of water with salt in it, father."

"Keep it aside for now. Bring it to me tomorrow morning," the sage said.

Early the next morning, Shvetaketu brought the pot back. 

"Can you see the salt you put into this pot last evening, Shvetaketu?" his father asked.

The salt had completely dissolved. "No father, I cannot," Shvetaketu replied.

"Now taste the water at the top of the pot. How is it?"

Shvetaketu took a sip. "Mm... it's salty."

"Now taste from the middle of the pot."

Shvetaketu dipped a finger in the middle of the pot and tasted the water. "It's salty again, father."

"And now taste the water at the bottom."

"It still is salty father."

"Now look again for the salt you put in it yesterday," Uddalaka commanded.

Shvetaketu peered into the clear water. "But I cannot see it."

"Similarly, Shvetaketu, you cannot *see* the essence of the universe. But just like the salt in the water, it is always present everywhere," Uddalaka concluded. "This omnipresent essence is Atman, that pervades everything. You too are that, O Shvetaketu."

Shvetaketu bowed deeply, finally understanding. "I am grateful, father... You have helped me gain the supreme knowledge with which the unknown becomes known, the unseen becomes seen."


# Tales from the Upanishads - 2. Raikva the Cartman

Janashruti, the king of Mahavrasha, was widely known as a benevolent and generous ruler. He built comfortable rest houses along the main roads of his kingdom, where free food and lodging were provided to weary travellers. He also built hospitals and homes to care for the sick, the needy, and the ageing.

And above all, he taxed his subjects fairly. Quite naturally, Janashruti was immensely pleased with himself for having done so much for his kingdom.

"Glory to King Janashruti!" his people would cheer whenever he appeared.

His fame spread far beyond the borders of Mahavrasha. Unfortunately, it did not take long to turn him into a vain, arrogant man. 

*Ah! It is I who am providing food to all these people. How much merit I have earned,* Janashruti thought proudly. *The devas too must be pleased with me...*

One evening, while he was relaxing on the terrace of his palace, two beautiful swans flew overhead. 

*Such lovely swans!* Janashruti thought, admiring them.

Just then, he heard them speak!

"Hey, you silly bird!" the first swan squawked to the second. "Beware of flying over King Janashruti... the bright band of light that emanates from him, due to the immense glow of his fame, will burn you to ashes."

The second swan scoffed. "Don't mock me like that. How can you speak of King Janashruti as if he were greater than Raikva of the cart?"

"Raikva?" the first swan asked.

Below them, the king froze. *Who is this Raikva of the cart, who has earned more merit than me?* Janashruti wondered, his ego deeply bruised.

Janashruti felt restless all that night. "Raikva! I must send my men tomorrow to find out who this Raikva is, where he is..." he muttered, unable to sleep.

Early the next morning, the elaborate daily ritual to wake up the royal personage began. 

"Wake up, o saviour of the masses!" the royal bard chanted. "Arise, o provider of the millions!! Arise o celestial among men!"

"Stop! Stop all this false praise!" Janashruti shouted, jumping out of bed. "You shall stop praising me thus, for I am not worthy of such praise!"

The bard was stunned. "What is it, Maharaj? What bothers our benevolent king?"

Janashruti told the bard all about the magical swans' conversation. "Go forth in all the directions and seek out Raikva, this most pious of all men," the king ordered.

A massive search was soon launched to locate Raikva. The king's men combed the entire kingdom for many days, asking everyone they met. 

At last, one day, an official approached a small village. "Have you heard of a man called Raikva? Raikva? The cart driver?"

"Yes," a villager pointed. "There, near that cart."

The official approached a simple man sitting under a cart. "Are you Raikva the cart driver?"

"Yes, I am Raikva," the man replied.

"Our king wishes to meet you," the official announced grandly.

"Well, here I am," Raikva replied simply. He did not even budge from his place to greet the royal messenger. 

The official was completely baffled. *What does our worthy king seek from an ordinary man like him?*

Regardless, Janashruti's delight knew no bounds when he was told that Raikva had finally been found. "Make preparations to leave immediately for Raikva's village," the king ordered.

Next morning, Janashruti started for Raikva's village with a massive entourage of his men. He took with him an impressive array of 600 prized cows, a heavy gold necklace, and a beautiful royal carriage as a magnificent gift to Raikva.

Arriving at the village, Janashruti introduced himself grandly. "I have heard of you as one who has earned great merit, one who has learnt all there is to learn, and one who knows Brahman. O wise Raikva, I shall present you with 600 cows, this splendid necklace, and this carriage... and whatever else that you desire."

Raikva looked completely unimpressed by the vast wealth. "Please impart to me the supreme knowledge of Brahman," the king demanded.

"O ignorant king! The knowledge of Brahman cannot be purchased," Raikva scolded him. "Knowledge is not a commodity. It cannot be bartered. Begone O Janashruti. You are not yet ready to receive the supreme knowledge."

A deeply disappointed Janashruti made his way back to the kingdom, at once sad and humbled. 

After a few weeks, when he had deeply pondered over his failed meeting with Raikva, he made preparations to meet Raikva again. *I wonder how I can convince him that I am really keen to receive the knowledge of the Brahman,* the king thought.

Once again he set out with his large entourage. This time, however, his beautiful daughter accompanied him.

On reaching the village, Janashruti did not act like a king. Instead, he prostrated himself completely on the dirt before the wise rustic. 

"O wise Raikva, last time I came to you as a king..." Janashruti said humbly. "...But I have shed my royal ego, and I surrender myself to you."

This time, Raikva saw that the king had truly lost all his vanity, and the genuine, pure desire for knowledge was evident from his eyes.

"I bless you, o virtuous Janashruti," Raikva smiled. "All the knowledge that is mine shall now be yours."

And then Raikva, drawing Janashruti close to him, spoke at length. "The supreme knowledge cannot be imbibed unless one has shed one's ego," Raikva explained. "Only the humble can perceive the Brahman. Now that you are truly humble, I will instruct you about the supreme knowledge."

And so saying, the great sage Raikva finally accepted King Janashruti as his disciple.


# Tales from the Upanishads - 3. When the Devas Were Humbled

This story was told by an ancient rishi to his disciples to illustrate how Brahman, the self-existing, all-pervading divine power, restores order in the world whenever evil and disorder seem to plague it.

"Long ago, the devas had a great war with the asuras," the rishi began. "You know, as it usually happens, the good lack strength and the strong lack goodness..."

After days of fierce fighting, it seemed that the asuras were getting the upper hand, for they were incredibly strong, unprincipled, and utterly ruthless.

And when the devas began to lose heavily, a few of the desperate gods prayed to Brahman to come to their aid. 

"O Brahman, if you do not help us in this war, only evil and disorder shall rule this earth," they prayed. "Come to our aid, O divine father."

Invigorated by a divine assurance of help, the devas fought back bravely. "Go forth and fight with all your might. Only good can triumph in the end!" they rallied each other.

With the unseen divine help of Brahman, it did not take them long to completely vanquish the fierce asuras. 

Overjoyed with their incredible victory, the devas celebrated with gay abandon. They ate, drank, and made merry for days, weeks, and months. 

"The victory is ours! We have won it!" they cheered. 

Until finally, they were utterly intoxicated by their success. "We're the greatest!!" they boasted. "We have no equals!!"

"The devas attributed the victory entirely to themselves, did they?" a disciple asked the rishi.

"Yes," the rishi nodded. "Neglecting their divine duties, they went on and on with the celebrations. Soon the worlds that the devas ruled began to fall into absolute ruin... And evil and disorder prevailed everywhere once again. That is when Brahman intervened."

One day, as the devas were completely immersed in their merry-making, a strange, glowing *yaksha* (a celestial being) appeared outside their heavenly abode. 

The bright yaksha soon caught Lord Indra's eye. 

"Go and find out who this being is and what brings him here, o Agni," Indra commanded the fire god.

Agni marched proudly out and approached the yaksha, intending to speak first. "I am Agni, the Lord of Fire..." he began grandly.

But before Agni could even finish his sentence, the yaksha asked him a simple question. "O Agni, Lord of Fire, what great powers do you possess?"

Agni scoffed at the question. "I can burn and destroy anything that exists."

The yaksha smiled slightly. He placed a single, dry blade of grass on the ground between them. "Then please come forward and burn this blade of grass—reduce it to ashes."

"What? This mere blade of grass? Ho ho haha!" Agni laughed heartily. "Yes, please, watch me burn this blade of grass."

And Agni, with all his mighty powers, ordered the divine fire to burn up that blade of grass. Agni raged and roared, and intense flames engulfed the tiny blade of grass. But no matter how incredibly hard he tried, he could not succeed. The grass remained perfectly green and unburnt. 

"Uh!" Agni grunted, straining his power to the absolute maximum, but to no avail.

After having failed to incinerate that single blade, Agni walked back to the palace, completely dejected and humbled. He wondered who this strange, powerful being was.

Indra then sent Vayu, the wind god.

Vayu strode out confidently. "I am Vayu, the Lord of the winds," he announced.

"And what great powers do you possess, O Vayu?" the yaksha asked.

"I can blow away anything from the face of this world," Vayu declared proudly. 

The yaksha pointed to the exact same blade of grass. "Then will you please try and blow away this blade of grass?"

Vayu summoned the fiercest of cosmic winds. Tornados and hurricanes blasted across the courtyard. But again, no matter how hard he tried, the tiny blade of grass just would not budge. 

"Uh!" Vayu gasped, exhausted and completely out of breath. 

Vayu too went back to the palace the way Agni had—fatigued and deeply humbled, but not realising who the yaksha actually was.

Finally, Indra himself stepped out to confront the stranger. But the moment Indra appeared, the yaksha suddenly disappeared!

Indra looked here and there for the yaksha but could not find him. Instead, his eyes fell upon Uma, the Goddess of spiritual knowledge, who happened to pass by.

"Uma!" Indra called out respectfully. "Did you see that bright, shining being who stood here until a little while ago? Where did he go? Who was he?"

And then Uma spoke, her voice filled with gentle disappointment. 

"Oh Indra! It's a shame that even you, the king of the devas, could not realise who that shining being was," Uma said. "Who could possess greater powers than Agni and Vayu? Think, O king of devas."

Indra's eyes widened in sudden realization. "You... you mean that was..."

"A manifestation of Brahman," Uma nodded. "You are blessed, Indra, that you have now realised who it was. He appeared thus in front of you only to bring you back to your senses, O devas. You have had enough of celebrations of your 'victory'. It is now time to get back to the onerous duties of ruling."

Realising their foolish vanity, the devas settled down to a much more sensible, humble life.

"And thus," the ancient rishi concluded to his disciples, "Brahman had once again brought order and method into the world."


# Tales from the Upanishads - 4. The Bold Beggar

Shaunaka Kapeya and Abhipratarin Kakshaseni were two wise sages who lived together in a quiet hermitage deep in the forest. They spent most of their time devoutly worshipping *Vayu*, the cosmic God of Wind.

One afternoon, as the two hungry sages sat down to begin their meal...

...a young *brahmachari* (a student ascetic) arrived at their hermitage with his begging bowl outstretched.

Before he could even ask for alms, one of the sages shook his head firmly. "No, my boy, we cannot spare you any food today. We have only just enough for ourselves."

The young brahmachari did not leave. Instead, he smiled politely and asked, "Learned sirs, could you tell me which deva you worship?"

"Vayu, the wind god," the sage replied proudly. "He is also known as *Prana* or the cosmic breath of life."

"Ah!" the young student smiled. "Then you must surely know that Prana pervades the entire universe... And that Prana is all that moves and does not move; all that is visible and invisible..."

"Yes, yes... we know all that," the sages muttered impatiently, eager to begin their meal.

"Now, respected sirs, could you tell me to whom did you make this food-offering before sitting down to eat?" the brahmachari asked smoothly.

"To Prana, who else?" the sage replied, slightly annoyed.

"If Prana truly pervades the universe, it pervades me too," the bold beggar pointed out logically. "Who am I but a part of the universe... am I not?"

The sages looked at each other. "Uh... yes, you are," they conceded.

"It is Prana that pulsates in me, as this hungry body of mine stands before you and speaks..." the young boy explained.

"You speak the absolute truth, o young Brahmana!" the sages agreed, struck by his profound logic.

"Then o revered rishis, in denying *me* food, you are actually denying Prana for whom you have prepared the food!" the brahmachari concluded with a triumphant smile.

The sages, who so far had only understood the literal meaning of the scriptures, realised then the true, living spirit behind the holy words. 

Deeply ashamed at their own ignorance and selfishness, they gladly and immediately shared their food with the wise young Brahmana!
