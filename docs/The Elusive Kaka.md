# The Elusive Kaka

Ranadhira, the mighty Maharaja of Mysore, was participating in a grand ceremonial procession. Seated atop his massive royal elephant, Naga, he waved to the cheering crowds. 

All of a sudden, Naga broke into a wild run. "Naga has gone berserk! Run for your lives!" the crowd panicked, scattering helter-skelter. The Maharaja clung desperately to the howdah as the furious elephant rampaged through the streets.

Just then, a strange, resonant sound echoed through the chaos. "RRRRR..." A young tribal man from the crowd was making the sound, and remarkably, it seemed to have an instant, soothing effect on the enraged elephant. 

The young man calmly walked up to Naga, patted his trunk affectionately, and helped the shaken Maharaja to get off safely. 

"I owe my life to you, young man," the Maharaja panted, immensely grateful. "Who are you? Where are you from?"

"I am Kacha, Mahaswami," the man replied, bowing respectfully. "I come from the hills."

Intrigued by his skill and simple demeanor, the Maharaja took Kacha to the royal palace. "I have been longing to visit the hill district, but I don't know when I will be able to," the King said warmly. "Tell me, why did you come to Mysore?"

Kacha's face fell, filled with a deep sorrow. "I wanted to get away, Mahaswami. I had always wanted a child. And I got one, a daughter. But as soon as she was born... my wife died."

"Kacha, I understand how you feel," the Maharaja sympathized. "Stay here in Mysore and look after my elephants. However, one day you will want to go back to your people... to your child, to the hills."

Years rolled by. Kacha became an expert handler of the royal elephants, living a quiet life in the palace. One day, the Maharaja summoned him. "Kacha, come have a look at these magnificent tusks. They are from your hills."

Kacha touched them reverently. 

"Your people have sent me their annual tribute through the local Heggade (feudal lord)," the Maharaja continued. "One pair of tusks, eight measures of honey, and one maund of sandalwood. Please collect some honey from the store when you leave, Kacha."

That night, tasting the honey from his homeland, Kacha could not sleep. The sweet, familiar taste brought back a flood of memories. His heart ached for his daughter and his beloved hills. 

The next morning, he went to the Maharaja. "Mahaswami, the hills are calling me..."

The Maharaja smiled knowingly. "I knew this would happen once you tasted the honey. Go back, Kacha. And if you ever need me, send a message. I will come."

Kacha returned to his village in the hills, where he was joyfully reunited with his sister and his now-grown daughter, Mollay. The entire village rejoiced at his return. 

The village officer, the Gowda, soon approached him. "Now that you are back, we want you to be the 'Kaka'—our village chief and elder."

"But Gowda, you already have a Kaka," Kacha pointed out, referring to Mava, the current chief. 

"That Mava? He's no good," Gowda complained. "He is quarrelsome and arrogant. He is not fit to be the Kaka. Please say yes, Kacha!"

Reluctantly, Kacha agreed to take on the responsibility. 

A few months later, the time for the annual tribute arrived. Kacha called a meeting. "We will have to send the Heggade four pairs of tusks, thirty-two measures of honey, and four maunds of sandalwood," he announced.

Gowda was shocked. "But the Heggade sends the Raja only *one* pair of tusks, eight measures of honey, and one maund of sandalwood!"

"Are you sure?" Kacha asked, his eyes narrowing.

"Of course I am! The Maharaja himself mentioned this to me when I was in Mysore."

Realizing the Heggade had been ruthlessly extorting the poor tribals for his own profit, Kacha formulated a plan. "What the Heggade collects, the Maharaja does not know. And what the Maharaja receives, we do not know. We'll tell the Heggade that we will send him only two pairs of tusks, sixteen measures of honey, and two maunds of sandalwood."

A messenger was sent, but he quickly returned with a furious reply. "The Heggade says he expects the usual tribute. Three parts for him and one for the Maharaja!"

A few weeks later, the enraged Heggade arrived with his armed men, camping at the foot of the hills. Gowda and Kacha went down to negotiate. 

"Gowda, I want thirty-two measures of honey, not a drop less!" the Heggade demanded arrogantly. 

"Sir, we are prepared to give what you ask for, but our Kaka must agree," Kacha said smoothly, pretending not to be the Kaka himself. 

"I don't care who he is. I am dealing with you, Gowda," the Heggade sneered. "If you don't send the dues by evening, we'll come to your village and collect them. Send your Kaka here. We'll keep him in our fort for four days and teach him what's good for him!"

Back at the village, Kacha addressed his people. "The Heggade says we must give him what he demands or quit the hills. Today he demands four maunds. Tomorrow he may demand forty. He may demand our timber and compel us to capture and kill our elephants. We are the children of the forest. The forest gives us what we need, and we should not take from it more than we need. This forest was here before us, and we must see to it that it continues to be here after we are gone."

He sent a secret message to the Maharaja of Mysore, invoking his old promise. 

Meanwhile, the Heggade finally realized that the humble man he had spoken to was, in fact, the defiant Kaka. Furious, he planned to arrest Kacha. 

The next day, Kacha, accompanied by a few villagers, met the Heggade again. When the Heggade's men tried to seize him, Kacha cunningly shouted, "Snake! Look out!" 

In the ensuing panic and confusion, the Heggade's son, Chikkaiya, tripped over a thick root and twisted his ankle severely, falling unconscious. Demonstrating his noble nature, Kacha and his men carefully carried the injured boy back to their own village to treat him. 

Under the tender care of Kacha's daughter, Mollay, Chikkaiya slowly recovered. During his convalescence, the young man and Mollay grew deeply fond of each other. 

A few days later, a mysterious stranger arrived in the village. The Heggade, who had come searching for his son, pointed at the newcomer. "There he is! I heard that someone has come from the Raja."

To everyone's astonishment, the stranger removed his disguise. It was Maharaja Ranadhira himself!

"Kaka! The Raja!" the villagers gasped. 

The arrogant Heggade immediately fell to his knees. "Pardon me, Maharaj. I should have recognised you."

The Maharaja looked at the Heggade sternly. "Heggade, do you know that this Kaka once saved my life?" He then recounted the story of how Kacha had tamed the mad elephant. 

Turning to Kacha, the Maharaja smiled warmly. "Kacha, I have kept my promise."

"Mahaswami," Kacha bowed deeply. "All these years I have been telling my people that I am not the true Kaka... The true Kaka is one who is a protector to the helpless, a father who takes care of his children. That is you, my Lord."

"That reminds me of the dispute," the Maharaja said, addressing the assembly. "Listen to my decision. Henceforth, the Kurubas of these hills will pay their tribute directly to me. I gift this territory to Kacha's daughter, Mollay. Her husband will be the new Heggade."

The old Heggade was taken aback. "But she is not yet married."

"She will soon be," the Maharaja smiled, looking at Chikkaiya and Mollay. "She will marry... your son."

Seeing the joy in his son's eyes, the Heggade humbly accepted the royal decree. "We abide by your decision, Maharaj."

"We will also build a strong fort here to mark this occasion," the Heggade offered eagerly. "Kaka's fort!"

But Kacha immediately protested. "No, Mahaswami, not a fort. If a fort is built here, trees will be uprooted, houses will be built, and a noisy town will spring up. Today we can smell an elephant in the distance, hear the footsteps of a tiger, or follow a bee to its hive at will. Nothing should be done to disturb the peace of the forest."

The Maharaja nodded in profound agreement. "As you wish, Kacha. We should refrain from imposing not only taxes but also our way of life on these children of nature."

And so, to honor the wise and elusive Kaka, the pristine, untouched forest region where he refused to let a fort be built came to be known forever as *Kakanakote*—the 'Fort of Kaka', a fort made not of stone, but of nature itself.
