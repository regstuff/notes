# Nandivishala and Other Stories

### Nandivishala

A kind Brahmana once received the gift of a magnificent bull calf, whom he named Nandivishala. The Brahmana brought the calf up with immense loving care, and it grew into a remarkably fine, sturdy bull. 

Wanting to repay his master's kindness, Nandivishala spoke to him one day. "Master, go to a rich merchant and tell him that your bull can draw a hundred loaded carts. Make a bet for a thousand pieces of gold."

The Brahmana could scarcely believe his ears, but recognizing his bull's extraordinary strength, he agreed and made the wager. On the appointed day, a hundred carts loaded with heavy sand, gravel, and stones were lined up. 

However, as the Brahmana yoked Nandivishala to the first cart, his pride got the better of him. Arrogant and overconfident, he cracked his whip and shouted, "Come on, you rascal! Pull! Be quick, you rascal!"

Hearing his beloved master insult him with such a harsh word, Nandivishala was deeply hurt and shocked. Refusing to move an inch, the bull stood his ground. The merchant gleefully claimed his thousand pieces of gold, leaving the Brahmana bankrupt and heartbroken. 

Later at home, the sad Brahmana asked his bull why he had let him down. 
"Why did you call me a rascal?" Nandivishala replied reproachfully. "Have I ever broken a pot, destroyed your property, or gored anyone? I only refused to pull because you insulted me."

Realizing his folly, the Brahmana apologized. Nandivishala forgave him and instructed, "Go and make the same wager for two thousand pieces of gold. But this time, call me a fine fellow."

The Brahmana returned to the merchant and doubled the bet. When the carts were lined up, the Brahmana gently stroked the bull's back and said, "Now then, my fine fellow! Pull the carts along."

With a single, mighty tug, Nandivishala pulled the massive train of a hundred carts forward. The Brahmana won his two thousand pieces of gold and went home a much happier, richer, and far wiser man, having learned the profound value of kind words.

### The Servant and the Treasure

Once, there were two old landowners who were close friends. One of them had a young wife and a newly born son. Fearing he might pass away before his son grew up, the old man consulted his friend.

"It would be wise to bury your wealth in the forest," the friend suggested. "Take your most trusted servant, Nanda, into your confidence and bury the treasure with him."

The old man did exactly that, burying his gold in a secret spot in the woods, known only to Nanda. Soon after, the old man passed away. 

Years later, when the son grew into a young man, the family's funds ran low. His mother told him about the hidden treasure and instructed him to take Nanda to the forest to retrieve it. 

The young man asked Nanda to guide him. However, when they reached a specific spot deep in the forest, Nanda—who had always been docile and obedient—suddenly turned incredibly arrogant and insolent. "What makes you think there is money buried here for you?" he shouted rudely. 

Taken aback by the servant's sudden hostility, the young man returned home empty-handed and confused. Seeking guidance, he visited his father's wise old friend and explained Nanda's bizarre behavior. 

The old friend smiled knowingly. "It's simple! Money can corrupt even an honest man. The moment Nanda stands directly over the spot where the treasure is buried, the proximity of the wealth goes to his head, and he becomes a different, arrogant man. The next time you go, the moment he begins to abuse you, tell him not to move, and start digging right where he stands."

The young man returned to the forest with Nanda. The moment Nanda reached the secret spot, he began shouting insults again. Without a word, the young man shoved Nanda aside and dug into the earth exactly where the servant had been standing. He uncovered his father's treasure chest and carried it home, leaving the foolishly transparent servant completely dumbfounded.

### What's in a Name?

In the ancient city of Takshashila, there lived a great Vedic scholar who had many disciples. One of his students was unfortunately named 'Lowly'. Constantly teased by his peers, the boy became deeply depressed. 

"Master, please give me a new name which sounds more respectable," Lowly begged. 
The wise teacher replied, "All right, my son. Go, travel through the land wherever your fancy takes you. Find a name you like, and I will gladly change yours to match it."

Excited, Lowly wandered from village to village. On the outskirts of a city, he saw a funeral procession carrying a dead body. 
"What was that man called?" Lowly asked a mourner. 
"His name was 'Life'," the man replied sadly. 
Lowly was struck with a sudden realization. *Even a man named 'Life' must die.*

Continuing his journey, he saw a harsh mistress brutally whipping her servant girl for failing to earn her daily wages. 
"Poor girl! She must have an accursed name," Lowly remarked to a bystander. 
"She is called 'Rich'," the passerby chuckled ironically. 
Lowly pondered this deeply. *A girl named 'Rich' lives in abject poverty.*

Further down the road, he found a confused traveler frantically searching for directions in a dense forest. 
"What is your name, friend?" Lowly asked. 
"I am called 'Guide'," the lost man replied helplessly. 
Lowly smiled to himself. *A man named 'Guide' has lost his way.*

His journey complete, Lowly returned to his teacher's ashrama. "Did you find a name you like?" the master asked. 
"Master, I find that death can come to 'Life', that 'Rich' can be poor, and that 'Guide' can be terribly misguided," the young man smiled. "I know now that a name serves only to tell who is who, and does not govern the owner's destiny. I am quite content with my own name and do not want to change it for another."

### The Hypocritical Sadhu

A cunning rascal, masquerading as a holy Sadhu, lived in a small hermitage on the outskirts of a village. Over time, he had completely won the trust and devotion of the local Zamindar (landlord) through his elaborate displays of piety. 

One day, fearing robbers, the Zamindar decided to hide his gold. Believing the hermitage to be the safest place on earth, he brought his treasure to the Sadhu and buried it in the ashrama grounds with the holy man's blessing. 

A few days later, the treacherous Sadhu dug up the gold and buried it in a different, secret spot in the forest. The next morning, he visited the Zamindar. 
"Holy sir, I am leaving my hermitage," the Sadhu announced piously. "It is not right for a man of God to live near buried wealth. It might tempt me. I must go far away."

Deeply moved by the Sadhu's extreme virtue, the Zamindar reverently bade him farewell. As the Sadhu walked away, he suddenly paused, plucked a stray piece of straw from his hair, and walked back to the Zamindar to hand it over. 
"A straw from your roof had stuck in my hair," the Sadhu explained seriously. "We ascetics may not take anything which is not freely bestowed upon us, not even a single straw. I have brought it back to you."

The Zamindar was astounded by this supreme display of honesty. However, a shrewd visiting merchant had watched the entire exchange. 
"Caught you, you rogue!" the merchant thought. He turned to the Zamindar and said, "That man has undoubtedly stolen your gold. Such extreme, theatrical displays of virtue over a mere straw are always a cover for profound deceit."

Alarmed, the Zamindar and the merchant rushed to the hermitage and dug up the spot where the gold was supposed to be. It was empty. They immediately chased after the Sadhu, caught him, and forced him to reveal where he had hidden the stolen treasure. The stolen gold troubled the Sadhu's conscience far less than a blade of straw!

### The Most Virtuous Student

In Varanasi, there lived a renowned scholar who had a large number of young, devoted students. When his daughter came of marriageable age, the scholar was struck by an idea to find the most worthy husband for her among his disciples. 

He gathered all his students and said with a heavy sigh, "I cannot afford the clothes and ornaments required for my daughter's marriage. Will you, my boys, help me out by stealing these things for me? But listen carefully: no one should see you stealing. It must be done in the strictest secrecy. Only then will I accept what you bring."

Eager to please their beloved teacher, the students enthusiastically set out to steal. Over the next few weeks, they brought back stolen gold, silks, and jewels, always sneaking into the ashrama under the cover of darkness. The teacher carefully collected and tagged the items, ensuring he knew exactly who they belonged to so he could return them later. 

However, one student brought absolutely nothing. He sat quietly in his room, looking dejected. 
The teacher approached him. "My son, I have no need of wealth. I had only asked you to steal as a test to find a truly virtuous man for my daughter. You alone are worthy of her. But tell me, why did you not bring anything?"

"Master," the young man replied softly, "you commanded us to steal only when there was absolutely no other person in sight, so it could be kept a secret. But because, even when there is no other person around, I cannot hide from myself! My own conscience would be watching. I find that there is no such thing as secrecy in wrongdoing."

Overjoyed by the young man's profound integrity, the teacher embraced him. After returning all the stolen goods to their rightful owners, the scholar proudly gave his daughter in marriage to his most virtuous student.
