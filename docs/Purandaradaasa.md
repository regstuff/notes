# Purandara Dasa: The Father of Carnatic Music

During the fifteenth century, in the bustling town of Purandargad near Poona, lived a remarkably wealthy and generous jeweler named Varadappa Nayak. Known throughout the region for his immense charity, no one who came to him for help ever went away disappointed. However, despite his vast wealth, Varadappa and his wife were deeply sorrowful because they had no children. 

Hoping for a miracle, the pious couple traveled to the great temple of Lord Venkateshwara at Tirupati to pray for a child. Their prayers were answered. A year later, they were blessed with a beautiful baby boy. Believing him to be a direct gift from the Lord, they gratefully named him Srinivasa. 

As little Srinivasa grew, he displayed a peculiar fascination. Unlike other children who played with toys, he loved playing with fake, copper coins, meticulously counting and hoarding them. "He will make a incredibly shrewd businessman," his father smiled proudly, unaware of how true and tragic those words would become. 

When Srinivasa came of age, he began apprenticing at his father's jewelry shop. One day, a customer offered to sell a gold chain for five hundred *panas* (coins). Realizing the chain was actually worth much more, Srinivasa flatly refused to buy it, hoping to aggressively bargain the desperate man down to an even lower price. 

When the insulted customer walked away, old Varadappa gently reprimanded his son. "You should have bought it," he advised. "In business, you must know when a deal is fair."
"But I am in business to make maximum profit, father," Srinivasa argued coldly. "Why pay more when I can force him to take less?"

Soon after Srinivasa was married to a deeply pious and gentle woman named Saraswati, old Varadappa fell terminally ill. The royal physician examined him and prescribed a rare medicine made from the ashes of precious gemstones. 

"Precious stones cost a fortune!" Srinivasa gasped, his greed instantly overriding his filial love. "Can't you find some other, cheaper medicine? Why waste perfectly good wealth on an old man who has to die one day anyway?"

Denied the life-saving medicine by his own miserly son, the great, generous Varadappa died the very next day. 

With his father gone, Srinivasa took complete control of the business and the household. His heart rapidly hardened into stone. He became infamous as the most ruthless, merciless miser in the entire kingdom. He ruthlessly exploited the poor, hoarded his vast wealth, and furiously forbade his gentle wife from giving even a handful of rice as alms to starving beggars. 

"O Goddess Tulasi," Saraswati would weep silently before her household deity. "Please make my husband less greedy. Enlighten his darkened soul."

Years passed, and Srinivasa became a millionaire many times over. One day, a desperately poor, elderly Brahman arrived at Srinivasa's shop. "Blessed one, I need money to perform my son's sacred thread ceremony," the old man begged. "Will you help me?"

"I am busy. Come tomorrow," Srinivasa dismissed him curtly. 

For six agonizing months, Srinivasa maliciously made the frail old Brahman walk miles to his shop every single day, giving him nothing but empty promises. Finally, utterly annoyed by the old man's persistence, Srinivasa tossed a single, heavily worn copper coin at him. It was one of the fake, worthless coins he used to play with as a child! 

Deeply humiliated and heartbroken, the weeping Brahman stumbled away. By sheer chance, he found himself outside Srinivasa's own mansion, where he met the kind-hearted Saraswati. 

When the old man poured out his tale of woe and showed her the insulting fake coin, Saraswati was paralyzed with deep shame for her husband's cruelty. Desperate to help, but knowing her husband tightly controlled all the household money, she secretly removed her own precious diamond nose-ring—a cherished gift from her own mother—and handed it to the Brahman. "Take this and sell it," she urged kindly. 

The grateful Brahman went straight to the biggest jewelry shop in town to sell it—which, unfortunately, belonged to Srinivasa Nayak. 

The moment Srinivasa inspected the exquisite diamonds, his sharp eyes immediately recognized his wife's unique nose-ring. His blood boiled with suspicious fury. "I'll need time to value this," he told the Brahman smoothly. "Leave it here and come back tomorrow."

Locking the nose-ring safely in his heavy iron strongbox, Srinivasa stormed back to his mansion. 
"Saraswati!" he bellowed. "Where is your diamond nose-ring? Fetch it and put it on immediately!"

Terrified of her husband's violent wrath, Saraswati panicked. She rushed into the locked *puja* (prayer) room. "I'd rather die than face his anger," she sobbed. Desperately, she grabbed a heavy grinding stone, violently smashed her diamond bangles into powder, mixed the lethal diamond dust with water, and raised the cup to her lips to commit suicide. 

But as she closed her eyes to swallow the poison, she heard a soft *clink* in the metal dish used for offerings. She looked down and gasped in absolute shock. 
Lying perfectly intact amidst the flower petals was her diamond nose-ring! 

Weeping with overwhelming gratitude for this divine miracle, she rushed out and handed the nose-ring to her stunned husband. 

Srinivasa snatched it, his mind reeling. He sprinted frantically back to his shop, unlocked his heavy iron strongbox, and threw open the lid. 
It was completely empty. 

In that profound, shattering moment, the dark veil of absolute greed that had blinded Srinivasa for decades instantly evaporated. "My greed almost killed my innocent wife!" he realized, falling to his knees in horror and deep remorse. "That was no ordinary old Brahman. It must have been the Lord Himself, coming to awaken me!"

A miraculous, total transformation came over him. The ruthless millionaire immediately unlocked his vaults and freely distributed every single coin, jewel, and property he owned to the poor and deserving. Having stripped himself of all his worldly burdens, he donned the simple, ragged robes of a wandering mendicant. Accompanied by his overjoyed wife and children, he set out on a lifelong spiritual pilgrimage. 

Wandering from place to place, singing ecstatic songs of devotion, Srinivasa finally arrived in the great capital of Vijayanagara. There, he met the renowned royal saint, Vyasateertha. 
Recognizing the divine spark within the former miser, the saint warmly initiated him. "You have started a completely new life," Vyasateertha smiled. "Henceforth, you will be known to the world as **Purandara Dasa**."

Purandara Dasa revolutionized devotion. He wandered the bustling streets of Vijayanagara, composing and singing incredibly melodious, simple, and profoundly moving songs in the common Kannada language, making the complex spiritual truths of the Vedas easily accessible to ordinary people. His musical genius was so immense that he is revered today as the legendary "Father of Carnatic Music."

His spiritual journey was marked by profound, humbling miracles. Once, while resting at the great temple in Pandharpur, Purandara Dasa asked his young disciple, Appanna, to fetch him some warm water to wash his tired feet. 

Annoyed when the boy took too long, the usually calm Purandara Dasa lost his temper. When Appanna finally arrived, the Dasa angrily snatched the heavy pitcher and violently poured the scalding hot water directly over the boy's feet. Appanna screamed in agony, and Purandara Dasa instantly felt a crushing wave of remorse for his sudden burst of anger. 

The next morning, a deeply regretful Purandara Dasa sought out the boy to apologize. 
"But sir, I don't understand," a confused Appanna replied. "I fell asleep early last night. I never brought you any water!"

Perplexed and deeply shaken, Purandara Dasa rushed into the main temple. To his absolute horror, he saw that the stone feet of the magnificent idol of Lord Vithala were severely swollen and blistered! 
"O Lord!" Purandara Dasa wept, collapsing at the altar. "You came to serve me Yourself, and I rewarded You with boiling water? When will I ever truly conquer my terrible anger?"

But his greatest test of humility was yet to come. That same morning, the temple priests discovered that the priceless golden bracelets adorning Lord Vithala's idol had been stolen during the night. 

A famous local court dancer named Vanajakshi suddenly arrived at the temple, proudly wearing the stolen divine bracelets. When fiercely questioned by the guards, she innocently pointed at Purandara Dasa. "He gave them to me!" she claimed. "Last night, Purandara Dasa came to my house to watch me dance. He was so pleased with my performance that he gifted me these bracelets."

"But I never left the temple courtyard all night!" Purandara Dasa protested. 
Refusing to believe a wandering beggar, the furious temple guards brutally tied the great saint to a stone pillar and began whipping him mercilessly for theft and hypocrisy. Purandara Dasa bore the agonizing lashes silently, accepting it as divine punishment for scalding the Lord's feet the night before. 

Suddenly, a booming, divine voice echoed through the temple courtyard. 
"Stop! Stop whipping him!"

The guards froze. Everyone turned to look at the altar. Miraculously, the priceless golden bracelets were glowing brightly, safely back on Lord Vithala's stone wrists! 

"I took his form last night to test the dancer," the Lord's voice echoed. "Purandara Dasa is utterly innocent." 
The terrified guards and the weeping dancer immediately fell at the bleeding saint's feet, begging for his forgiveness. 

Purandara Dasa's fame as a pure, enlightened soul spread across the empire. When he humbly presented his massive collection of newly composed Kannada devotional songs to his Guru, Saint Vyasateertha, the arrogant orthodox Sanskrit scholars of the court vehemently objected. 

"Songs written in the common, vulgar Kannada tongue cannot be given the same status as the sacred works written in Sanskrit, the language of the gods!" the haughty scholars protested angrily. 

"I will show you the true greatness of Purandara Dasa," Saint Vyasateertha replied calmly. He placed Purandara Dasa's humble manuscript on the floor, directly beneath the sacred *Vyasa Peeth* (the elevated, revered platform reserved strictly for the holiest scriptures). 

Before the shocked eyes of the arrogant scholars, a brilliant, divine light erupted from the manuscript. The book miraculously levitated from the floor and gently placed itself at the very top of the *Vyasa Peeth*, hovering above the ancient Sanskrit texts!

Utterly humbled by this divine validation, the scholars bowed their heads in deep reverence. From that spectacular day forward, the incredibly beautiful, soul-stirring songs of Purandara Dasa were officially recognized as the *Purandara Upanishad*. 

Having transformed from the most ruthless miser to the greatest saint and musical genius of his era, Purandara Dasa spent his final, peaceful days in a quiet retreat built specially for him by the King on the banks of the Tungabhadra river, leaving behind a massive, immortal legacy of divine music that still echoes through the heart of India today.
