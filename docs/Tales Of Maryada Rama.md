# Tales Of Maryada Rama - 2. The False Witnesses Trapped

Once, two men came to Maryada Rama to resolve a dispute.

Rama first heard the plaintiff. "Sir," the man explained, "I had a valuable ruby which I left with this man for safe-keeping when I went on a pilgrimage four years ago."

He pointed an accusing finger at the defendant. "Now he refuses to give it back."

"But I've returned it to him, sir, in the presence of three witnesses!" the defendant cried out defensively.

"That's a lie, sir," the plaintiff insisted.

Maryada Rama raised a hand to silence them. "Summon the witnesses," he ordered.

The defendant, however, had bribed his own washerman, barber, and cook to come and support his story. 

When the three men arrived at court, Rama asked them, "Did you see the precious stone being returned?"

"Yes, sir," they all answered in unison, bowing respectfully.

Rama turned to his attendant. "Give all five of them some clay," he instructed, "and ask each of them to make a model of the precious stone in separate rooms. When the models are ready, send them to me."

The plaintiff and the defendant had no problem. They knew that the precious stone was a ruby and got down to work on their models, quickly shaping the clay into a small, faceted gem.

But the false witnesses, who had never actually seen the ruby, did not know what to do. So, each of them made a model of what was to him a "precious stone"—the stone used in his trade!

*The stone on which I wash my clothes is the most precious stone,* the washerman thought, shaping his clay into a large, flat slab.

*That's it! It must have been a whetstone,* the barber thought. *What stone could be more precious!* He modeled his clay into a long sharpening block.

*The precious stone in question cannot be anything else but a quern,* the cook thought, making a model of a large stone used for grinding spices.

When the five wildly different models were placed before Rama on a tray, he simply smiled.

"These models prove that the witnesses are lying," Rama declared. "They have never seen the ruby."

He looked sternly at the terrified defendant. "These witnesses have been hired by you. You and your witnesses shall be punished for perjury, after the ruby is returned to the plaintiff, its rightful owner."


# Tales Of Maryada Rama - 3. The Fowl Thief

One day, a woman's plump hen strayed into her greedy neighbour's house. The neighbour saw the bird wandering in her yard and immediately grabbed it. *Ah! I shall have roast chicken for lunch,* she thought mischievously.

At dusk, the rightful owner of the fowl became anxious. *My hen has not returned yet. I'd better go and fetch her,* she thought, heading next door. 

"Where is my hen? I saw her straying in here a few hours back," the owner asked her neighbour.

"I don't know what you are talking about," the neighbour replied innocently, wiping her mouth.

"What else but my hen? Return it or I'll go and lodge a complaint against you," the owner threatened angrily.

The greedy neighbour scoffed. "I've never set eyes on your hen. I'm not afraid of your threats."

The owner went straight to the court of Maryada Rama and complained. 

"Do you have any evidence?" Rama asked.

"No sir, but I saw the hen enter her house," the woman replied.

Rama sent for the neighbour and brought her to the court. "You are under oath. Now tell me the truth," he commanded.

"Believe me, sir. I don't know what she's talking about. You can search my house," the neighbour said, entirely confident that the cooked chicken was long gone.

Rama was inclined to believe the plaintiff's story, but there was no concrete proof of the suspect's guilt. *I'm sure she's cooked the fowl and eaten it,* Rama thought. *I'll get the truth out of her yet.*

He pretended to weigh the matter carefully, closing his eyes in deep thought. Then, he announced, "You may go now. I'll give you the verdict tomorrow."

Relieved, the greedy neighbour smirked at the owner. But as the two women turned to leave, Rama suddenly spoke up loudly to his attendant. 

"Have you ever seen such an impudent woman?" Rama declared in disgust. "She steals her neighbour's fowl, eats it, and then has the audacity to come to court and swear innocence with a feather of the fowl sticking in her hair."

The moment the guilty woman heard Rama's words, she instinctively reached up and passed her hand over her hair to quickly brush away the nonexistent feather. *How could I have been so careless!* she thought in panic.

The next moment, Rama pointed a finger squarely at her. "Seize her! She is guilty."

The guards instantly apprehended the woman, who now stood shame-facedly before the clever judge, realizing she had fallen for his trap.

"You will replace the stolen bird," Rama pronounced sternly, "and pay a heavy fine to the court for uttering a falsehood under oath."


# Tales Of Maryada Rama - 4. The Dishonest Debtor

A farmer once signed a bond and borrowed a hundred gold coins from a wealthy money-lender, promising to return the amount within three months.

At the end of the three months, the money-lender visited the farmer and asked him to repay the sum. 

"Come to my farm tomorrow with the bond, and I'll return the amount along with the interest," the farmer said respectfully.

The money-lender went the next day with the bond in hand.

"There seems to be some mistake about the date," the farmer frowned, holding his hand out. "Let me see the bond."

The unsuspecting money-lender gave the bond to him. The farmer immediately walked over to a boiling pot over an open fire. Before the money-lender could react, the farmer tore the bond into pieces and tossed them into the flames!

"What are you doing?" the money-lender cried out in shock.

"I'm making sure that you'll never trouble me for the money again," the farmer sneered.

Heartbroken and enraged, the poor money-lender complained to the city judge, Maryada Rama. Rama immediately sent for the farmer.

"Why did you tear up the bond?" Rama asked the farmer sternly.

"What bond, sir? I know nothing of any bond. This man is a liar!" the farmer acted completely outraged by the accusation.

Rama did not say anything. He calmly looked at both of them and declared, "You may go now. I'll see you tomorrow."

As they walked out, the farmer grinned to himself. *But the money-lender will never see his gold,* he thought smugly.

Later that evening, Rama secretly sent for the money-lender. "What was the size of the bond?" Rama asked him.

"It was about a span long, sir," the money-lender replied honestly.

"All right. Now do as I say," Rama instructed. "When I question you in court tomorrow, insist that it was a cubit long."

"As you say, sir," the money-lender agreed.

The next day at court, the plaintiff was placed under oath to speak nothing but the truth.

"How long was the destroyed bond?" Rama asked the money-lender.

"A cubit long, sir," the money-lender stated loudly.

Rama then turned to the farmer. "Now tell me, what was the size of the bond you claim was destroyed?"

The farmer, eager to prove the money-lender a liar, immediately blurted out, "That's a blatant lie! It was only a span long."

A hushed silence fell over the court. The farmer's eyes widened in horror as he realized his fatal mistake. The farmer knew he was trapped and finally confessed to destroying the bond.

"How do you know it was a span long if you've never seen the bond?" Rama asked dryly. 

He then delivered his verdict. "You shall first return the amount with interest, and then go to prison."


# Tales Of Maryada Rama - 5. The Dead Elephant and the Broken Pots

Once two men came to Maryada Rama's court, arguing loudly.

"What is the matter?" Rama asked.

The plaintiff stepped forward, pointing angrily at the other man. "Sir, this man borrowed my elephant for his son's wedding procession. During the event, the animal died. I want my elephant back alive."

The defendant looked absolutely exasperated. "What can I do, sir? The animal suddenly died of natural causes. I offered to compensate him handsomely with money, but he stubbornly insists that I restore his animal to life and return it to him!"

Rama tried to patiently persuade the plaintiff to accept the money, explaining the impossibility of the demand, but it was all in vain. 

*The man is a stubborn, unreasonable fool. I'll have to teach him a lesson,* Rama thought to himself. 

"Let us postpone the case for a day," Rama announced smoothly. "We'll take up the matter again tomorrow."

An hour later, Rama secretly sent for the defendant. 

"Pile up all the pottery you have in your house directly against your front door tomorrow morning," Rama instructed the puzzled man. "And don't come to court till the plaintiff arrives personally at your house to call you."

The defendant smiled as he immediately caught on to the clever judge's plan. 

The next day, the plaintiff came to court at the appointed hour, eager for a verdict. But the defendant was nowhere to be seen.

"We cannot take up your case till the defendant too is here," Rama said.

The plaintiff was furious. "That scoundrel! He's deliberately stayed away. I'll go and bring him myself."

He rushed angrily to the defendant's house and violently pushed the front door open. 

To his complete surprise, there was a massive, deafening *CRASH!* Dozens of clay pots that had been stacked against the door shattered into a thousand pieces all over the floor.

The defendant burst out of the house, clutching his head in mock horror. "What have you done?" he screamed at the shocked plaintiff. "My precious heirlooms, all destroyed! They have been in our family for generations!"

"Please! Please forgive me! I'll pay for the damage," the plaintiff pleaded, looking at the broken mess.

"I don't want money. I want my pots back!" the defendant yelled. "I shall take the matter to court."

He went straight to the court and loudly lodged his complaint before Maryada Rama. "Sir, this man must give me the very same pots back. No amount of money can compensate me for my loss. They were my family heirlooms."

Rama suppressed a smile and turned to the plaintiff, who was now forced to be a defendant. 

"He will bring your elephant back to life the day you return, completely intact, the very pots you have broken," Rama declared wisely. "You may go now."


# Tales Of Maryada Rama - 6. The Brahman's Gold

An old Brahman and his wife once decided to go on a long, holy pilgrimage. Before leaving, they placed all their life savings—a handful of valuable gold coins—in a brass pot and filled it to the brim with *dal* (split pulses) to hide the treasure.

"I'll leave it with the neighbours for safe-keeping," the Brahman told his wife.

At the neighbour's house, the neighbour smiled reassuringly. "You may leave it right in that corner. No one will touch it. Trust me."

One night, a few days later, the neighbours had eight unexpected guests arrive for dinner.

"What shall I do?" the neighbour's panicked wife whispered in the kitchen. "I don't have anything in the house to feed them, and it's too late to go to the bazaar."

Her husband thought for a moment. "We could borrow some of the dal from the Brahman's pot and replace it tomorrow," he suggested.

But as he poured out the dal from the pot into a bowl, something clinked.

"What's this! Gold coins!" the neighbour gasped, his eyes wide with greed.

His wife quickly hushed him. "Let's keep the coins and fill the vessel back up with our own dal," she whispered wickedly.

Greed got the better of the man, and he did exactly as his wife suggested.

Months later, as soon as the Brahman and his wife returned from their pilgrimage, they went next door to retrieve their pot.

"There you are!" the neighbour smiled warmly. "We haven't even moved it from that corner."

The Brahman thanked him, took the pot, went home, and happily tipped out the contents. 

"Where is the gold?" his wife screamed, sifting frantically through the lentils. "They've stolen the gold!"

"Alas! What will we do? We are too old to work," the Brahman wept.

They ran back to the neighbour in tears. "Please, please give us our gold," the old man begged. "We've almost starved ourselves to save it. Give it back to us."

The neighbour scoffed indignantly. "What gold are you talking about? Stop making a scene at my door. What will the neighbours think?"

Heartbroken, the Brahman went to the court of Maryada Rama with his tale.

"Were there any witnesses who could swear on your behalf?" Rama asked gently.

"No, sir. I trusted my neighbour," the old man cried.

Rama thought for a while. This was a tricky case with absolutely no proof. Suddenly, his eyes lit up. *I have it!* he thought. 

"Go home, my good man. You'll soon get back your gold," Rama promised.

Rama immediately sent for a local basket-maker. "I want a hollow idol made of cane," the judge instructed. "It should be large enough to conceal a small child and light enough to be carried by two people. Cover it completely with lac to make it look exactly as if it's made of solid wood."

When the idol was brought to court, Rama sent for a bright, trusted child. "Now sit inside here without making a single sound, and listen carefully to whatever you hear," Rama whispered, sealing the boy inside.

When the child was comfortably seated inside the idol, Rama sent for the greedy neighbour and his wife.

"Carry this idol round the temple, swear before Goddess Kali that you have not taken the Brahman's gold, and then come back here," Rama commanded the couple.

Trembling with fear at the thought of lying to the fierce goddess, the couple lifted the surprisingly light idol, carried it to the temple, and began walking round it. 

While they were at the back of the temple and out of earshot from the crowds, as he believed, the husband turned angrily upon his wife in a hushed whisper. 

"See what you've done!" he hissed. "We'll have to swear to a falsehood before the goddess. She will never forgive us for it."

Inside the idol, the child grinned. *The judge will be glad to hear this,* he thought.

As soon as the idol was brought back to court and set down, the child popped out of a hidden door!

"Sir, this man said to his wife..." the child began, and loudly repeated exactly what he had heard behind the temple.

Knowing that he was completely trapped, the neighbour fell on his knees and confessed his crime.

Rama looked at the guards. "See that they return the gold, and send them to prison."


# Tales Of Maryada Rama - 7. The Cat's Paw

Once, a rather strange case came up before Maryada Rama. The plaintiffs were three wealthy cotton merchants, and the defendant was their fourth business partner. 

"Sir, the four of us deal in cotton bales," the first plaintiff explained to the judge. "Disgusted with the rat menace in our warehouse, we recently bought a cat."

"We became so terribly fond of the cat that each of us took charge of one of its legs and showered attention on it," the second plaintiff continued. 

They had pampered the animal endlessly. "Here is a fine anklet for my leg," one partner would say, slipping a jewel onto the cat's paw. 

"It's not as fine as mine," another would boast.

"One day," the first plaintiff went on, "the cat hurt one of its legs—the specific leg belonging to the defendant."

The defendant nodded. "I bound the wound with an oil-soaked rag, comforting the poor creature, thinking it would soon be all right."

"But that night," the plaintiff cried, "as the cat limped about after the rats in the warehouse, it accidentally stepped on a burning oil lamp, and the oil-soaked rag caught fire!"

Mad with pain, the flaming cat had dashed hither and thither, desperately trying to escape the burning rag. In its panic, it set all the highly flammable cotton bales on fire. 

"When we woke up, it was too late. All the goods were utterly destroyed," the merchants wailed. 

The first plaintiff pointed an accusing finger at the defendant. "Since it was *his* leg that caused the fire, he will have to pay us all for the damages!"

Rama looked at the miserable defendant. *The poor fellow cannot be blamed. It was a tragic accident,* the judge thought. *His greedy partners must be taught a lesson.*

Rama turned to the three plaintiffs with a perfectly serious face. 

"It is you three, in fact, who will have to make good to the defendant the value of one fourth of the goods destroyed," Rama declared.

"What!?" the plaintiffs gasped in outrage. "But why?"

Rama smiled wisely. "For... it was the three *sound* legs that belonged to you that helped the cat jump about the warehouse and set fire to the bales. The injured leg was merely dragged along!"
