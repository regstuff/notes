# The March to Freedom - 2 [A Nation Awakes] - 1. A Nation Awakes

At the beginning of the twentieth century, Bengal presented a beautiful picture of unity in diversity. Rich and poor, Hindus and Muslims lived together harmoniously, sharing in agricultural work and participating in each other's religious festivals. 

Furthermore, the Bengali people were highly educated and politically conscious, increasingly demanding a greater role in their own governance from their British rulers. 

This growing political awareness caused immense dismay among British officials. Lord Curzon, the British Viceroy, devised a cunning, malicious plan to break their unity and weaken their political power. 

He decided to partition Bengal. By separating East Bengal and joining it with Assam, he aimed to create a new province where Muslims would be the majority, while in the old province, Bengalis would be outnumbered by Biharis and Oriyas. It was a classic "divide and rule" strategy. 

When the partition was announced in 1905, the people of Bengal were furious. They realized the British ignored their petitions and appeals, so they adopted a powerful new weapon: Boycott and Swadeshi. 

"Firangees are a nation of merchants. They understand one language: Boycott," the leaders declared. 

They urged the people to boycott all British-made goods (especially Manchester cloth) and instead use only *Swadeshi* (indigenously made) goods. 

On October 16, 1905, the day the partition came into effect, the people observed a "Day of Mourning" and unity. Hindus and Muslims marched barefoot to the Ganga, bathed, and tied *rakhis* on each other's wrists as a symbol of their unbreakable brotherhood. The air echoed with the powerful cry of "Bande Mataram!" (Hail to the Motherland!).

The Swadeshi movement spread like wildfire. People refused to buy foreign cloth, causing British imports to plummet. Village weavers dusted off their old looms and the indigenous handloom industry was revived. 

The boycott extended to all foreign goods. Women discarded imported bangles, priests refused to offer foreign sugar to the deities, men gave up foreign cigarettes and liquor. Students played a massive role, picketing shops selling foreign goods and organizing bonfires to burn imported clothing. 

The British government, led by the ruthless Lt. Governor Fuller, panicked and cracked down violently on the students. They banned the slogan "Bande Mataram," expelled students from government schools, and even publicly flogged young boys. 

In response to the expulsions, patriotic leaders established "National Schools" across Bengal, completely boycotting the British educational system. 

When Lord Minto succeeded Lord Curzon and bluntly refused to annul the partition, the agitation intensified. The goal shifted from simply reuniting Bengal to achieving complete freedom from British rule. 

The government tried to exploit the poverty of the peasants to incite communal riots between poor Muslim peasants and rich Hindu landlords. However, patriotic leaders quickly stepped in, spreading pamphlets to remind the peasants that their true enemy was the British, not each other. 

Faced with brutal police repression, some impatient young revolutionaries turned to violence. The first bomb was thrown by Khudiram Bose in 1908. The British responded with mass arrests, rounding up leaders like Aurobindo Ghosh and deporting the great Bal Gangadhar Tilak to Mandalay for six years. 

With its major leaders jailed or deported, the Swadeshi movement gradually lost its initial vigour. When Aurobindo stepped out of prison in 1909, he was greeted by silence instead of the roaring cries of "Bande Mataram."

However, the movement was not a failure. It successfully forced the British government to revoke the partition of Bengal a few years later. 

More importantly, the Swadeshi movement had awakened the nation. It had taught the Indian people the immense power of unity, boycott, and self-reliance— tools that Mahatma Gandhi would later use to finally win India's independence.
