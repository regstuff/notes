# The Dullard

There were once four Brahmans who had grown up together in the same village. Three of them spent all their time poring over scholarly texts, but not so the fourth. One day the three suggested travelling to the city to make use of their learning and collect a fortune. "We're not taking that dullard with us. He doesn't know a thing!" one said, until another reminded them he'd grown up with them and shouldn't be left behind. "Oh, all right! Let him come," they relented, and the four set out.

Passing through a forest, they came upon the bones of a lion. "Why don't we test our learning on these bones?" one scholar said. "I can put the bones together and make a skeleton." Another added muscle and skin with his learning. A third declared, "Now it's my turn. Watch this! I can give the skeleton life." As he prepared to bring the lion back to life, the dullard begged him to stop. "No! No! I'm not! It's a lion! If it comes alive it will kill us all! Please don't..." "Be quiet, you ungrateful fellow," the scholar snapped. "It was I who coaxed the others to let you come along. And now you want to deny me the chance to test and prove my scholarship!" "Ignore him. What does he understand of these things?" the others agreed. "All right. Do as you wish. But let it not be said that I didn't warn you," the dullard said, and scrambled up a tree.

The scholar breathed life into the skeleton. The lion sprang up and killed all three learned men. The dullard, watching from the tree, thought sadly: "My poor learned friends! If only you had heeded what my common sense told me." **Moral: Mere scholarship without common sense is futile.**

**The Greedy Barber.** Manibhadra the merchant was virtuous, rich, and generous, and men flocked to his home to enjoy his hospitality — until a bad investment lost him all his wealth, and with it, his friends. Alone and despairing, he decided to starve himself to death. That night he had an amazing dream: a monk appeared and told him, "A good man like you must live. I will come to your door tomorrow in this form. Touch me on my head with a stick and I'll turn into gold — more gold than you could use in this lifetime."

The next morning, a barber knocked at his door for his usual work. As he got down to it, another knock came — and this time it truly was a monk, exactly as seen in the dream. Manibhadra struck him with a stick, and the monk turned into a heap of gold on the spot. Delighted, he gave some to the barber as thanks, warning him to keep it secret. The barber, seeing what he'd just witnessed, drew his own conclusion: monks turn to gold when struck. The next day, he brought a whole group of monks to his own house on the pretext of buying canvas and alms — then set upon them with his stick, screaming for them to turn to gold. The monks fled for their lives, crying murder, and the city guards dragged the barber before the judge. When Manibhadra was summoned and explained the whole story of his dream, the judge was blunt: "Not just a fool but a greedy fool! You shall pay for your crime by going to prison!" **Moral: Blind imitation is a dangerous thing.**

**The Mongoose and the Brahman's Wife.** A kind Brahman found an orphaned baby mongoose whining near its dead mother and brought it home. "You'll have to bring up this little baby too," his wife said, and though wary that the creature was, after all, a wild animal, she raised it alongside her own newborn son, and the two grew up playing together like brothers.

One day, leaving to fetch water, the wife told her husband to watch that the mongoose didn't hurt their son — "how can he hurt his own brother? You worry unnecessarily," he said, and went off to beg for alms, leaving the child alone. While he was gone, a snake slithered toward the sleeping baby, and the mongoose fought it off fiercely, killing it — but in the process got blood on its own snout. Proud of protecting its "brother," it ran out to greet the returning mother — who, seeing the blood, panicked, screamed "You brute! You've killed my son!" and struck the mongoose dead with her water pot before ever checking on the child. Rushing inside, she found her baby alive and well, with the dead snake lying beside the hammock — and realized, too late, what she had done. **Moral: Act in haste, repent at leisure.**

**The Wheel-Bearer.** Four friends once travelled to the remote hermitage of the ascetic Yogi Bhairavananda, seeking gold or hoping to die trying. He gave each of them a lit wick and told them to walk toward the mountains — wherever a wick fell from a friend's hand, they should dig, and find treasure. One by one, as their wicks fell, the friends found copper, then silver, then gold, and each in turn was satisfied and turned back — except the last, most ambitious of them, who pressed on alone in search of gems, his wick still burning.

He grew thirsty and lost in the mountains, and at last came upon a strange, tortured man with a spinning wheel bound to his own head, bleeding and begging for it to be taken away. When the wheel-bearer asked for water instead, the tortured man attacked him — and the wheel leapt from his head onto the newcomer's own, its torment passed on. The former sufferer explained: this was the curse of a seeker who'd wanted more than copper, silver, or gold, and now, freed at last, he thanked his rescuer and departed. Meanwhile, the fourth friend, worried he hadn't returned, backtracked and found him suffering under the very same wheel. "What you call a quirk of fate was your own greed," he told him sadly, powerless to help — and left him to his punishment. **Moral: Be ambitious, but not avaricious.**

**Ekabuddhi.** Two fish, Shatabuddhi and Sahasrabuddhi, and a frog named Ekabuddhi, lived together in a shallow pond with Ekabuddhi's wife. One evening, two fishermen spotted the pond and planned to return the next morning to net it. Ekabuddhi urged the fish to flee at once, but Sahasrabuddhi scoffed: "Besides, I know another hundred tricks if your one thousand fail!" Shatabuddhi agreed they shouldn't abandon their home over mere words. Ekabuddhi and his wife fled that night to a safer pond. The next morning, the fishermen returned and netted the entire pond — including both overconfident fish, who were carried off to be sold. **Moral: Prevention is better than cure.**

**The Lazy Brahman.** A lazy Brahman who lived on alms was once given more flour than he needed by a kind housewife. Rather than use it, he decided to save it — hanging it safely from the rafters, away from rats — and lay back on his charpoy to dream of the fortune it would bring him one day, should famine ever strike and flour become scarce.

Lying there, he imagined it all: selling the flour for money, buying goats, then a bull and cow, then buffaloes, then horses, until he pictured himself the wealthy owner of a grand mansion, married to a beautiful bride, with a baby son he'd name Soma Sharma — a son he'd have to protect from the horses in his own stable as the boy learned to crawl. In his daydream, he pictured his wife too busy to notice the child crawling near the horses, and worked himself into such a fury imagining the danger that he leapt up and kicked out wildly at the horses in his fantasy — and in doing so, kicked the real pot of flour hanging above him, which came crashing down and spilled uselessly onto the floor. **Moral: Don't count your chickens before they are hatched.**
