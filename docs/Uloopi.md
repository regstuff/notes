# Uloopi

Arjuna, the valiant Pandava prince, once accidentally broke a sacred vow. For this offence, he was sentenced to go on an exile for twelve long years. Accompanied by learned Brahmans, he set out from Indraprastha, the capital city. 

His first halt was at Gangotri, the holy source of the river Ganga. "I'll visit the holy places of the land," Arjuna told his companions. Wherever he went, Arjuna performed the fire sacrifice, looking upon it as his sacred duty. 

One day, as usual, Arjuna waded into the freezing waters of the Ganga to bathe before beginning the sacrificial rites. As he was about to step out, he suddenly froze. "I feel helpless. I can't move a step forward!" he realized in panic. "Am I under a spell?"

"Help!" he cried out. The Brahmans rushed forward to his rescue, but by the time they reached the spot, Arjuna had vanished from sight beneath the swirling waters.

Arjuna was dragged deep down into the river by a powerful, invisible force until he arrived at a beautiful, glowing underwater palace. 

"I wonder where I am, and to whom this palace belongs," Arjuna thought, bewildered. Looking around, he noticed a ritual fire burning brightly. "Ah! A sacrificial fire! Perhaps it is meant for me." 

Unseen by Arjuna, a beautiful lady was watching him intently. He calmly sat before the sacrificial fire and performed his rites with deep devotion. After finishing, he lifted his eyes and saw the lady. 

"Who are you?" he asked, startled. 

"I am Uloopi, the daughter of the Snake-King, Kauravya," she answered softly. "You are in the underwater city of the snake-people, the Nagas."

"Why have you brought me here?" Arjuna asked. 

"Because... I love you," Uloopi confessed, stepping closer. "Please make me your wife."

Arjuna sighed. "How can that be possible? I am an exiled prince. My fire sacrifice at Gangotri is incomplete. I will take you back tomorrow, but please be with me today."

"It is the duty of a prince to relieve the distressed," Uloopi pleaded tearfully. "If you do not agree to my proposal, I shall surely die."

Arjuna was deeply touched by Uloopi's sincere pleadings. "I'll do what you wish, dear one!" he agreed warmly. 

Throughout the hours of his stay, Uloopi looked after his every need with absolute tenderness and affection. The next morning, she guided him back to the surface. 

"You have saved my life. I am grateful to you, my beloved husband," Uloopi said sadly as they neared the shore. "I shall take you back, as I promised." She thought to herself with a heavy heart, *He will soon forget me.*

As Arjuna emerged from the water and turned around to thank her, he found he was alone. "Where is she? Where has she gone?" he called out. But unable to bear the grief of parting, Uloopi had disappeared back into the depths. 

Arjuna's Brahman companions rushed forward, overjoyed to see him alive. After briefly explaining what had befallen him, Arjuna completed his fire sacrifice. He then made a decision. "You may continue staying here if you wish. I intend visiting the holy places in the East." Leaving the Brahmans behind, Arjuna journeyed eastwards alone.

After a long journey, he reached the kingdom of Manipur. As he wandered through the city, he came upon a magnificent palace. Entering the palace gardens, he suddenly stopped in his tracks. Before him stood a stunningly beautiful girl. 

*Is she Uloopi?* his heart leaped for a moment. But as he went closer, he realized, *No, she is not Uloopi.*

"Who are you, fair maiden?" he asked gently. 

"I am Chitrangada, the daughter of the King of Manipur," she replied, suddenly blushing in confusion under his intense gaze, before turning and fleeing. 

*What power is it that draws me towards her?* Arjuna wondered, instantly captivated. 

Chitrangada was equally smitten and sought an audience with her father, the King. Later, at the royal court, Arjuna formally introduced himself. "O King! I am a noble Kshatriya's son. I wish to marry your daughter."

"Whose son are you, O valiant one?" the King asked. 

"I am Arjuna, the son of Pandu."

The King's eyes widened in delight. "Arjuna! The Pandava! You shall certainly marry my daughter but... she is my only child. The son born to her must become my successor and rule my kingdom after me. He cannot leave for your kingdom."

"It shall be as you wish, O King," Arjuna agreed readily. 

Arjuna married Chitrangada and lived happily with her in Manipur. Months later, a strong, handsome son was born to them. He was named Babhruvahana. 

"Good," Arjuna said, looking at his infant son. "Now it is time for me to continue on my pilgrimage." 

"Please stay on for some more time," Chitrangada begged. Arjuna, loving his new family, agreed. He stayed in Manipur until Babhruvahana was over a year old before finally resuming his exile. 

Meanwhile, deep underwater, Uloopi had been very unhappy since she parted from Arjuna. *Arjuna does not love me,* she wept. *Therefore I don't want to go to him. But I can spend my time with Chitrangada and Babhruvahana, whom he loves.*

Using her magical Naga powers, Uloopi changed her form and visited Chitrangada's palace. "Who are you, dear maiden?" Chitrangada asked, welcoming the stranger. 

"Uloopi, the daughter of the snake-king, Kauravya," she replied softly. "Your co-wife."

Chitrangada welcomed her warmly. "I suppose, this is our son," Uloopi said, lovingly picking up the toddler. "What's your name?"

"Babhruvahana," the boy babbled. 

"He shall become a valiant prince and the unequalled King of Manipur," Uloopi blessed him. As she prepared to leave, she promised, "I have to leave now. But I shall often come to visit him."

"We shall await your visits with pleasure, dear sister," Chitrangada smiled. 

As Babhruvahana grew up, Uloopi, true to her word, often visited. She wielded a great and positive influence over the young prince. She taught him discipline and focus, often telling him thrilling stories about Arjuna's incredible achievements to inspire him. 

Years passed. When Babhruvahana's grandfather passed away, the young prince became the King of Manipur, proving to be a fit and noble successor. Uloopi's visits continued. 

One day, an older Babhruvahana asked her, "You have magical powers, Mother! Tell me, how is my father?"

Uloopi's face grew serious. "Your father and uncles have lost their kingdom to their cousins, the Kauravas, in a deceitful game of dice. Now your father is preparing for the great battle against the Kauravas. It is time I left."

Meanwhile, at the epic battle of Kurukshetra, Arjuna was forced to fight against his revered grand-uncle, Bheeshma. Shielded by Shikhandi, Arjuna showered a volley of arrows on Bheeshma, the commander of the Kauravas. Bheeshma, pierced by countless arrows, fell grievously wounded. 

All the celestials had gathered in the sky to witness this monumental battle. Among them were the Vasus, the divine brothers of Bheeshma. 

"That was unfair," the Vasus declared angrily. "Arjuna should be punished."

The Vasus went to Ganga, the river goddess and their mother. "Arjuna has sinned," they complained. "For this, he should go to hell when he dies."

"So be it," Ganga agreed, cursing Arjuna. 

Uloopi, watching unobserved from the waters, heard everything. Horrified, she rushed to her father, King Kauravya. "My husband does not deserve such a fate. Worse sins have been committed on the battlefield of Kurukshetra. Father, please save my husband from hell."

Kauravya, moved by his daughter's plea, propitiated the Vasus. "What boon do you seek, Kauravya?" the gods asked. 

"Let the curse on Arjuna be lifted!" the Snake-King begged. 

"The curse will be redeemed," the Vasus finally decreed, "only if Arjuna is killed in battle by his own son, Babhruvahana."

Kauravya returned and told Uloopi the grim condition. "That is bad," Uloopi thought, her heart heavy. "But when the time comes, I will have to be there to save my husband."

Meanwhile, the Pandavas had won the terrible war, and Yudhishthira, the eldest, was crowned King of Hastinapura. Soon afterwards, Yudhishthira decided to perform the grand Ashwamedha (Horse) Sacrifice to establish his undisputed sovereignty. 

A magnificent horse was selected. "Let the horse wander at will over the whole earth, proclaiming your glory, O King!" a sage advised. 

"Arjuna, you will protect the horse," Yudhishthira commanded. "If any king stops it, he will have to fight with Arjuna and his army."

As the horse was let loose, Arjuna followed it closely. After wandering through various kingdoms, either winning or conquering allies for the Pandavas, the horse finally arrived at the borders of Manipur. 

"A great kingdom!" Arjuna thought proudly. "My son is its king. I hope he prepares to fight with me like a true Kshatriya."

But when the horse entered the city, Babhruvahana did not order his army to stop it. Instead, he came forward unarmed, accompanied by his ministers, to respectfully welcome his father. 

"Father, I am proud to be your son. I welcome you to my kingdom," Babhruvahana bowed deeply. 

Arjuna frowned angrily. "Oh King! This is not proper. You have failed in your duties. I have come here as the protector of Yudhishthira's horse. If you don't fight, you will be called a coward."

Babhruvahana was stunned by this harsh rebuke. "How can I fight my beloved father?" he asked helplessly. 

Just then, Uloopi arrived on the scene. Knowing the terrible curse of the Vasus, she knew what had to be done. "Do as I tell you, my son," she commanded firmly. "Do not hesitate. Fight your father. It is the only righteous and dutiful thing for you to do."

Babhruvahana thought for a while. *I have lost my father's respect. I can win it back only by fighting him. I will fight.*

He put on his armour, mounted his war chariot, and decisively ordered his men to seize the sacrificial horse. "Seize the horse of Hastinapura!" 

"Ah! My son! My worthy son!" Arjuna cheered inwardly, proud of the boy's warrior spirit, even as he drew his bow to check Babhruvahana's advance. 

A fierce and terrible battle commenced. An arrow sent by Babhruvahana pierced Arjuna through the shoulder. Weak with sudden pain, Arjuna rested briefly, leaning against his bow, looking almost dead. 

"Excellent! O son of Chitrangada! I am pleased by your skill," Arjuna roared as his strength returned. "Now face my arrows and fight without running away!"

But Babhruvahana was a master archer. He effortlessly cut Arjuna's incoming arrows into bits. Infuriated, Arjuna stepped up his attack. He cut off the royal standard on Babhruvahana's chariot and swiftly killed his horses. 

Enraged by the loss of his chariot, Babhruvahana leaped to the ground and fought his father on foot. Arjuna attacked him relentlessly. Finally, blinded by battle-rage, Babhruvahana drew a powerful arrow and shot it straight into Arjuna's heart. 

Arjuna dropped dead on the field. At the exact same moment, partly through extreme exertion but mostly out of overwhelming grief for what he had just done, Babhruvahana collapsed, unconscious. 

All the while, Uloopi stood watching the tragic scene, unperturbed, a picture of absolute serenity. 

A terrified messenger ran to Queen Chitrangada. "I have sad news for you. Your husband has been killed by the King, who himself now lies in a swoon."

Chitrangada turned deathly pale. "Take me to the battlefield!" she ordered. 

When she arrived and saw the devastation, she fell to the ground weeping. When she finally came to her senses, she noticed Uloopi standing calmly nearby. 

"O Uloopi! I know how devoted you are to our husband. Yet I do not find you grieving," Chitrangada said, confused and hurt. Seeing Uloopi remain silent and emotionless, Chitrangada rushed to Arjuna's body. "Rise, dear husband. Here is that horse of yours." 

When all her desperate attempts to revive Arjuna failed, she turned back to Uloopi, pleading. "Bring him back to life, dear Uloopi. I know you can."

But Uloopi remained unmoved. 

"If you don't, I shall give up my life," Chitrangada threatened, sitting stubbornly at Arjuna's feet. 

Just then, Babhruvahana groaned and regained consciousness. "Alas! What a painful sight!" he wept, seeing his mother grieving beside his dead father. "And I killed him!"

His angry gaze fell on Uloopi. "O mother! Your heart seems to be very hard. It does not break even on seeing your husband lying dead before you! Why should it? By killing Arjuna in battle, I have done what you wanted of me. But for me, there is no redemption. I shall have to sink into hell. So dreadfully have I sinned in killing my own father."

Uloopi bore his bitter accusations in complete silence. 

"Listen mother," Babhruvahana declared, addressing Chitrangada. "If my father does not come back to life, I shall follow him. I shall starve myself to death right here on the battlefield. Rejoice, mother! Your son too will die before your eyes." Babhruvahana slumped down next to his parents, ready to die. 

Uloopi watched the three of them carefully. *It is time I acted,* she thought. 

She closed her eyes and focused her mind on the *Sanjeevani*, the magical, life-giving gem of the snake people. Hearing her silent, powerful call, the precious gem came sailing swiftly through the skies and dropped gently into her waiting palms. 

Uloopi walked over to Babhruvahana. "Rise, O son! Your father has been killed by you for his own good."

Babhruvahana looked up, confused. 

"He had been cursed by the Vasus to go to hell for his actions at Kurukshetra," Uloopi explained gently. "The curse could be redeemed only if you killed him in the battlefield."

Babhruvahana stared at her, the horrific guilt slowly lifting from his shoulders, though he still looked at his dead father with immense sorrow. 

"This magic gem of the snake people can revive the dead," Uloopi continued, offering him the glowing jewel. "Place it on your father's wound. He will arise, as valiant as before."

Babhruvahana was delighted. "Dear mother, how could I have ever doubted you? Please forgive me. I am ever grateful to you," he cried, taking the gem. 

He rushed to Arjuna and gently placed the gem on his chest where the fatal arrow had pierced him. Instantly, the wound healed, color returned to Arjuna's face, and he woke up, rubbing his eyes like one who had been fast asleep for a long time. 

He saw Babhruvahana looking down at him in wonder and joy. Arjuna smiled and embraced his son tightly. "My son, I am proud of you. Your valour is unmatched!"

Only then did Arjuna notice Chitrangada and Uloopi standing nearby on the battlefield. 

"What are your mothers doing on the battlefield?" Arjuna asked, surprised. 

Babhruvahana bowed his head respectfully. "Mother Uloopi will explain all."

Uloopi stepped forward and told Arjuna all about the terrible curse of the Vasus, her father's intervention, and how she had orchestrated his 'death' in battle to cleanse his soul and save him from hell. 

Arjuna listened in awe, deeply moved by her incredible sacrifice, foresight, and unwavering devotion. He walked up to her and took her hands in his. 

"Beloved wife, I am indebted to you," Arjuna said softly, his eyes filled with profound love and respect. "Both for this life and my life hereafter."

And so, Uloopi's long-held dream of winning Arjuna's true love and recognition was finally, beautifully fulfilled.
