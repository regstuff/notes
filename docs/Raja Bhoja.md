# Raja Bhoja: A Patron of the Arts and Literature

In the eleventh century A.D., King Sindhula of Dhara (in modern Madhya Pradesh) was blessed with a son, Bhoja, late in his life. Because Bhoja was only a five-year-old child when the aging king's health began to fail, Sindhula grew deeply worried about the future of his kingdom. 

Knowing his younger brother Munja was a mighty, capable, and ambitious warrior, Sindhula made a practical decision. "I have decided to give the kingdom to Munja," the king told his loyal ministers, headed by the wise Buddhisagara. "I will leave Bhoja in his care and renounce the world. It is a wise decision assuring Bhoja the throne after Munja's reign."
So, Sindhula formally handed the crown to Munja. "Bhoja is now your son," Sindhula told his brother. "Train him to rule after you." Having ensured a peaceful succession, Sindhula retired to the forest. 

However, absolute power soon corrupted Munja. A few days later, a highly accomplished Brahman visited the court. Munja, wishing to test the man's wisdom, asked him to read young Bhoja's royal horoscope. 

"O King, Bhoja will rule for over fifty years," the Brahman predicted accurately. "His reign will be incredibly legendary. Even Lord Brahma will not be able to predict the limit of his prosperity."

These words struck deep fear and vicious jealousy into Munja's heart. *What if this prophecy comes true?* Munja panicked. *If Bhoja takes the throne, my own sons will be nothing!*

Consumed by treacherous greed, King Munja secretly summoned his most ruthless royal bodyguard, Vatsaraj. "I want you to take Bhoja to the lonely temple of Bhuwaneshwari tonight," Munja ordered coldly. "Kill him there and bring back his severed head to me."
Though Vatsaraj desperately tried to reason with the king against murdering a harmless, innocent child, Munja's mind was made up. "It shall be done," Vatsaraj finally bowed heavily. 

That night, a deeply conflicted Vatsaraj placed little Bhoja in his chariot and drove out to the dark, isolated temple. As they arrived, Bhoja, sensing the grim atmosphere, remained completely, unnervingly calm. 

"Knave! Who are you to summon me, the prince, here at this dark hour?" the brave five-year-old demanded. 
"Forgive me, my prince," Vatsaraj wept, drawing his sword. "I am only carrying out a royal command. The King has ordered your death."

Instead of crying or begging for his life, young Bhoja simply asked for a fig-leaf. Using a small pin, he calmly scribbled a short verse on the leaf. "Give this to the King," Bhoja instructed bravely. "I am ready. Carry out the royal command."

Looking at the unbelievably fearless boy, Vatsaraj was suddenly filled with intense revulsion for his bloody task. He dropped his sword. *I cannot kill you!* Vatsaraj decided. He secretly smuggled the young prince to an underground hideout outside the city to keep him safe. 

Returning to the palace, Vatsaraj presented King Munja with a fake, bloody head fashioned from magic and clay, along with the scribbled fig-leaf. 

Munja eagerly opened the leaf and read his nephew's final words:
*"Great kings like Lord Rama and Yudhishthira ruled this earth and have gone to heaven. But Bhoomidevi (Mother Earth) did not go with a single one of them. Will she go with you, O Munja?"*

The profound, stinging truth of those words struck Munja like a physical blow. The absolute futility of his treacherous greed suddenly dawned on him. "Alas! In my blinding greed, I have murdered an innocent child whose wisdom far exceeded his years!" Munja wept uncontrollably, collapsing in overwhelming remorse. When he awoke, he hysterically ordered a funeral pyre to be built so he could burn himself alive to atone for his terrible sin. 

Seeing the king's genuine, suicidal remorse, the wise minister Buddhisagara quickly intervened. He brought Vatsaraj, disguised as a powerful 'Shaivite ascetic', into the court. 
"This holy man can bring the prince back to life!" Buddhisagara announced. 

When Vatsaraj finally revealed the living, perfectly healthy Prince Bhoja, Munja burst into tears of overwhelming relief. He tightly embraced his nephew, immediately stepped down from the throne, placed the young boy upon it, and left the palace forever to live a life of strict renunciation to atone for his sins. 

***

Raja Bhoja grew up to be a brilliant, incredibly strong, and magnanimous young king. He was deeply committed to justice, wisdom, and the ultimate pursuit of knowledge. 

One day, a poor, wandering Brahman approached the young king. "The human body being mortal, it is only the absolute glory of kings that lives after them," the Brahman spoke boldly. "Unmatched generosity is the greatest quality of a glorious king. Since you, O King, are not generous, I see no point in acknowledging your royalty!"

Instead of being angered by the insult, Raja Bhoja was deeply impressed by the man's fearless honesty. He heavily rewarded the Brahman and threw open his royal treasury. 

Word of Raja Bhoja's unequaled generosity, deep wisdom, and immense love for literature rapidly spread across the country. The greatest poets, scholars, and brilliant savants of the era flocked to his grand court in Dhara, including legendary figures like Vararuchi, Bana, Mayura, and Shankara. 

But the greatest of them all was a brilliant new poet who arrived one evening at dusk. His name was **Kalidasa**. 

Kalidasa’s poetry was so breathtakingly beautiful that Raja Bhoja took an immediate, intense liking to him. A deep, powerful friendship was quickly born between the king and the poet. 

However, this incredibly close friendship sparked bitter, burning jealousy among the other established court poets. Unable to match Kalidasa's sheer genius, men like Bana and Mayura resorted to vicious treachery. They bribed a cunning palace slave-girl named Tarangavati to help them execute a sinister plot. 

One night, while Tarangavati was gently massaging the sleeping King Bhoja's feet, she noticed the king stirring. Pretending to be deep in sleep herself, she began loudly "sleep-talking":
*"The evil Kalidasa... disguised as a slave-girl... meets the unfaithful Queen Lilavati secretly in her apartments..."*

Hearing these seemingly innocent, subconscious words, Raja Bhoja sat bolt upright, utterly consumed by a sudden, blinding storm of jealousy and grief. Early the next morning, without conducting a single investigation or asking a single question, the furious king summoned Kalidasa. 
"Leave the kingdom forthwith!" Bhoja commanded coldly. "Let me not set sight on your treacherous face again!"

Deeply hurt and completely puzzled by the sudden, unjust banishment, Kalidasa secretly took refuge in the hidden underground cellar of his loyal friend, Vilasvati, choosing to remain in the city. 

Meanwhile, Queen Lilavati heard the shocking news. She furiously confronted the king. "Why did you banish the absolute jewel of your court?"
Bhoja glared at her. "I spared *your* life out of sheer kindness, Queen!" 

Realizing she was being accused of vile infidelity, the proud Queen laughed sarcastically. To prove her absolute, unblemished innocence, she willingly underwent a terrifying trial by fire, emerging completely unscathed. 

Realizing he had made a horrific, completely baseless mistake, Raja Bhoja was crushed by intense guilt. Desperate to find his lost friend, he devised a brilliant plan. He summoned all the remaining, jealous poets to his court and presented them with an incredibly complex, half-finished poetic stanza. 
"Complete this stanza," the king challenged them coldly. "If you cannot, I shall banish every single one of you from my land!"

For eight agonizing days, Bana and the other poets desperately racked their brains, but the poetic words completely evaded them. Finally, facing humiliating exile, they packed their belongings on bullock carts and began leaving the city. 

As they trudged dejectedly down the road, they passed by Vilasvati's house. Hiding in the cellar, Kalidasa heard their desperate, weeping voices. Taking absolute pity on his foolish rivals, Kalidasa quickly disguised himself as a wandering, bearded bard and approached them. 

"What troubles you, learned men?" the disguised Kalidasa asked. 
When the desperate poets explained the impossible stanza, the disguised Kalidasa simply smiled and effortlessly completed the impossibly complex poem in seconds! 

Absolutely astounded, the poets rushed back to the royal court. The cunning Bana proudly recited the completed, breathtaking stanza as if it were his own creation. 

Raja Bhoja's face immediately lit up. He knew instantly that only one man on earth possessed the sheer genius to compose such flawless beauty. He deliberately handed Bana a massive reward of 15 lakh gold pieces, completely ignoring the others. 

Furious at being cheated out of their share, the other jealous poets immediately broke rank. "Bana has done us a great injustice, O King!" they complained loudly. "He didn't write it! We got the answer from a wandering bard!"

"A bard?" Raja Bhoja smiled knowingly. "Call for my horse! Get ready to ride out!"

Following the poets' directions, King Bhoja rode straight to Vilasvati's house. Bursting through the doors, he finally found Kalidasa. "Dear Kalidasa, I have wronged you terribly!" the king wept, pulling his beloved friend into a tight embrace. "Please say that you will forgive me and ride back to court with me on my own horse!"

Ever magnanimous, Kalidasa instantly forgave the king. He was triumphantly reinstated as the absolute crown jewel of the royal court. Raja Bhoja, having learned a profound lesson in trust and true wisdom, went on to become one of the greatest, most legendary patrons of arts and literature in Indian history.
