# Krishna and Jarasandha

Long, long ago, the handsome, wealthy, and powerful King Brihadratha ruled over Magadha. He was married to the beautiful twin daughters of the King of Kashi. Though he loved both queens equally, he had no sons. 

One day, the illustrious sage Chandakaushika visited the city. Brihadratha went to pay his respects and sought a boon for an heir. The sage closed his eyes, meditated, and gave him a divine mango. "Let your wife eat this and obtain a son," the rishi blessed him. 

Brihadratha walked away with the mango, unable to decide which queen to give it to. "I cannot give the fruit to one and make the other unhappy. They shall each have half of it." 

When the queens delivered, each gave birth to only half a baby. Shocked and horrified, the midwives threw the two halves into the backyard of the palace. 

A few hours later, a Rakshasi named Jara passed by. Her nose led her to the two bundles of flesh. "What! One body rent in two. I'll join the halves. It will be easier to carry one bundle," she thought. 

The moment she joined the two pieces, the baby came to life and roared loudly! Hearing the roar, King Brihadratha and his queens rushed out. 

"O King, I, Jara, have saved your son for you. Take him," the Rakshasi said kindly. 

"Good lady, since it was you who put him together and made him complete, he shall be named Jarasandha," the grateful King declared. 

When Jarasandha grew into a mighty young lad, Sage Chandakaushika visited Magadha again and blessed him. "Your son shall become the King of Kings. All who stand in his way will meet with destruction." Brihadratha retired to the forest, leaving the throne to Jarasandha. 

Jarasandha brought almost all adjoining kingdoms under his sway. He married his daughters, Asti and Prapti, to Kamsa, King of Mathura. When Krishna killed the evil Kamsa, the widowed daughters returned to Magadha in tears. 

Infuriated, Jarasandha swore, "I shall rid the earth of that vile Yadava, Krishna, and all his tribe!" 

Jarasandha attacked Mathura seventeen times with massive armies, but each time Krishna and Balarama decimated his forces, deliberately letting Jarasandha escape so he would assemble more evil warriors for them to destroy. 

On the eighteenth occasion, Kalayavana also attacked Mathura. Krishna used his yogic power to safely transport the citizens of Mathura to the new island fortress of Dwaraka. He then lured Kalayavana into the cave of Muchukunda, where the Yavana was reduced to ashes. 

Returning to Mathura, Krishna and Balarama faced Jarasandha's army once more. Pretending to be afraid, the two brothers fled on foot to Mount Pravarshana. Jarasandha set fire to the mountain to burn them alive. But Krishna and Balarama leaped from the mountain top, crossed the surrounding armies, and returned to Dwaraka unnoticed. Jarasandha returned to Magadha, believing he had killed them. 

Conceited with his perceived victory, Jarasandha began capturing all kings who owed allegiance to the Yadavas, planning to sacrifice one hundred kings to Lord Shiva. He had already imprisoned eighty-six kings. 

Meanwhile, in Indraprastha, Yudhishthira desired to perform the Rajasuya Yajna to establish his suzerainty. Krishna advised him, "To perform the Rajasuya is the privilege of an Emperor. Jarasandha will have to be defeated, and the kings imprisoned by him released." 

Yudhishthira hesitated, knowing Jarasandha's invincibility. But Bhima and Arjuna eagerly volunteered to accompany Krishna. 

Disguised as Brahmins, Krishna, Arjuna, and Bhima set out for Magadha. They reached Girivraja, Jarasandha's capital, at the hour he entertained petitioners. 

Jarasandha, seeing their scarred forearms, suspected they were Kshatriyas in disguise. "Seek whatever you desire. I am prepared to give you everything, even my head," he offered. 

"Then grant one of us a single combat with you," Krishna demanded, revealing their true identities. 

Jarasandha laughed contemptuously. "I will not fight you, Krishna, for you are a coward who fled to the sea. Arjuna is too young. Bhima alone is my equal. I'll take him on." 

Jarasandha handed a huge mace to Bhima. In the arena outside the city, the two mighty warriors fought. They hurled maces at each other until the maces broke into pieces. Then, with fists of steel, they wrestled barehanded for twenty-seven days without a decisive outcome. 

On the twenty-eighth day, Bhima confided in Krishna, "Krishna, I remain undefeated, but I cannot overthrow Jarasandha in a duel." 

Krishna, knowing the secret of Jarasandha's birth, picked up a twig, split it in two lengthways, and threw the two pieces in opposite directions. 

Bhima caught the hint. Stepping into the arena with fresh confidence, Bhima waited for an opportunity. As Jarasandha charged, Bhima stepped aside, caught him by the leg, and threw him to the ground. Pressing one foot on Jarasandha's lower body, Bhima took hold of his other leg and tore the body of the evil king into two, flinging the two halves away from each other! Jarasandha died instantly. 

Krishna installed Jarasandha's son, Sahadeva, on the throne of Magadha, after making him promise to liberate the captive kings. Thousands of imprisoned kings were freed, showering praise on Krishna. 

Krishna, Bhima, and Arjuna returned victorious to Indraprastha, clearing the way for Yudhishthira to perform the grand Rajasuya Yajna.
