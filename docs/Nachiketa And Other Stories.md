# Nachiketa

Thousands of years ago, there lived a sage called Vajashrava. He had a young son named Nachiketa, who was deeply eager to study and learn. Vajashrava sent his son to a Gurukula, a forest school, where he was taught sacred hymns and the duties of a disciple. 

When Nachiketa returned home, he found his father preparing to perform the *Vishvajit* sacrifice. It was one of the greatest of sacrifices, for the one who performs it gives away everything he possesses. Nachiketa watched as his father brought in his herd of cows to give away as gifts to the Brahmanas. 

"Oh, God! What a poor offering this is!" Nachiketa thought, seeing the pathetic state of the animals. "These cows are sick and old, too weak to give milk. By giving away these pathetic cows, father will surely go to the dark world of the joyless. But what can my poor father do? This is all he possesses!"

Determined to help, Nachiketa approached his father. "I, too, belong to my father," he reasoned to himself. "I am hale and hearty and I can be of some use. By offering myself, I can save my father."

He asked Vajashrava, "To whom will you give me, father?"

Vajashrava, busy with the rites and irritated by the interruption, ignored him. Nachiketa repeated his question a second, and then a third time. 

Finally, losing his temper, the sage yelled, "I will give you to Yama, the god of death!"

Vajashrava instantly regretted his angry words, crying out in sorrow. But Nachiketa remained calm. "O my son! What have I done?" his father wept. "No, don't leave me."

"Father, do not grieve," Nachiketa replied softly. "Men of old have passed away and those of days to come will also pass away. Let me go with your blessing. A promise made during a sacrifice must be kept."

Nachiketa departed and arrived at Yama's abode, but the Lord of Death was away. For three days and nights, the young boy waited outside the gates without a morsel of food or a drop of water. 

When Yama finally returned, he was horrified to learn that a Brahmana had been left waiting unhosted. "Hope and expectations, sacrifices and good deeds are all snatched away from him who does not offer hospitality to a Brahmana," Yama worried. He hurried to Nachiketa. "Since you have come as a guest and have received no hospitality for three days, you may choose three boons to atone for my neglect."

Nachiketa bowed respectfully. "Grant my father peace of mind and make him cheerful. Let him greet me cheerfully when he sees me released from the jaws of death. This is the first boon I choose."

"He shall have it," Yama granted easily. 

"In heaven, there is no hunger, thirst, old age, or death," Nachiketa continued. "Teach me the sacrifice by the performance of which no hunger and thirst will be felt. This is the second boon I choose."

Yama gladly taught him the intricacies of the fire sacrifice. "Henceforth such a sacrifice will be known as the Nachiketa sacrifice," Yama declared, pleased with the boy's sharp intellect. 

"Now for the third boon," Yama prompted. 

"Some people believe that there is life after death; others don't. Tell me who is right," Nachiketa asked. 

Yama became thoughtful. He had not expected such a profound question from someone so young. "Even the gods are not certain about this," Yama said. "It is not easily understood. Ask for something else. Ask for cattle, elephants, gold, an empire, long life—anything but this." He even offered celestial maidens to sing and dance for him. 

But Nachiketa was resolute. "I am not interested in things that bring momentary happiness. I want that which will bring everlasting bliss. I do not choose any other boon."

Yama was deeply impressed. "Nachiketa, two paths are open to men. The wise take the good path and realise the Self. The others foolishly choose the path of pleasure and go through the never-ending cycle of birth and death. This boy has steadfast faith and is free from temptation. Since he is so worthy, I will instruct him further."

Yama then began to teach Nachiketa the ultimate truth. "When a man is dead, it is the body that perishes, not the Self. The Self resides in the body as a master does in his chariot. The intellect is the driver. The sense organs are the horses that pull the chariot... The Self is never born, nor does it die... When all desires have been given up, man becomes immortal and even in this world he realises Brahman, the Supreme Being."

Enlightened by the Lord of Death himself, Nachiketa took leave of Yama and returned home. His father joyously welcomed him back. As Nachiketa grew older, his renown spread, and many seekers of truth came to his hermitage for guidance, knowing him as one who had attained true self-realization.

# Satyakama

Gautama was one of the foremost sages in ancient India. Many seekers of truth found their way to his forest-school, hoping to be accepted as his disciples. 

One day, an eight-year-old boy approached the sage. "Salutations to you, O Master," the boy said. "Accept me, Satyakama, as your disciple."

"Tell me, child, to which family do you belong?" Gautama asked gently. 

"I don't know to which family I belong, nor does my mother," Satyakama replied honestly. "I have no father, sir. My mother, Jabala, has asked me to call myself 'Satyakama Jabala'. Please take me as your disciple."

Before the sage could speak, a senior disciple angrily interposed. "Sir, only a Brahmana is entitled to sacred knowledge. Satyakama may not be one."

Gautama looked at the boy and smiled. "He who speaks the truth without fear or hesitation is a Brahmana. Satyakama, I accept you as my disciple. What goal have you set for yourself?"

"Sir, I want to experience Brahman—the Supreme Reality," Satyakama declared. 

The master became thoughtful. "This child is truthful and sincere," he thought. "I will help him in achieving his goal." 

Gautama took Satyakama to the cattle pen, which held four hundred weak and sickly cows. "Take them to a distant forest," Gautama instructed. "Come back only when they are fat and healthy and number over a thousand."

"I will obey at once," Satyakama said, leading the cattle away into the wilderness. 

At first, he wondered why his Guru had sent him to the forest to experience Brahman. But soon, immersed in the beauty and peace of nature, he stopped worrying. He cared for the cattle diligently, and under his gentle care, the cows improved in health and multiplied. 

Years passed. One day, while searching for a missing calf, Satyakama felt a strange peace as he gazed at an aged bull. He thought he heard the bull speaking to him: "Brahman is in the North. He is in the South, the East and the West." He realized that Brahman was present everywhere. 

Noticing that the herd had grown to over a thousand, Satyakama began his return journey. 

That evening, as he sat by the fire he had built, he felt the fire speaking to him: "Brahman pervades the earth, the sky, the atmosphere and the ocean."

The next morning, watching the sunrise over the water, he wondered, "How luminous is the sun! Who makes it shine?" A passing swan seemed to answer, "Brahman is in the sun, in the moon, in the lightning and in the fire. It is Brahman who makes the sun shine!"

As he continued his journey, profound questions arose in his mind. "Then who makes me breathe? Who makes me see, hear and think?" As a bird flew past him, the answer came clearly: "It is Brahman. It is Brahman. It is Brahman."

When Satyakama finally reached the forest-school, Gautama looked at him in wonder. "Satyakama, you glow like one who has attained knowledge of Brahman. How did this happen?"

Satyakama described his experiences with the bull, the fire, the swan, and the bird. "But knowledge imparted by the teacher helps the most in attaining one's goal," he added humbly. "Please instruct me, sir."

Gautama then formally imparted the sacred knowledge to his deserving disciple, who later became a great sage himself, knowing that nature itself was the greatest teacher of all.

# Prajapati

In ancient times, the Devas (celestial beings), the Manavas (men on earth), and the Asuras (demons) all found themselves dissatisfied with their lives. 

The Devas lived in heaven, enjoying all the comforts and pleasures they could aspire for. Yet, they felt a lingering emptiness. "We are free from hunger, thirst, old age, and death," they pondered. "Why aren't we happy? We seem to have everything, yet we feel the lack of something."

The Manavas, unlike the Devas, led a hard life of toil on earth. Even when they earned riches and stored away good harvests, they found no peace. "We have no contentment," they lamented. "What we lack is peace of mind."

The Asuras lived in the netherworld, ruling through fear and violence. But despite their power, they too were overcome by a feeling of dissatisfaction. "Although we are powerful and feared by all, we are not happy," they admitted to each other. 

Seeking answers, all three groups journeyed to the abode of Prajapati, the father of all creation, and prayed for guidance. 

"O Father, tell us what we should do to achieve contentment and peace," they asked in unison. 

Prajapati looked at his children and uttered just one syllable: 
**"DA...."**

"DA? What could that mean?" everyone wondered. "I think it is the first syllable of a secret word which will tell us what to do," they concluded. They all left for their respective homes to ponder over the syllable. 

Upon returning to heaven, the Devas were about to resume their life of pleasure when one of them suddenly had a realization. "I've got it! I know what 'DA' means!" he exclaimed. "'DA' means 'Damyata' (Restrain yourselves). Yes, the Father has asked us to restrain ourselves."

The Devas put aside their endless indulgences. They began to practice self-control, and in doing so, they experienced a profound satisfaction they had never known before. 

On earth, a Manava was furiously throwing stones at birds stealing his grain. Suddenly, he paused. "I've got it! 'DA' means 'Datta' (Give)," he realized. "The Father wants us to give away, not to hoard. Let the birds share our grain." Men began to practice charity, discovering that there was immense joy in giving. 

In the netherworld, an Asura was about to execute a captive when he remembered the syllable. "Wait! 'DA' means 'Dayadhvam' (Be compassionate)," he shouted, dropping his sword. "Yes, the Father wants us to show compassion. Release him." The Asuras began to practice compassion, and their hearts were filled with a kind of joy they had never experienced before. 

And so, the Devas practiced virtue and forsook their vanity; the Manavas practiced charity and found peace; and the Asuras practiced compassion and shed their cruelty. 

And even today, to remind people of the three cardinal virtues—Self-control (*Damyata*), Charity (*Datta*), and Compassion (*Dayadhvam*)—the heavens thunder now and again: 

**DA! DA! DA!**
