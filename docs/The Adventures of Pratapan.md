# The Adventures of Pratapan

Kanakachala Mudaliar, a prabhu of Satyapuri, had one son, Pratapan. His sister's husband, Sambanda Mudaliar, another prabhu, had one daughter, Jnanam. The two cousins had grown up walking the same garden paths, and by the time they were of age, Pratapan was hopelessly in love with her.

"Your face is as lovely as this rose, Jnanam," he told her one afternoon among the rose bushes, offering her a bloom.

"A rose in bloom soon withers away," she said.

"So will the beauty you admire."

"No, Jnanam. Your face will ever remain lovely — like the moon."

"The moon only reflects the light it receives from the sun," she answered, unmoved by flattery. "You must make what is unchanging, everlasting, and without flaw the object of your devotion — not me, a mortal full of imperfections."

"Beauty, intelligence, and humility — what a rare combination! How can I help but love you, Jnanam?"

A sudden commotion cut the moment short — a runaway bull came crashing through the garden, and Pratapan lunged to catch Jnanam and pull her clear before it reached them. Shaken, she clung to him for a moment. "I thought it was the end," she said. "Never," he told her. "My love will always protect you."

Their fathers were only too pleased. "No proposal could have been more welcome, Kanakachalam," said Sambandam, and the two men happily arranged the match. But at the engagement ceremony itself, an offhand question — where would the young couple live after the wedding — set the two prabhus at each other's throats. Each accused the other's grandfather of being descended from a barber, a washerman, from lowly trade rather than noble blood, each swearing he'd sooner marry his child to a dog, even to a frog, than to the other's house. An elderly guest tried to shame them into sense — "Now, now, even commoners would not speak like that, and you claim to be prabhus!" — but they ignored him entirely, and the engagement broke apart on the spot.

Pratapan was heartbroken. Wandering among the ruins near his home, he decided there was only one way out: he and Jnanam would run away together and marry without their parents' blessing. He wrote to tell her so. Her answer disappointed him. "What you suggest goes against my grain," she wrote back. "I will not marry anyone without my parents' consent. If, however, our parents do not respect our wishes, we are free to remain unmarried." Pratapan understood, and accepted it — she would not defy her family, but neither would she ever belong to anyone else.

Some months later, Sambanda Mudaliar left Satyapuri with his family to visit his native village, and Pratapan lived with the ache of watching her come and go without being able to speak to her. Then one day her party returned — every palanquin but hers. "Where is her palanquin?" the servants cried. Jnanam had vanished.

Pratapan set out after her at once. On the road he heard cries for help and found two bandits attacking a bullock cart; he fought them off, and out from the cart stepped two frightened women — one of them Jnanam. She had been abducted, she told him, by men sent by the chief prabhu of Poongavoor, whose son wanted to marry her and who, offended that his letters asking for her hand had gone unanswered, had simply taken her by force. She'd escaped once already while her captors were distracted, only to be caught again before this second rescue.

Pratapan brought her home to her father, who by now had had quite enough of pride costing him his daughter. "Who is behind this? Tell us all, if you value your life," he demanded of the men Pratapan handed over, and once he understood, there was no more hesitation. Sambandam and Kanakachalam, chastened, agreed at last that the couple should marry — with a face-saving compromise besides, that the newlyweds would live six months of the year at each father's house in turn, so that neither prabhu need feel slighted. And so Pratapan and Jnanam were married, with Pratapan's old friend Kanakasabhai, son of Devaraja Pillai of Adivoor, among the guests.

The peace didn't last. Within months the two fathers were quarrelling again — this time over whose grandchild-to-be would belong to whom, each unable to resist provoking the other, mother-in-law and father-in-law each pointedly asking Jnanam and Pratapan which of them mattered more. Disgusted by the pettiness, the young couple ran away one night to escape it all, and rode for Adivoor, where Devaraja Pillai took them in and sent word to both families that their children were safe.

A few weeks after the wedding — before all this later trouble — Pratapan had gone hunting on an elephant with Kanakan, waiting to meet him further down the trail. A tiger sprang from the undergrowth; Pratapan fired and wounded it, but the shot sent his elephant into a panicked, days-long stampede through the hills. On the third day, thrown violently against a rock outcrop, Pratapan managed to grab hold of the ledge and haul himself up as the elephant crashed on without him — alive, but alone, filthy, and lost on a cliffside with no idea where he was.

He made his way to a nearby town, where a poor cobbler sat selling slippers under a tree. Pratapan bought a pair, then — moved by the man's threadbare state — impulsively promised to "give him a price that would make him happy," but paid only a single rupee, far short of what the cobbler had hoped for. "What! Just one rupee? I am not at all happy," the old man said, refusing to hand back the money but refusing just as firmly to let Pratapan leave until his promise of happiness was kept. The dispute went to the local judge, who ruled squarely against Pratapan: he had made a promise and broken it, and the law of the land required he be made to answer for it — by daybreak, or else. In his anger and confusion, Pratapan blurted a rash oath he barely meant: that the sun itself would never rise again over that land. The furious judge sentenced him to death at dawn for the insult, and he was thrown in prison.

There he met a fellow prisoner who explained the strange kingdom he'd stumbled into: Vikramapuri, whose king had died two years before leaving only an infant daughter, since when corrupt ministers had ruled in her place, throwing into prison anyone who dared object. Pratapan, as it happened, knew something his cellmates didn't — a total solar eclipse was due to begin that very morning before sunrise. "All that will end tomorrow, when the sun refuses to rise," he told them with a confidence that seemed like madness, "you will see better days soon."

When dawn came and the sky stayed dark, panic swept the kingdom — the stranger's curse had come true, and only a great yogi could have such power. The people begged at his cell door for mercy; when the eclipse passed and the sun reappeared right on cue, they hailed him as its cause and their saviour in the same breath. With no king and a citizenry desperate for one, they turned to their traditional method of choosing a ruler: releasing the sacred elephant to garland whoever it chose. It garlanded a stranger elsewhere in the crowd, who was declared king on the spot — and when he was brought before his new subjects, he recognised Pratapan as the very yogi whose curse and pardon had thrown the kingdom into chaos and back out of it again.

The cobbler, still smarting over his unresolved grievance, pushed forward: "This man has stolen my happiness. I sold him a pair of slippers. In return he promised to make me happy. But he failed to keep his word." "You shall have justice right away," the new king said — and ruled, to general delight, that Pratapan's debt was cleared simply by having brought back the sun for the whole kingdom, cobbler included. He ordered Pratapan released, and the next day, to the court's approval, named him chief minister — "The yogi? An excellent choice!"

That night, a soft hand touched his forehead as he slept restlessly in an old choultry. It was Jnanam — she had followed his elephant's tracks all the way from the mountain where it had bolted, disguising herself as a man to search for him, and had arrived in Vikramapuri only after he'd already been swept into office. Reunited at last, they settled into court life together; Jnanam took a personal interest in the education of Anandi, the young daughter of Vikramapuri's own king, dead two years and leaving the girl too young to rule in her own name — which was the very reason the throne had stood empty until the elephant intervened.

Under Pratapan's ministry and Jnanam's tutoring, both the kingdom and the princess were set back in order. Once Jnanam judged Anandi capable of ruling in truth, she told the court so herself, and the sitting king — who had held the throne only as its accidental, temporary keeper — announced he meant to hand it back to its rightful heir and return home to his own land. Grateful courtiers begged him to stay, even floating marriage to Anandi as a reason to remain, but he and Pratapan's household had both had their fill of Vikramapuri's palace intrigues. Before the succession could be settled, Pratapan and Jnanam slipped away quietly one night, leaving nothing behind but a letter at Anandi's bedside revealing who they really were and why they had come.

A week later they were home in Satyapuri — and the next day, a visitor arrived at their door: Anandi herself, who had tracked them down. "You are like my sister," she told Jnanam. "I have neither father nor mother. Please come and bless me on my coronation day." They readily agreed, and travelled back to Vikramapuri one final time. Amid the cheering of the crowds, Jnanam crowned Anandi queen of Vikramapuri with her own hands. "Long live our queen!" the people cried — and Pratapan and Jnanam, their strange adventure finally complete, returned home once more to Satyapuri, no longer the quarrelling in-laws' problem to solve, but simply, at last, husband and wife.
