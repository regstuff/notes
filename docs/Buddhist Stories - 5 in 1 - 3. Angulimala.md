# Angulimala: The Bandit Who Took Refuge in Buddha

Long, long ago, a king called Prasenajit ruled over Kosala from his capital, Shravasti. One night when he was fast asleep, his room was suddenly lit by incredible flashes of light. He woke up with a start. 
"Who - what's that? The weapons!" he gasped. "What's happening to them? Why do they glitter so?"

The next moment, however, all was dark again. "Was it a nightmare? Was my imagination playing tricks on me? Or was I really dazzled by the light from the weapons?" King Prasenajit wondered. 

The next morning, any doubts he might have had vanished. His generals reported, "At midnight, the weapons in the armoury and in every house of Kosala blazed, for a moment, with a brilliant light." 
"A strange thing happened last night, your majesty!"

At that exact moment in the city, a son was born in the house of the royal priest, Garga. 
"How handsome is our newborn son!" his mother smiled. 
"He is, no doubt," Garga frowned, pacing the room. "But I am worried. Immediately after his birth, strange lights issued forth from the weapons of the night-guards."

When the perturbed Garga consulted a learned astrologer, the old man shook his head grimly. "I regret to tell you that your son is going to be a robber—a menace to our people."
Garga went to the palace and told the king the story. 
"Ah! That explains why the weapons shone so brightly last night!" Prasenajit realized. 
"Your majesty, I cannot rear a son who is destined to be a menace to the kingdom," the royal priest pleaded. "The scriptures say that one life may be sacrificed to save a hundred. Therefore permit me to put him to death."

"No! I won't allow the killing of an innocent baby!" King Prasenajit firmly refused. "Lord Buddha has taught me to believe in the innate goodness of every living creature. Let your son have a sound education. Under your guidance, I have no doubt that he will grow up to be a good citizen."
"Thank you, your majesty. I will try my best to instil virtue in him."

The infant, who was named Ahimsaka, grew up to be an exceptionally intelligent and well-behaved boy. "One as dedicated as he is to the study of the scriptures can never become a robber," Garga thought proudly. 
When Ahimsaka was about fourteen years old, he requested, "Father, please permit me to go to Takshashila."
"You may go, my son. You have my blessings."

Ahimsaka left for the great university of Takshashila that very day. "All the way to Takshashila for the sake of learning!" he smiled to himself. "How can Ahimsaka ever become a robber? There must have been some mistake in the prophecy."

On reaching Takshashila, Ahimsaka went to a great scholar of those days. "I, Ahimsaka, son of Garga of Shravasti, salute you. Accept me as your disciple, master."
"You are welcome, my son. I will teach you."

Ahimsaka soon won the heart of the master. "Look at Ahimsaka," the master praised him in front of the others. "Within such a short time, he has mastered what you could not in the last three years. He has a great future before him."

Such comments aroused the deep jealousy of the other disciples. "Ahimsaka thinks too much of himself," they grumbled. "Our teacher's praise has gone to his head. We must do something about it."
They hatched a plot to estrange the master from his favourite disciple. 

One day, they saw the master approaching. "Quick! The master is approaching. Let us begin our little drama," the lead disciple whispered. 
"Ahimsaka is a great scholar," one said loudly. 
"That's why our master praises him so," another added. 
"Indeed, he is a greater scholar even than our master!" the first declared. 
"At least the wife of our revered master thinks so," the second mocked. 

The master overheard this and became deeply thoughtful. "Perhaps there is an element of truth in what the boys say. One day Ahimsaka may prove to be more learned than me. I must see that he is made an outcaste, shunned by all."
When he entered his house, he saw Ahimsaka chatting with his wife. "Does my wife really think he is a greater scholar than me? How attentively she listens to what he says! Does she have a greater regard for him than she has for me?" he wondered jealously. "Whatever the truth may be, Ahimsaka will have to go."

He entered the hall stealthily so that his wife and Ahimsaka, deeply engrossed in their discussions, did not notice his presence. 
"Ahimsaka! Get up!" the master suddenly barked. 
"Pardon me, master. I..." Ahimsaka stammered, jumping up. 
"I have been standing here all this while. Have you become so arrogant that you forget to acknowledge the presence of your guru?"
The master, however, didn't give him a chance to continue. "Humility should be the outcome of knowledge, not arrogance. You have no place here. You may go."

As a bemused, heart-broken Ahimsaka walked away from the master's house, the other disciples gloated over the success of their plot. "It worked!" 

When Ahimsaka returned to Shravasti, his father was furious. "Why are you back so soon? Have you finished your studies?"
"No, father. I was sent away. I incurred the displeasure of my master. But I..."
"What! Incurred the displeasure of your master!" Garga exploded. "Don't show me your accursed face! Go away from here!"

Ahimsaka was thrown out onto the streets. "What have I done to deserve such a fate? What shall I do? I will try to make a living with what little knowledge I have."
But Ahimsaka's efforts to find some work proved futile. "Employ a youth who has incurred the displeasure of his master? Never!" people scoffed. 
He was completely shunned by all. "Close the door! I don't want him to enter our house and pollute it," neighbors sneered when he walked by. 

"They slam their doors on my face," Ahimsaka wept. "Why my master sent me away, I still do not know. Father did not even want to hear my story."
Deep in thought, Ahimsaka left Kosala. "What was my fault? Why have I been treated so cruelly? They label me a sinner. But it is I who have been sinned against!"

Suddenly, a highway robber jumped out. "Surrender whatever you have if you value your life!"
Ahimsaka's grief turned into explosive rage. "No! No! No!" he screamed, brutally punching the robber to the ground. "Take this! And this! There! I am giving you all that I have!"
Disgusted, Ahimsaka let the bleeding man go. "He has shown me the way," Ahimsaka decided darkly. "I will be a highway robber. I'll be making a living while I take revenge on the society that rejected me."

Later, in the evening, a fleet of bullock carts carrying merchandise passed that way on their way to Kaushambi. 
"Halt!" Ahimsaka yelled, stepping onto the road with a sickle. 
"My god! A robber! He is one, we are many. Let us capture him!" the merchants shouted, drawing their swords. 
But the merchants were no match for Ahimsaka's furious strength. He slaughtered them all. 
"All dead! Good!" he panted, looking at the bodies. "I will keep their *angulis* (little fingers) and make a garland of them."

Soon, Ahimsaka's atrocious deeds became the talk of Kosala. 
"He is a monster," terrified citizens whispered. 
"No one knows who he is nor where he comes from."
"He wears a strange garland... so I've heard—an *Angulimala*! (A garland of little fingers)."
And Ahimsaka began to be known across the land as Angulimala. 

The traders who had to travel were the worst hit by Angulimala, as the shortest route to Kaushambi was through his haunted forest. 
"Ha, ha! There is no escape from me. Get down all of you," Angulimala would laugh as he stopped their carts. As soon as the merchants were massacred, he would feed their bodies to the vultures. "Come, my friends! This feast is for you."
"Spare my life. Have mercy on me," survivors would beg. 
"Mercy? What does that mean?" Angulimala would laugh maniacally, chopping off their little fingers. "I want to kill you for your little finger, ha! ha!"

At last the terror-stricken subjects turned to King Prasenajit. "Your majesty, deliver us from Angulimala."
"I will command my forest guards to capture the notorious murderer," the king promised. 
As a large contingent of heavily armed forest guards entered Angulimala's haunt, the bandit watched from a cliff. "A whole army of them! Good! That many more fingers for my garland!"
Lifting up huge boulders, Angulimala hurled them down at the guards, and then sprang upon them like a tiger. Only a few were able to escape with their lives. 

When they reported back to Prasenajit, the king was stunned. "Just one man, and you were helpless against him!"
That evening, the royal drummer went round the city. "Give ear! Give ear! People are advised to avoid Angulimala. Those going to Kaushambi should take the route via Magadha..."

As travelers entirely abandoned the forest route, Angulimala waited in vain for victims. "My garland has begun to wither," he realized. "I need new fingers! I wish someone would pass this way."
Suddenly he spotted a lone monk walking calmly by. "Halt!" Angulimala yelled, grabbing his sickle. "My prayer has been heard!"

Angulimala ran fiercely after the monk. To his astonishment, however, the monk, who seemed to be walking at a very leisurely pace, was always exactly a step ahead. No matter how fast Angulimala sprinted, he could not reach him. 
At last, exhausted and panting heavily from the chase, Angulimala paused. "O monk, stop! Stop moving!"
The Buddha turned to look at him. "I am not moving. I am at rest. It is you who are in perpetual motion."
"What do you mean?"
"My mind is at rest," the Buddha explained gently. "You are yet to find peace of mind, my child."

Angulimala was stunned. *"My child! He calls me his child! No! He cannot mean it,"* he thought. 
"Don't you know who I am?" Angulimala snarled, raising his bloody sickle. "I don't want your preaching. I want your little fingers."
"Take them, my son, if it makes you happy," Buddha replied peacefully. 
"Along with your fingers, I take your life too!"
"By all means do, my son, if it brings you peace of mind."

Overwhelmed by the Buddha's absolute fearlessness and unconditional love, Angulimala's rage shattered. He dropped his weapon and collapsed weeping at the feet of the monk. "Master!"
"Get up, my child."
"Master, henceforth I will never kill," Angulimala vowed. 

The monk brought him to the monastery on the outskirts of Shravasti. "Anathapindika, I have brought yet another brother—Angulimala," he introduced the fearsome bandit to the other monks. 
The loving monk was none other than Lord Buddha. 

The next morning, King Prasenajit visited the monastery. He was dressed in full battle armor. 
"It looks as though you have started on a military expedition," Buddha smiled. 
"Yes, master. I want to exterminate that monster—Angulimala. I have come for your blessings."
"Supposing Angulimala gives up the path of violence and begins to live the life of an ascetic, what will your reaction be?"
"I will salute him then, my lord," the King admitted. "But pardon me, I can't imagine Angulimala as an ascetic."
"But he has become one. There he is, watering the plants."
"What!" Prasenajit gasped. 

Angulimala had completely shaved his head and wore the yellow robes of a monk. He was devoted to Buddha and listened carefully to the master's words of wisdom. 
"I could not subdue Angulimala with all my strength, and you have won him without lifting your little finger," Prasenajit marvelled. "We are grateful to you, lord."

Angulimala lived peacefully in the monastery, nursing the sick and meditating. But his past haunted him. 
One day, when Angulimala went into town to beg for his food, a woman kindly offered him rice. "You are generous, my child. May you, your mother and father be blessed."
"I have no father. He is dead," the woman sighed. "He was killed by the wicked Angulimala."
Angulimala froze. "Oh!"

Sick at heart, Angulimala walked back to the monastery, where he spent many a sleepless night. The screams of his victims echoed in his mind. *"I am an orphan. You made me one! Murderer! You killed my father. Yes, you are a murderer!"*
"Oh! No, no!" Angulimala wept in the dark. 

That Angulimala had suddenly become despondent did not escape the notice of Buddha. "Why are you so sad, my son?"
"Master, these hands of mine are stained with blood. I am a sinner without a future, without hope!"
"Why child, repentance is the only fire that is capable of burning sins already committed. You are on the right path," Buddha assured him. 

But Angulimala continued to suffer from terrible guilt. "Father, how long must Angulimala suffer? Can't you ease him of his burden?" the other monks asked Buddha. 
"All in good time," Buddha replied. 

Then one day, Buddha took Angulimala with him on his rounds. As they proceeded, they heard a woman screaming in agony by the roadside. 
"Master, someone seems to be in pain. Let us hurry," Angulimala urged. 
They found an old woman sitting with her pregnant daughter. "What's the trouble, mother?" Buddha asked. 
"My daughter is about to deliver a baby, sir. We were on our way to town, but she is dying," the old woman wept. 
"Can't you alleviate her misery, master?" Angulimala begged. 
"My son, *you* bless the woman and say: 'When I killed people, I did so out of ignorance. If I speak the truth, let the woman get well.'"
"But master...!" Angulimala stepped back in horror. "How can I, a ruthless murderer, save her?"
"Oh, sir, don't say no! Please don't! Save her," the old woman pleaded. 

With much reluctance, Angulimala obeyed his master's orders. He placed his hand on the dying woman's head. *"I don't know if I had killed with full knowledge. If I am correct in saying so, let this woman get well,"* he prayed sincerely. 
Instantly, the woman's pain eased, and she delivered a healthy baby. The old woman brought the child to them. "Sir, you blessed my daughter and saved her life. Bless her little son, too."
"Mother, merciful Buddha will bless him," Angulimala smiled humbly. 
"I want both of you to bless the child."

When the old woman went away, Buddha turned to Angulimala. "Angulimala, at least now are you convinced that you have overcome your past deeds? You have given life."
"I am, master, thanks to you."
"Son, you no longer need me. You must walk alone in the world."
"If you insist, I will," Angulimala bowed deeply. "But it is you who have given me the strength to do so. *Buddham Saranam Gacchami.*"

After parting from Buddha, Angulimala decided to go back to Shravasti alone to beg for alms. As he entered the city, a man recognized him. "Look! Did you recognise him? He is none other than Angulimala in disguise!"
The news spread instantly, and people began to panic. "Angulimala is coming! Run for your lives!"
But some youngsters mustered up their courage. "No, we shall not run away. Come, we will kill him before he kills us all."

Arming themselves with swords, sticks, and stones, a furious mob approached Angulimala. 
"Look! A child has come out to offer rice to the monster in disguise," a man yelled, seeing a little boy approach the monk. 
"He is going to strangle that child!"
But Angulimala simply knelt and kissed the child's forehead gently. 

As soon as Angulimala stepped out of the house, a stone hit him hard on the forehead. It was followed by many more. Then the mob let loose its pent-up fury, beating him mercilessly with heavy sticks. 
"Hit him hard! Give me a chance. It's my turn now!"
"What does it feel like? Why don't you fight back?" they screamed at him. 

But the former fearsome bandit did not raise a single finger to protect himself. He accepted their wrath silently. 
Severely beaten, bloody, and dying, he started crawling towards the abode of his master. 
"May you die a dog's death," the mob spat as they left him. 

When he finally dragged his broken body back to the monastery, Buddha knelt beside him. "Angulimala, my child!"
"Buddham... Saranam... Gacchaa..mi," Angulimala whispered weakly. 
"Were you angry when they beat you up?" Buddha asked softly. 
"No, father. When I killed people, I did not know what I was doing. Similarly, they too did not know what they were doing."
"Do you feel better, my child?"
"Never before have I felt better than I do now, master."
"Are you at rest now?"
"Yes, father. I am at peace with the world," Angulimala smiled. 

And Angulimala breathed his last. 
Buddha addressed the weeping monks. "Angulimala was a great soul. He conquered vice, cruelty and anger. He has attained Nirvana."
