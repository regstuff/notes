# Rana Pratap

Rajasthan in western India was the home of the valiant Rajputs. Throughout history, they repeatedly fought for the honour of the country. But the Rajputs of Chittor surpassed all in deeds of bravery and personal sacrifice. Their women were no less heroic; Chittor's Queen Karma Devi had defeated the powerful hordes of Qutab-ud-din, and Queen Padmini along with hundreds of Rajput women performed *Sati* (self-immolation) to save their honour from the invader Ala-ud-din Khilji.

The Mughals were the first invaders who succeeded in occupying Chittor. Almost all major Rajput kings had surrendered to the Mughal Emperor Akbar, but Rana Pratap, the king of Chittor, steadfastly refused. 

"I swear that I will sacrifice my very life for Chittor," Rana Pratap pledged solemnly. "We also pledge that till we attain freedom, we will not sleep on a bed but on the ground, and we will not wear fancy clothes."

His vow resonated with the people. A handful of Rana Pratap's warriors attacked a fort under Mughal occupation near Chittor. Defeating the Mughal forces, Pratap declared: "I am your true king. I order that nobody shall till the land till we attain freedom." 

When the Mughal governor in Chittor found a farmer tilling his land, he demanded to know why the man disobeyed orders. The farmer bravely replied, "I am the king of Chittor, and I have not given you the permission... your crop will finally feed the enemy." The next day, the courageous farmer was hanged by the Mughals. 

Meanwhile, in the magnificent court of Akbar, the Emperor was informed of Pratap's persistent defiance. Akbar consulted his Rajput commander, Man Singh. "A brave man like Pratap should be our friend," Akbar suggested. "Please go and ask him to stop this revolt against us."

Man Singh traveled to meet Rana Pratap, hoping to secure his friendship for the Mughal Empire. "He may be a king for you," Pratap told his minister, "but to us he is an invader, an enemy." 

When Man Singh arrived, Rana Pratap refused to eat with him. "I am sorry, but I can't eat with you," Pratap said coldly. 
"May I know why?" asked Man Singh.
"Because you have sold your honour to the enemy," Pratap replied. 
Insulted, Man Singh left, promising to meet Pratap on the battlefield. 

Soon after, a massive Mughal army led by Man Singh and Prince Salim marched toward Chittor. Rana Pratap, heavily outnumbered with only 22,000 soldiers and no cannons against Akbar's 80,000 men and sophisticated artillery, prepared for war. 

Rana Pratap wisely chose to wait for the Mughals in the narrow valley of **Haldi-Ghati**, hoping the terrain would negate the Mughal's numerical advantage. When the massive Mughal force entered the valley, the legendary Battle of Haldi-Ghati began. 

The Rajputs charged fiercely. Even Pratap's incredibly faithful horse, **Chetak**, participated in the battle, rearing up to strike the elephant of the Mughal commander, Man Singh. Pratap threw his spear at Man Singh, but the elephant moved just in time, and Man Singh was saved. 

Despite their incredible courage, the Rajputs suffered heavy losses. Rana Pratap himself was severely wounded. Seeing their king in mortal danger, Pratap's loyal friend, Mana, rushed to his rescue. In an act of supreme sacrifice, Mana placed Pratap's royal helmet on his own head, drawing the enemy's attention. The Mughal soldiers, mistaking Mana for Pratap, attacked and killed him, allowing the real Rana Pratap to be carried away to safety by his faithful, wounded horse, Chetak. 

Though Pratap survived, his family faced an incredibly hard time hiding in the jungles. They had nothing to eat but wild berries and roots. One day, when Pratap's young son was eating a dry chapati, a wild cat snatched it away. Seeing his children starve, the great Rana's resolve briefly wavered. Heartbroken, he wrote a letter of surrender to Akbar. 

When Akbar received the letter, he showed it to Prithviraj, a Rajput poet in his court who secretly admired Pratap. Prithviraj immediately wrote a powerful, inspiring poem back to Pratap, reminding him that he was the absolute last hope of Rajput freedom. 

His fiery pride reignited by the poem, Rana Pratap tore up the surrender letter and resumed his fight. With the financial help of a devoted, wealthy merchant named **Bhama Shah**, who gave his entire personal wealth to the cause, Pratap raised a powerful army of Bhil tribals. 

Using brilliant guerrilla tactics, Pratap and the Bhils fiercely attacked the Mughal forces, eventually managing to liberate vast areas including Devar, Udaipur, and Komalmir. They captured dozens of forts and drove the Mughals out of much of Mewar. 

However, despite fighting relentlessly for twenty years, the great fortress of Chittor itself remained under Mughal occupation. 

As he lay on his deathbed, old and very sick, Rana Pratap's final thoughts were still of his beloved, unliberated motherland. "How unlucky I am that I could not liberate my motherland, Chittor," he sighed. 

Thus, with his lifelong dream only partially fulfilled, the legendary Rana Pratap passed away. True to his sacred oath, till the absolute last day of his life, he strictly adhered to his vow. Even when sick and dying, he did not sleep on a comfortable bed, but on the hard ground. He set an immortal example to leaders of all times: they have no right to live in luxury when their country and their people suffer.
