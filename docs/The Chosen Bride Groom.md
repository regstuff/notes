# The Chosen Bridegroom

In the city of Ayodhya ruled King Viraketu, a just and beloved ruler under whom people felt safe. The city's leading merchant, Ratnadatta, had a daughter born to his wife Nandayanti around the same time — a lovely girl they named Ratnavati, "what a gem." She grew up graceful and beautiful, and suitors came from every quarter to ask for her hand. All of them she refused. "I don't care whether he is a pauper or a prince or a god," she told her mother flatly. "I will never marry." When Prince Rajdutt himself sent gifts and asked for her, hoping flattery might succeed where others had failed, she was no less blunt: "I hate men. I'd rather die than get married."

Her stubborn vow became the talk of the town — until a more urgent problem eclipsed the gossip. A gang of thieves had begun robbing the city every single night, and the frightened citizens went to King Viraketu for help. He posted extra sepoys and ordered every arrival and departure logged, but the robberies continued unchecked. "The thieves are very cunning, your majesty," his minister reported, showing him a peculiar shoe. "They wear special shoes that make reverse footprints." Even trained trackers were no match for it, and night after night, the king's soldiers came back empty-handed.

Determined to solve the mystery himself, Viraketu disguised himself one night — darkened his face, tied up his beard with horsehair to look different, swapped his silken robes for a rough cloak — and slipped alone into the dark streets. In the merchants' quarter he startled a lurking figure, who sprang up in fright. "Who are you?" the king demanded. "I am a thief. And you?" "Me too. Come, let's work together," the king answered smoothly, and the two "thieves" fell in together for the night, working the back streets before heading off, in thieves' cant, to "wet their whistles" at the gang's own den.

The trail led to a rocky hill outside the city, marked by hidden signals — hoots and toots answered by a dozen armed men rising from the grass — then a secret jungle path, palm prints in vermilion paste on a marked rock, and finally a hidden trapdoor leading down a bamboo ladder into an underground cave heaped with stolen wallets, weapons, and jars of wine. The king, quietly marking the exits and trail as he went, was welcomed as "a new addition to our gang" by the chief himself.

As the gang ate, drank, and dropped off to sleep one by one, an old woman serving as maid recognised the king despite his disguise. "O King Viraketu! What are you doing here? They will kill you if they recognize you. Run away," she whispered, and led him across the sleeping bodies to a secret exit — which he carefully marked on his way out. By dawn he was back in the palace, just in time to hear fresh complaints about the previous night's robbery, "the biggest yet." "Just give me another day," he told his ministers, and quietly summoned his best archers for a secret mission that night.

But the gang, waking to find their newest recruit vanished, grew suspicious. "I suspect he was a spy," one said, and when they caught and threatened the old maid-servant, she confessed everything. "It was K-King Viraketu." Rather than flee, the chief made a bold choice: "We will not run away. We will confront him." They spent the day sharpening their weapons, and that night ambushed the king's soldiers as they approached — walking, as the chief noted with satisfaction, "with their weapons down," certain the thieves were still holed up unaware in their cave.

The fighting was fierce. Viraketu himself confronted the chief in single combat, and the two matched each other blow for blow until, at last, the king cleverly disarmed his opponent. Seeing their chief defeated, the rest of the gang scattered and fled — but Viraketu had them run down and captured. The chief was bound, brought to the palace, and — on the king's orders — bathed, dressed in fine clothes, and locked up rather than killed outright. The stolen goods were recovered from the den, and the next morning the thief-chief was mounted on a camel and paraded through the city as a warning: "Behold the chief who robbed and plundered our city! He'll be executed today, in the public square."

As the procession passed the house of the merchant Ratnadatta, the commotion drew Ratnavati to her window — and despite the man's wounds and dust, she was instantly, hopelessly smitten. "Father! I want to marry that man," she told her astonished parents. "Haven't you heard? He's a bandit and is to be executed," they protested. "I don't care who he is," she said. "I have chosen him as my husband." Her father, remembering how she had turned away princes and nobles alike, could hardly believe it: "You want to marry this hardened criminal?" But Ratnavati was immovable — "Arrange to free him, father, or I'll kill myself too" — and Ratnadatta went to the palace to beg the king directly, offering all his wealth for the man's freedom. Viraketu refused outright: "He has been plundering our town. I risked my own life to capture him." Disappointed, Ratnadatta returned home with the bad news, and Ratnavati, over her parents' pleading, dressed and went to the public square herself to watch — and be seen by — the man she had chosen.

At the execution ground, word reached the bound prisoner that a woman wanted to marry him. "Marry me?" he said, not sure whether to laugh or weep at the strangeness of it, after having watched Ratnavati turn down princes for years. The execution went ahead regardless — but as the sentence was carried out and a grief-stricken Ratnavati prepared to take her own life rather than live without the man she'd chosen, Lord Shiva himself appeared before her, moved by her steadfast devotion. "I am pleased by your steadfast devotion. Ask for any boon." She asked first for her father to be blessed with many sons; granted that, Shiva pressed her to choose a boon for herself as well. "Let the man whom I have chosen as my husband be granted his life," she asked. "So be it," Shiva said. "He will henceforth lead a good life, and be forgiven his sins by King Viraketu."

With Shiva's blessing, the executed thief rose to his feet alive and unharmed, and Ratnadatta welcomed him at once: "Come, let us take our bridegroom home." When King Viraketu heard what had happened, he was quick to forgive. "You are indeed a brave man, and are blessed by Lord Shiva to become noble. I forgive you," he told the former bandit — and, rather than punish him further, appointed him commander of his own army. Ratnavati, who had refused every eligible suitor in Ayodhya for a condemned criminal seen only once from a window, lived happily ever after with the husband she alone had chosen.
