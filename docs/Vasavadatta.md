# Vasavadatta

King Pradyota of Ujjaini was a notoriously vain and proud monarch. One day, as he was strolling through his royal gardens, he asked his chief minister, "Minister, is there any king on earth who is greater than I am?"

The minister hesitated, knowing the King's temper, but decided to speak the truth. "Excuse me, Your Majesty. There is one such king. King Udayana of Kaushambi."

Pradyota was instantly displeased. His vanity deeply bruised, he issued a rash command. "Minister, I want King Udayana captured and brought before me as a prisoner!"

"It is not possible, Your Majesty," the minister sighed. "King Udayana's military is vastly superior. We can never defeat him in a direct battle."

"What has that to do with the battle?" the King demanded impatiently. 

"Your Majesty, King Udayana knows the magical art of charming elephants," the minister explained. "He can recite secret spells, play on his magical *veena* (lute), and effortlessly drive all our war elephants away in a panic."

Despite this, King Pradyota was obsessed with humiliating his rival. He pressured his minister until the clever man finally devised a cunning, deceitful plan. 

"I know, Your Majesty! You want King Udayana captured somehow, and I have thought of a plan," the minister announced a few days later. 

In the thick forest lying between the kingdoms of Ujjaini and Kaushambi, a local forest dweller spotted a massive, pure white elephant roaming among the trees. "Just the sort of elephant our King would like to own," the man thought excitedly. "I must report this."

He rushed to the court of King Udayana in Kaushambi. "Your Majesty, I have seen a magnificent white elephant in the forest!" 

King Udayana, an avid lover of elephants, was thrilled. "I must have that white elephant," he declared, immediately mounting his royal elephant and setting out with a small group of attendants to capture the rare beast. 

However, it was a trap. The "white elephant" was actually a massive, cleverly constructed wooden contraption, painted white and hollow inside, hiding King Pradyota's fiercest armed soldiers! Pradyota's spies had been secretly watching Udayana's movements. 

As Udayana approached the forest, he saw the white elephant in the distance. Eager to capture it alone, he galloped faster, leaving his attendants far behind. 

"What a beautiful animal! I am lucky," Udayana smiled, dismounting and beginning to play enchanting melodies on his veena, reciting his magical spells. 

But the wooden beast did not react. "How strange! My spells seem to have no effect on it," Udayana frowned, stepping closer. 

Suddenly, the sides of the wooden elephant burst open, and Pradyota's armed men poured out! "Capture him! Take him to our King!" they shouted, overwhelming the surprised and outnumbered Udayana before he could draw his weapons. 

When Udayana's attendants finally caught up, they found only the wooden shell and signs of a struggle. "This seems to have been a trap," Udayana's loyal minister realized grimly. "Our King has fallen into the hands of the enemy. Let us build a heavily armed stockade just outside the borders of Ujjaini and plan to free our King."

Meanwhile, Udayana was thrown into a dark prison cell in Pradyota's kingdom. After languishing there for three days, the proud King of Kaushambi angrily confronted his guards. "Your King has brought disgrace on all Kshatriyas (warriors) by using such cowardice! He ought to release me honorably or kill me in battle."

The guards reported his defiance to King Pradyota, who decided to visit his royal prisoner. 

"Very well," Pradyota sneered through the iron bars. "I will release you. But on one condition. You must teach me the secret spell of taming elephants."

Udayana, refusing to be cowed, stared back. "I will. But only if I receive due salutation from you. Isn't it customary for a pupil to pay homage to his teacher?"

Pradyota's immense pride flared up. "Do you really want me to pay homage to you? I won't! And if you don't teach me, I will have you killed!" he roared, storming out of the prison in a terrible rage. 

Back in the palace, Pradyota complained to his minister. The clever minister proposed an alternative. "What do you think of Princess Vasavadatta learning the secret of taming the elephants from King Udayana?"

"A good idea! I too have thought about it," Pradyota agreed. But his paranoia persisted. "But I must ensure that they don't see each other. They might fall in love."

Pradyota returned to the prison. "Will you teach the charm to a relative of mine?"

"Yes, why not?" Udayana agreed. "Be she hunch-backed or crippled, I will teach the one who pays homage to me."

Satisfied, Pradyota then went to his beautiful daughter, Princess Vasavadatta. "My dear, there is a leper in the prison who knows a priceless charm. Will you learn it from him for my sake?" he lied. 

"Oh father, from a leper?" Vasavadatta shuddered in disgust. 

"I know it is difficult, but you can sit behind a thick curtain. Then you will not see his repulsive face," her father suggested. Reluctantly, she agreed. 

The bizarre lessons began. Udayana sat on one side of a heavy curtain, and Vasavadatta on the other. 

"Repeat this verse exactly as I say it," Udayana instructed patiently. 

Vasavadatta tried her best, but she mispronounced a complex Sanskrit word. 

"No! Wrong again!" Udayana snapped in frustration, assuming his student was an ugly hunchback. "You villain of a hunchback! Are you deaf too?"

Furious at being insulted by a supposed leper, Vasavadatta snapped back, "You villain of a leper! How dare you call me a hunchback?" 

Shocked by her melodious, angry voice, Udayana impulsively reached out and tore the curtain aside. He was absolutely astonished to see a breathtakingly beautiful princess, and she was equally stunned to see a handsome, regal king instead of a hideous leper. 

"Who are you?" they asked in unison. 

When they explained the lies King Pradyota had told them both, their anger turned into shared amusement, and then, inevitably, into deep, passionate love. They began meeting secretly during their "lessons," desperately wanting to be together. 

Months passed. "Dear daughter, have you mastered the chant yet?" Pradyota asked impatiently. 

"Not yet, father," she lied. 

But Udayana knew their time was running out. "How long can I remain a captive?" he worried. "Besides, your father will not hesitate to kill me if he learns of our love. We must escape."

"I have a plan," Vasavadatta smiled. "Tell him you need a real elephant to put your pupil's knowledge to the test. I'll do the rest."

Pradyota eagerly agreed to this "test" and placed a swift royal elephant at his daughter's disposal. 

Udayana then made his next move. "I have only one more request. I want a city gate left open at night."

"My father may not agree to this request," Vasavadatta worried. 

"Tell him the 'leper' wants to pick a certain rare herb for charming the elephant. Tell him it is a luminous herb and can only be detected in the dark," Udayana suggested. 

Vasavadatta brilliantly convinced her father. "Very well, a gate will be left open," Pradyota agreed. However, the suspicious King secretly ordered his spies: "Listen carefully. Don't let King Udayana know that he is being followed. Capture him only if he tries to run away."

For three successive nights, Udayana mounted the elephant and rode out of the city gates. Aware of the spies trailing him in the shadows, he diligently searched the forest floor, seemingly collecting herbs, before returning peacefully to his cell. 

"He really means to collect herbs," the spies reported back, throwing off their guard. 

On the fourth night, while King Pradyota was distracted by a grand musical performance in the palace gardens, Udayana and Vasavadatta made their daring move. 

"Princess! Now is the time to flee. Be quick," Udayana whispered as he brought the elephant to her balcony. 

Vasavadatta quickly climbed aboard, carrying two heavy bags she had prepared. 

"What have you got in them?" Udayana asked as he spurred the elephant towards the open city gates. 

"This one is full of silver coins, the other has gold coins," the clever Princess replied. "You will know soon why."

Hours later, a maid discovered Vasavadatta's empty room. The alarm was raised, and a furious King Pradyota roared, "Go quickly and bring them to me, dead or alive!"

Pradyota's fastest cavalry guards mounted their horses and charged into the night, easily catching up to the slower-moving elephant on the forest road. 

Hearing the pounding hooves behind them, Udayana reached into the first bag and scattered handfuls of silver coins across the dark road. 

"What is this?" the pursuing guards shouted, reining in their horses as the moonlight caught the glittering metal. "It's silver! Let us pick it up!" Greed overcame their duty, and they stopped to gather the loot, allowing Udayana to gain precious ground. 

When the more disciplined guards finally resumed the chase and closed in again, Udayana opened the second bag. He scattered the heavy gold coins far and wide. 

"Oh! It's gold! Real gold!" even the most loyal guards gasped. Entirely forgetting their King's orders, the soldiers leaped from their horses, frantically scrambling in the dirt to grab the fortune. 

By the time the greedy soldiers realized their folly, King Udayana and Princess Vasavadatta had successfully crossed the borders out of Ujjaini and reached the safety of the heavily armed Kaushambi stockade! 

Udayana's waiting army easily overpowered any of Pradyota's exhausted soldiers who dared to cross the border. 

Safely back in his own kingdom of Kaushambi, King Udayana was given a rapturous, jubilant welcome by his loyal people. Amidst great rejoicing, grand feasts, and the blessings of the entire kingdom, Princess Vasavadatta was officially married to the brilliant King Udayana, their daring escape becoming a legend told for generations to come.
