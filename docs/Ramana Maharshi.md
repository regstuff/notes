# Ramana Maharshi

On December 30, 1879, in the small South Indian village of Tiruchuzhi, a child was born to a pious couple, Sundaram Iyer and his wife, Alagammal. It was the highly auspicious day of *Ardra Darshan*—the sacred day on which Lord Shiva is said to appear before his devotees. The child was named Venkataraman. 

Venkataraman grew up in a deeply loving and devout atmosphere. However, tragedy soon struck the happy family when Sundaram Iyer suddenly passed away. Unable to support the children on her own, Alagammal sent Venkataraman and his elder brother, Nagaswami, to live with their uncle in the city of Madurai. 

In Madurai, Venkataraman was a strong, incredibly high-spirited boy. He attended the American Mission High School and was far more interested in wrestling, swimming in the Mariamman tank, and playing sports than in his academic lessons. He was famous among his friends for his strict sense of fair play and his incredibly deep, heavy sleep—he slept so soundly that his friends could literally carry him around the compound without waking him up!

Everything changed one fateful day when a visiting relative casually mentioned that he had just come from "Arunachala." 

Venkataraman was stunned. He had heard the sacred name in hymns, but only now did he realize that Arunachala was an actual, physical place on earth—the holy town of Tiruvannamalai. The very name struck a profoundly deep, resonant chord in his soul. Shortly after, he borrowed a copy of the *Periapuranam*, a book detailing the lives of the sixty-three great Shaiva saints. He was absolutely captivated by their pure devotion, secretly wishing he could emulate them. 

Then came the defining moment of his life. On July 16, 1896, the sixteen-year-old Venkataraman was sitting entirely alone in his uncle's house when a sudden, terrifying, and overwhelming fear of death violently gripped him. 

Instead of panicking, the young boy made a brilliant, highly unusual decision: he decided to intellectually and physically investigate the experience of death itself. 

He lay flat on the floor, held his breath, and made his limbs entirely stiff. *“My body is dead,”* he thought to himself. *“It will soon be cremated and reduced to ashes. But with the death of this body, am ‘I’ dead? No! I can still feel the full force of my being. Therefore, the ‘I’ is the Spirit, which is immortal and absolutely deathless!”*

In that intense, terrifying half-hour, the playful schoolboy attained instantaneous, complete spiritual realization. 

From that day forward, Venkataraman was a completely changed person. He lost all interest in food, friends, and studies, preferring to sit in deep, silent meditation. He frequently visited the famous Madurai Meenakshi temple, standing before the idols of the sixty-three saints with tears of pure, overwhelming devotion streaming down his face. 

His elder brother, Nagaswami, grew intensely irritated by this sudden transformation. "If you want to behave like a sage, why don't you become one?" Nagaswami scolded him sharply while Venkataraman was trying to meditate over his schoolbooks. "Why do you continue to live this kind of life?"

Realizing his brother was absolutely right, Venkataraman made a firm decision. Taking three rupees from his brother's college fee money to pay for a train ticket, he left a brief, unsigned note for his family: 
*"I have set out in search of my Father and his command... Do not spend any money in search of this... Your college fees have not been paid."*

On August 29, 1896, Venkataraman left Madurai and embarked on an arduous journey to Tiruvannamalai. After running out of money, pawning his ruby earrings for a meager meal and a train ticket, and briefly fainting from sheer exhaustion, he finally reached his divine destination on September 1. 

He walked straight into the magnificent Arunachaleshwara temple. "O Father, I have come!" he whispered, completely surrendering himself. He immediately shaved his head, threw away all his money, discarded his clothes except for a simple loincloth, and sat down to meditate in the Hall of a Thousand Pillars. 

Completely lost in the bliss of the infinite, the young sage became utterly oblivious to the physical world. When mischievous local boys pelted him with stones, he silently retreated into a dark, underground cellar known as the *Patala Lingam*. He sat there for weeks in absolute, motionless trance. Vermin, ants, and scorpions bit and chewed away at his thighs, leaving festering, bleeding wounds, but Venkataraman remained completely conscious of nothing but the Divine. 

Finally, a local holy man named Seshadriswami discovered the bleeding boy. Shocked by the severity of the wounds, the temple priests gently carried the unconscious young sage out and nursed him back to health. 

Word of the incredibly young, silent, and deeply radiant ascetic quickly spread. His desperate mother, Alagammal, finally tracked him down and begged him tearfully to return home. Breaking his strict silence only to write on a piece of paper, Venkataraman gently but firmly explained that a higher power controlled their destinies and that he could not leave. Heartbroken, his mother returned home. 

Venkataraman moved into the small Virupaksha cave high up on the slopes of the sacred Arunachala hill. Devotees began to slowly gather around him, drawn by the profound, radiating peace of his absolute silence. When a great, highly educated scholar named Ganapati Muni came to him seeking the ultimate truth, Venkataraman spoke a few simple, profound words that instantly cleared the scholar's lifelong doubts. 

Deeply moved, the great scholar formally proclaimed that the young ascetic should henceforth be known to the world as **Bhagavan Sri Ramana Maharshi**. 

The Maharshi's life was a testament to absolute compassion and profound simplicity. He treated everyone—wealthy scholars, poor untouchables, and innocent children—with the exact same boundless love. He actively helped in the ashrama kitchen, expertly chopping vegetables and strictly forbidding any food to be wasted. 

His compassion extended deeply to animals. Monkeys, squirrels, peacocks, and even leopards freely roamed his ashrama. The ashrama's beloved cow, Lakshmi, would walk right up to the Maharshi for his gentle affection. He never referred to any animal as an "it," but always as a "he" or "she," recognizing the divine soul within every single living creature. 

Even when violent thieves brutally raided the ashrama one night, mercilessly beating the Maharshi with an iron bar, he remained perfectly calm and deeply compassionate. "They are completely blinded by ignorance," he told his furious, bleeding devotees. "Let us not give up our Dharma (duty) just because they have forgotten theirs."

Years later, his aging mother Alagammal returned, this time to live permanently with her beloved son. She tirelessly ran the ashrama kitchen, feeding the growing number of devotees. When she finally fell terminally ill, the Maharshi sat by her side, placed his hands on her head and chest, and personally guided her soul to ultimate *Mukti* (liberation). She was buried at the foot of the hill, and the famous **Sri Ramanasramam** was slowly built around her shrine. 

As the decades passed, the Maharshi’s profound teachings—centered purely around the simple, intense self-inquiry of *"Who am I?"*—drew thousands of seekers from all over the globe. He strongly discouraged his followers from attributing cheap miracles to him. "The ultimate truth lies within you," he always insisted. "Don't seek it outside."

In his final years, a painful, cancerous growth developed on his left elbow. Despite undergoing several agonizing, unanesthetized surgeries solely to please his desperate devotees, his condition rapidly worsened. Yet, through the excruciating, unimaginable pain, the Maharshi's face never once lost its radiant, perfectly serene smile. 

When weeping devotees begged him not to leave them, he simply smiled warmly. "Why do you grieve? I am not going anywhere. Where could I possibly go? I am right here."

On the evening of April 14, 1950, surrounded by hundreds of loudly chanting, grieving devotees, the Maharshi's breathing slowly deepened. At precisely 8:47 PM, he opened his eyes wide in a flash of divine ecstasy, shed a single tear of pure joy, and peacefully left his physical body. 

At that exact, breathtaking moment, a brilliant, incredibly luminous meteor slowly cut a bright path across the darkening sky, trailing directly behind the sacred peak of Arunachala hill. 

The Maharshi was physically gone, but true to his final words, his profound, deeply comforting presence remains forever alive, continuing to guide millions of seekers toward the ultimate peace of self-realization.
