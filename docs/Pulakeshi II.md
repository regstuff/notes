# Pulakeshi II: The King Who Defeated Emperor Harsha

In the sixth century A.D., the magnificent city of Vatapi (modern Badami in Karnataka) stood as the fortified, impregnable capital of the early Western Chalukyas. The empire, originally founded by the mighty King Pulakeshi I, was vastly expanded and enriched by his valiant son, King Keertivarman I. 

However, tragedy struck the prosperous empire when King Keertivarman fell terminally ill. His eldest son and rightful heir, Prince Pulakeshi, was only five years old. Knowing the boy was far too young to rule, the king's ministers appointed Keertivarman's younger brother, Mangalesha, as the regent to govern the empire until Pulakeshi came of age. 

Initially, Mangalesha was a dutiful regent. He affectionately raised his young nephews and focused on completing the magnificent rock-cut Vishnu temple at Vatapi that his late brother had begun. But as the years rolled by, the intoxicating taste of absolute power slowly poisoned Mangalesha's mind. 

Driven by fierce ambition, Mangalesha launched a massive military campaign, conquering numerous neighboring kingdoms and expanding the Chalukya empire even further. Drunk on his spectacular victories and the adulation of his courtiers, he began to see himself not as a mere caretaker, but as the true, undisputed Emperor. 

When Prince Pulakeshi turned twenty-three, he formally approached his uncle, expecting him to gracefully step down and hand over the reins of the kingdom. To his deep shock, Mangalesha completely ignored him, refusing to even grant him an audience. 

The horrifying truth finally dawned on the young prince. "Treason!" Pulakeshi realized furiously. "This is a betrayal of my father and of me. He plans to keep the throne for himself and pass it on to his own son!"

Knowing that his life was in immediate danger in the hostile court, Pulakeshi secretly fled Vatapi under the cover of darkness. He roamed the vast kingdom, appealing directly to the loyal citizens and minor chieftains who still vividly remembered the glory and justice of his great father, Keertivarman. 

Before long, the charismatic Pulakeshi had successfully gathered a massive, fiercely loyal army of supporters. He confidently marched his forces back toward Vatapi to claim his rightful birthright. 

Mangalesha was furious at the rebellion and rode out with the royal army to crush his nephew. A bitter, bloody civil war ensued. On the battlefield, uncle and nephew finally met face to face. During the fierce combat, Mangalesha was struck down and killed. 

Amidst great rejoicing, the rightful heir ascended the throne as Emperor Pulakeshi II. However, the crown he inherited was made of thorns. The brutal civil war had severely crippled the empire, and sensing weakness, enemies surrounded the Chalukyas on all sides. Several vassal kings openly revolted. 

Pulakeshi II wasted no time. Moving with terrifying speed and brilliant military strategy, he marched against the two primary rebel chiefs, Appayika and Govinda. In a decisive, bloody battle fought on elephant backs on the banks of the Bhima river, Pulakeshi utterly crushed the rebellion. He cleverly befriended Govinda, while Appayika was forced to flee for his life. 

Having violently suppressed the internal revolts, Pulakeshi launched a breathtaking series of conquests to secure his borders. He forced the powerful Alupas into submission, completely defeated the Mauryas of Konkan, and married a Kadamba princess to cement a vital political alliance. 

But his greatest challenge lay to the north. The legendary Emperor Harshavardhana of Thaneswar, the undisputed Lord of the North, had set his greedy eyes on the wealthy southern kingdoms. Commanding a vast, seemingly invincible army, Harsha marched relentlessly toward the Chalukya borders. 

Refusing to be intimidated, Pulakeshi II marched his forces northwards and met Harsha's massive army on the banks of the Narmada river. 

A monumental, earth-shaking battle ensued. Despite Harsha personally leading his famous, terrifying elephant battalion, the Chalukya forces fought with unimaginable ferocity. For the very first time in his legendary military career, Emperor Harsha was completely, devastatingly routed. He was forced to order a humiliating retreat, leaving his dreams of conquering the South permanently shattered on the banks of the Narmada. 

Triumphant, Pulakeshi II returned to Vatapi, assuming the grand, imperial title of *Parameshvara* (Supreme Lord). Over the next few years, he expanded his empire all the way to the eastern coast, easily overrunning Kosala, Kalinga, Pishtapura, and Vengi. 

For the next fifteen years, Emperor Pulakeshi II ruled his vast empire in glorious peace. Art, literature, and magnificent architecture flourished under his generous patronage. His court poet, the brilliant Jaina scholar Ravikeerti, composed the famous *Aihole Prashasti* in his honor. Pulakeshi's fame spread so far that he even exchanged rich gifts and ambassadors with King Khusru II of distant Persia. 

But peace was not destined to last forever. To the deep south, the powerful Pallava King, Mahendravarman, began aggressively preparing for war, posing a direct threat to the Chalukya territories in Vengi. 

Deciding to strike first, Pulakeshi marched his strong army straight toward the Pallava capital of Kancheepuram. At the battle of Pullalur, Pulakeshi inflicted a crushing, humiliating defeat upon Mahendravarman, forcing the Pallava king to hastily withdraw from the battlefield. Deeply traumatized by the massive defeat, Mahendravarman died a broken-hearted man shortly after. 

But Pulakeshi had drastically underestimated the depth of the Pallavas' desire for revenge. Mahendravarman’s son, the brilliant and furious Narasimhavarman, immediately raised a massive, fresh army. 

Learning from his father's mistakes, Narasimhavarman did not wait for the Chalukyas to march south. Instead, he launched a series of shockingly quick, entirely unexpected attacks, penetrating deep into Chalukya territory before Pulakeshi could properly mobilize his main forces. 

Before Pulakeshi realized the sheer scale of the danger, the vengeful Pallava army had reached the very gates of Vatapi! 

Caught completely unawares and vastly outnumbered, Pulakeshi II threw open the gates of his beloved capital and bravely led his men out to fight for their honor and their survival. 

The battle was a horrific slaughter. Driven by a burning desire to settle an old score, Narasimhavarman kept up relentless, crushing pressure on the defending Chalukyas. Slowly, inevitably, Pulakeshi’s men began to break and flee. 

Surrounded by his falling soldiers, the great Emperor Pulakeshi II sat upon his warhorse, realizing with the agonizing force of a thunderbolt that the battle was lost, and his glorious capital was doomed to fall. 

After this tragic, devastating defeat, the mighty Pulakeshi II completely disappears from the pages of history. The great stone inscriptions and Ravikeerti’s proud *Aihole Prashasti*, which had so vividly detailed his glorious triumphs, suddenly become completely, hauntingly silent, as if refusing to record the tragic, heartbreaking end of one of India's greatest emperors.
