# The Celestial Necklace

King Shrenika of Rajagriha was a pious king who had done many good deeds. One day a Deva appeared before him and rewarded him with a necklace of precious stones — but warned him to treat it with care, "for if it breaks, the man who repairs it will die at once."

Shrenika gave the necklace to his favourite wife, Queen Chellana, who wore it every day, delighted by its heavenly shine. But one morning, as she was about to put it on, the string gave way. Her maid carefully gathered up the scattered stones, and when the king was informed, he was deeply disturbed — not merely irritated, as he let his wife believe. "I don't think we can have your necklace repaired," he told her, and finally admitted why: whoever restrung it was fated to die the moment the work was done. Chellana was unmoved. "I must have my necklace. I can't do without it!"

Unwilling to risk any of his subjects' lives, yet cornered by his wife and his own minister Abhay Kumar into offering some solution, Shrenika reluctantly issued a proclamation: one lakh of gold coins for stringing the queen's necklace — with the fatal condition disclosed plainly. Volunteers came forward eagerly at the mention of gold, only to withdraw the moment they heard the fine print. At last an old, poor goldsmith, near the end of his life anyway, accepted. "If I die to earn the reward, my sons will benefit," he reasoned, over his sons' pleas that his life was worth more to them than the money.

Alone in his workshop, the goldsmith found the stones' holes far too small for any needle — small enough, he realised, for only an ant to pass through. He dipped one end of a string in honey, laid it beside the row of stones, and let a passing ant drag the honeyed thread through hole after hole. His plan worked perfectly. The moment he fastened the clasp on the finished necklace and lifted it to admire his handiwork, he fell dead, exactly as fated. His four sons, grieving, remembered his last instructions and carried the finished necklace to the king to collect the rest of the reward.

King Shrenika turned suspicious the moment he learned the goldsmith himself hadn't come. "How do I know you are really his sons? You may have stolen the necklace!" he snapped, and threw them out rather than pay — quietly relieved, in truth, to avoid parting with the gold at all.

Years later, reborn as a monkey after his death, the old goldsmith happened to be sitting in a tree when he overheard his own sons — now grown men — passing below, still lamenting that the king had cheated them of the reward he'd have wanted them to have. The memory of his past life came flooding back. "The king has no right to the necklace till he pays for the work. I must get hold of it," he resolved, and made his way to the palace grounds, where he waited by the bathing tank until Queen Chellana left her necklace with her maid Kapila and went to bathe. The monkey swooped down, deftly lifted the necklace without the maid noticing a thing, and carried it back to his old family home, tossing it in through the window to his startled sons.

"Don't be alarmed. It is I, your father. I was born again in this form," the monkey explained, and told them to keep the necklace hidden until the king made good on his debt. The sons, though frightened, buried it beneath a bed for safekeeping.

When the theft was discovered, King Shrenika ordered a citywide search that turned up nothing — and his minister Abhay Kumar noted shrewdly that whoever took it clearly had no intention of selling anything so recognisable; their motive must be something else entirely. With no leads, the king proclaimed a death sentence for the necklace's thief, unless the culprit came forward and surrendered it, in which case he would be fully pardoned.

Hearing this, the monkey rushed to stop his sons from digging the necklace back up in fright. "No, dear sons. Don't return it. Give me the necklace. I'll take care of this matter," he told them, and carried it off to hide in the hollow of a tree, moving only under cover of darkness. At dusk, near a temple, he watched the renowned ascetic Acharya Suhasti emerge and stand lost in deep meditation beneath the same tree. Struck by an idea — where could the necklace be safer than around the neck of so honest a man? — the monkey slipped down, placed the necklace on the meditating ascetic, and left satisfied.

That same night, the king's minister Abhay Kumar happened to be at the temple, keeping vigil with Suhasti's disciples. One by one, as each disciple finished a watch attending the ascetic through the night and returned, he spoke only two strange words instead of the usual greeting: "Fear exists," then "Great fear," then "Extreme fear," each recalling, when asked, an episode of fear from his life before becoming a monk. By morning, as the last disciple finished his tale and Abhay Kumar made his way out past Suhasti himself, he spotted the queen's necklace gleaming around the meditating ascetic's neck — and suddenly understood the disciples' unease: for men who had renounced the world, the mere presence of wealth nearby was the closest thing to fear they had left to feel, since wealth is the root of every worldly evil. Suhasti himself remained utterly calm and untouched by it, having achieved complete detachment — proof enough to Abhay Kumar that the sage could never have stolen it himself. He gently lifted the necklace from the unaware ascetic's neck and returned it to the king.

"There was no thief, King. I found it round the neck of Acharya Suhasti," Abhay Kumar reported. Baffled — the pure sage could never have taken it — the king and his minister puzzled over the mystery until Abhay Kumar connected the threads: the old goldsmith had died exactly when the necklace was repaired, which was why he'd never returned for the rest of the reward; and someone, it seemed, wanted the king to recognise the injustice done to that goldsmith's sons.

Struck by the truth of it, Shrenika reflected that he had let his own reluctance to pay masquerade as prudence — and that a king who fails his subjects in their hardship, however petty the sum, fails his duty as their father. He summoned the goldsmith's four sons at once, apologised, and paid them the reward in full. "Not for nothing did the Deva gift him with the Celestial Necklace," the sons said, satisfied that the king had, in the end, done right by them. Watching from a nearby tree as his sons left the palace happy and provided for, the old goldsmith, his mission as a father finally complete, looked on with quiet satisfaction — while in the palace, Queen Chellana rejoiced simply to have her beautiful necklace back where it belonged.
