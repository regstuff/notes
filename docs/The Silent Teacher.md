# The Silent Teacher

Brahmadatta, the King of Varanasi, had always been a loving husband. But recently, he felt even more concerned about his Queen's happiness and well-being. After years of longing for an heir, they were at last going to have a child.

"They say that only a happy mother will bear a healthy baby," the King told her. "So... ours shall be the healthiest one ever born. I have never felt happier or healthier. But... is something troubling you?"

The Queen sighed softly. "I have just one longing not fulfilled."

"What is it?" the King asked.

"I long to get away for a while to the peace and quiet of the forest. I wish to have a son who will rule the world... not one who will give it up."

The King smiled understandingly. "I understand. You don't want a son who will renounce the world! What are you waiting for? Let's go to the forest!"

The royal couple spent a peaceful time in the forest. Soon, it was time to return. 

"Shall we go back?" the King asked.

"Y...es," the Queen replied reluctantly. As they made their way back to the city, they passed a beautiful, serene lake. "How still and calm that lake looks!" she remarked. "How I'd love to absorb some of its serenity!"

That wish too, the King soon fulfilled, taking her on a boat ride across the tranquil waters. And in the silence of that lake, a beautiful son was born to them. As he was born on the lake, he was named Abja.

Abja did everything a child was expected to do, but as he grew, it became apparent that he made no attempt to stand up. His nurse tried propping him against a wall, but he simply fell over. 

The King called in a renowned physician, but the prince did not respond to his treatment. 

"Aren't you disappointed that our only son is a cripple?" the King asked the Queen one day.

"No, I love him as he is," she replied warmly. 

"I'm glad," the King said. "Because I have decided that he shall succeed me... even though he is handicapped."

But the King was in for another shock. A few weeks later, his minister approached him. "Maharaj, could you ask a physician to examine the prince? Children of his age speak fluently. And the prince has not uttered even one word."

The King turned to the Queen. "Is that true?"

"Yes," she admitted.

"A crippled king, people may accept," the King worried. "But will they accept a dumb one?"

The King began to try new physicians. One after another they came, but they all went away without being able to suggest a remedy. 

"What shall we do now?" the King asked in despair. "We should treat him like a normal child. Give him a good education..."

"What can a dumb child learn?" the Queen asked softly.

The commoners too had their doubts. "What do you think of our dumb prince?" one man asked another. 

"Well... some mute people say more by their silence than we who have the power of speech," his friend replied. "But we do not understand them."

"That too is a handicap!" a third man pointed out. 

Meanwhile, the Queen engaged scholars to read the books of wisdom to the mute prince. 

When Prince Abja was sixteen, a celebrated physician from a far-off land came to Varanasi. 

"I have requested the learned man to examine our son," the King told his wife.

"Every physician tries to find out what our son *cannot* do," the Queen sighed. "They are not interested in what he *can* do."

"What can one who cannot walk and cannot talk do?" the King scoffed. 

"He can talk," the Queen smiled gently. "He speaks to me... with his eyes. And what they say fills me with peace... like the silent forest... the still lake..."

Just then, a guard announced the arrival of the learned physician. The King led him to the prince. The physician took the boy's hand, feeling his pulse. "Breathe in... hold your breath... breathe out..." he instructed. 

Then he looked the prince in the eye. "Now answer me. What is your name? Who are you? Can't you speak?"

The prince remained perfectly silent. 

"Well. That's all," the physician said. "Now open your mouth." He carefully examined the boy's throat. 

Later, the physician spoke to the King in private. "He is neither lame... nor dumb. Are you sure?"

The King was astounded. "The prince is only pretending to be lame and dumb?"

"Yes. He can hear all right, and there is nothing wrong with his organs of speech, or his spine, or his legs!"

"Then why...?"

"If you want to see him walk and talk, you must do as I tell you. Listen carefully..." the physician whispered his plan. 

The King went to the Queen. "That's what the physician recommended. You must not stop me. I'll tell you why. I have seen quite a few cases of this sort. I'll be doing it for his own good."

The Queen remained quiet. 

"Why are you quiet? Why don't you say something?" the King demanded. 

"Because I know the peace of the mute," she replied. 

"What do you mean? I don't understand."

"You wouldn't. If you understood, you would not try so hard to make our son talk. You'd try to listen... to his silence. Please! Must you always talk in riddles?"

The following day, the King convened an open assembly and ordered the prince to be brought in. 

"Prince Abja," the King announced sternly in front of his courtiers, "the man who sits on this throne after me has to rule a vast kingdom. Since you can neither walk nor speak, you cannot rule either. So I have ordered the executioners to bury you alive."

The dumb prince remained perfectly silent. And not a single voice in the entire court spoke out in protest. 

The executioners lifted the prince, put him on a wooden cart, and drove away towards the forest. 

On the way, the prince suddenly opened his mouth. "Is this city of Varanasi deserted or inhabited?"

The executioners jumped in shock. "Eh?! Did you... did you speak?" 

Realizing what had happened, they rejoiced. "It's the prince! The prince spoke! Let's take him back to the King."

They turned the cart around and sped back to the palace. "Maharaj, the prince spoke!" they announced. 

"My son!" the King cried happily. The executioners repeated what the prince had uttered. 

"I am so glad! You will speak from today, won't you?" the King asked the boy. 

But the prince made no answer. He had returned to his silence. 

Frustrated, the King commanded, "Take him away."

The executioners took the prince back towards the forest. On the way, they passed a funeral procession. The prince looked at the procession and asked clearly, "Is this corpse that of a dead man or a living man?"

"Eh! The prince spoke again!" the executioners gasped. Once again, they took the prince back to the King. 

"Is it true that you spoke again?" the King demanded. 

There was no answer. 

Furious, the King roared, "Take him away... and bury him alive!"

The reluctant executioners took the prince with them. When they reached the deep forest, they got down and began to dig a pit. 

When the grave was ready, one of them turned to the boy. "We have to bury you... alive. We hate doing it. But we have to obey the King's orders. Why don't you walk and speak and save us from committing this crime?"

For a moment, there was silence. 

Then, the prince spoke. "All right. I'll walk back to the palace and speak to the King... provided he agrees to grant me a wish."

"He'll give you anything. You have only to ask," the executioner pleaded. He sent his companion back to the palace. "Go to the King and let him say he agrees."

The King, upon hearing the news, readily agreed. "Why one, I'll grant him a thousand wishes! Go, tell him I accept his condition."

He turned to his Queen, triumphant. "You have agreed to grant him his wish, without knowing what it is. It will be the throne... kingship... power... money! What else could a young prince want?"

The city was decorated, and the citizens stood on either side of the road to see the "lame" prince walk home. 

"Here he comes!" they cheered as Prince Abja walked gracefully through the city gates and into the palace. 

"My son!" the King beamed. "What do you want? Let me hear you speak."

The prince bowed respectfully. "I seek your permission... to become an ascetic."

"NO!" the King shouted, horrified. "Have I longed to hear you speak only for this? NO! NO! NO!"

"You have given your word," the Queen reminded him softly. 

"Heartless woman!" the King yelled. "How can you part so easily with a son you claim to love?"

"Love is freedom, my lord. Let him go."

The King turned to his son, bewildered. "But why didn't you speak all these years?"

The prince looked at his mother, who answered for him. "He did speak! Only the language he used was silence. Silence sometimes is more eloquent than all the loud speeches and declarations of orators. Especially if the message one wants to spread is one of peace and love."

"And why did you not walk?" the King asked. 

"He did not need to go anywhere. He had arrived," the Queen replied. 

"Then why should he choose to go away from us?" the King demanded. 

"To free us from the fetters that bind us to him," she said. 

The King turned back to the prince. "The first time you spoke, you asked whether Varanasi was deserted or inhabited. What did you mean by that question?"

"You ordered my execution in an open assembly," the prince explained gently. "No one in that assembly protested. When a city has no one to question injustice, it is as good as deserted."

The King lowered his head. "I understand."

"And the second time," the King continued, "you wanted to know whether the corpse you saw was that of a living man or a dead man. What did that mean?"

"A man who does good deeds lives in the memory of men even though his body is dead. So his corpse is the corpse of a living man. Whereas a man who harms others is as good as dead even while he lives. So his corpse is the corpse of a dead man."

The King looked at his son with newfound reverence. "My son, you are wise beyond your years. I grant you your wish."

"Salutations to you, O teacher," the courtiers chanted, bowing to the young ascetic. 

The prince discarded his princely robes and fell at the feet of his parents to seek their blessings. Then he rose, turned his back on the royal life, and walked away into the silent forest.
