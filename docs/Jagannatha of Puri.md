# Jagannatha of Puri: Dreamt by a King, Sculpted by the Gods

Long ago, King Indradyumna of the Sun Dynasty ruled over Utkal (ancient Orissa). Though he had conquered many lands and won great fame, he yearned to do something truly wonderful: to build the most beautiful temple on earth. However, he needed an equally beautiful image of God to enshrine in it. 

His question was answered in a dream. A divine voice told him to find Nilachala hill, where in a cave he would discover the most beautiful image ever seen of Nilamadhav - the blue-coloured Krishna. The next day, he sent his trusted officers to search for Nilachala. 

Among them was a young man named Vidyapati. He combed the jungles tirelessly but found no trace of Nilachala. Lost, exhausted, and out of provisions, Vidyapati staggered up to a tree. He was saved by a mysterious little cowherd who gave him warm, sweet milk. When Vidyapati asked about Nilachala, the boy replied, "I know where it is, but I won't tell you. Search with faith and sincerity and I'm sure you'll find it!" before vanishing. Vidyapati wondered if the boy was Lord Nilamadhav himself. 

Continuing his search, Vidyapati met a Savara tribal girl named Lalita, daughter of the chieftain Vishvavasu. He stayed in their village as a guest, and soon, he and Lalita fell in love and married. Vidyapati noticed that his father-in-law left early every morning and returned at dusk. When he asked Lalita, she revealed that Vishvavasu went to a cave on Nilachala hill to worship Lord Nilamadhav. 

Vidyapati pleaded to be taken there. Vishvavasu reluctantly agreed but insisted on blindfolding him. Cleverly, Vidyapati secretly took a handful of mustard seeds from the kitchen and dropped them along the path as they walked. At the cave, his blindfold was removed briefly, and Vidyapati was awestruck by the beautiful blue image of Nilamadhav. 

When the monsoon rains arrived, the mustard seeds sprouted into a path of yellow flowers. Vidyapati followed the trail to the cave, confirming the location for his king. He then returned to Utkal and informed King Indradyumna. 

Overjoyed, Indradyumna set out with his queen, Gundicha Devi, and Vidyapati to bring Nilamadhav back. On the way, the king proudly declared that he would take Nilamadhav away from the "selfish" Vishvavasu and make the Lord his alone. Gundicha warned him against such arrogant and possessive thoughts, reminding him that God belongs to everyone. 

Her warning was prophetic. Because of the king's unworthy thoughts, a furious sandstorm arose, burying Nilachala hill, the cave, and the image. When Indradyumna arrived, he found only sand. Believing Vishvavasu had hidden the image, the angry king had the chieftain bound as a prisoner. 

Devastated by the loss, Indradyumna wept until he fell asleep. In a dream, the Lord told him that he would not appear as Nilamadhav again, but would come as a *Daaru* (a sacred log of wood) floating in the sea. 

The next morning, they found a massive log in the sea bearing the symbols of Vishnu (conch, wheel, mace, and lotus). However, despite harnessing elephants, horses, and many men, the log would not budge. Desperate, Indradyumna prayed to Vishnu. A voice told him that he must free his beloved devotee Vishvavasu; only if the two rival devotees lifted the log together would it move. Indradyumna sought forgiveness, embraced Vishvavasu, and together, the two men easily lifted the massive log out of the sea. 

Indradyumna decided to have the *Daaru* carved into a replica of Nilamadhav. However, every expert craftsman failed; their finest axe-blades broke upon striking the wood. Finally, an old man appeared—believed to be the divine architect Vishvakarma in disguise. He agreed to carve the image on one condition: he must be locked in a room for fifteen days and not be disturbed. 

The old man began his work. After ten days, when the sound of carving stopped, Queen Gundicha became anxious, fearing the old man might be dying of starvation. She pleaded with the king to open the door. Reluctantly, Indradyumna broke the seal. They found the room empty, the old man vanished, and three incomplete wooden images left behind. 

Sage Narada then appeared, explaining that it was Lord Vishnu's will that the images remain in this form. Accepting this, Indradyumna proceeded to build a magnificent temple on the seashore. A huge tank was also created nearby, which became known as the Indradyumna tank. 

To consecrate the grand temple, Indradyumna and Narada travelled to Brahmaloka to invite Lord Brahma. They had to wait a while as Brahma was meditating. When Brahma agreed to come, Narada suddenly realised a terrible truth: one day in Brahmaloka equals thousands of years on earth! 

By the time Indradyumna returned, thousands of years had passed. His queen, Vidyapati, and Vishvavasu were long gone. The temple had been buried under sand and recently excavated by the current ruler of Utkal, King Gala Madhav, who claimed it as his own. 

Indradyumna tried to reclaim his temple, but Gala Madhav thought he was a madman. "Where is the proof that this is your temple?" Gala Madhav demanded. 
An ancient tortoise named Akupar rose from the Indradyumna tank and testified that he had lived there for centuries and remembered Indradyumna building the temple. Lord Brahma then appeared, confirming the truth. Gala Madhav asked for forgiveness. 

The incomplete images, which had also been buried and forgotten, were found exactly where Vishvakarma had left them. Brahma consecrated the temple and enshrined the images. 

Brahma then offered Indradyumna a boon. "My lord, let no one claim this temple as his after I die. Henceforth, this temple shall belong to all mankind," the selfless king requested. 

Pleased with this noble gesture, Brahma declared, "As you have given over this temple to the people of the world, it shall be known as the shrine of Jagannatha - Lord of the Universe." 

Indradyumna's devotion earned him a place in heaven, and the memories of his queen Gundicha, Vidyapati, Lalita, and Vishvavasu are honoured to this day in the magnificent chariot festival of Jagannatha, the Ratha Yatra in Puri, Orissa.
