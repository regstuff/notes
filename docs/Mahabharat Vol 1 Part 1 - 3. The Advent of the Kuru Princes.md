# The Advent of the Kuru Princes

Following his marriage, King Pandu embarked on a massive military campaign, brilliantly expanding the Kuru empire. He conquered the Dasharnas, defeated the arrogant King of Magadha, vanquished the Videhas, and subdued the kingdoms of Kashi, Suhma, and Pundra. Returning to Hastinapura with immense wealth and glory, Pandu established himself as the undisputed sovereign of the land. 

Having secured the empire, Pandu handed over the administration to his blind brother Dhritarashtra and the wise Bheeshma. Seeking rest and recreation, Pandu retreated to the serene slopes of the southern Himalayas for an extended hunting expedition, accompanied by his two wives, Kunti and Madri. 

One spring day, while hunting in the dense forest, Pandu spotted a deer engaged in the act of mating and shot it down with a well-aimed arrow. But as the creature fell, it revealed itself to be a powerful sage in disguise. 
With its dying breath, the deer-sage placed a terrible curse upon the king: "As you have killed me at such a time, the same fate shall befall you. You shall die the moment you touch your wife in desire!"

Shocked and stricken with grief, Pandu renounced his royal life immediately. He ordered his attendants back to Hastinapura and informed his wives that he would now live as a wandering, celibate ascetic. Devoted to their husband, Kunti and Madri refused to leave him, vowing to share his austere life in the forest. 

When the royal attendants returned to Hastinapura with the tragic news, Dhritarashtra was deeply disturbed by his brother's fate. Meanwhile, his own wife, Queen Gandhari, had been pregnant for two long years following a boon from Sage Vyasa promising her a hundred sons. Desperate and impatient, she struck her womb in frustration and delivered a hard, lifeless lump of flesh. 

Sage Vyasa immediately appeared and intervened. He sprinkled holy water on the mass, divided it into a hundred and one parts, and placed them in sealed pots of ghee. In due course, from the first pot emerged Duryodhana, braying loudly like a donkey as terrifying omens—howling beasts and raging fires—swept the land. 
Vidura, sensing the evil portents, advised Dhritarashtra to abandon the child to save the dynasty. "This child shall be the ruin of your race," Vidura warned. But Dhritarashtra's blind love for his firstborn son deafened him to the wise counsel. Soon, the remaining ninety-nine sons and one daughter, Duhshala, were born.

Meanwhile, high in the Shatashringa mountains, Pandu lamented his inability to father heirs. He urged Kunti to use the ancient practice of *Niyoga* to bear children through holy sages. 
Kunti hesitated, then revealed a profound secret. "In my maidenhood, I served the irritable Sage Durvasa so well that he granted me a powerful mantra," she explained. "With it, I can summon any deity to bless me with a child."

Overjoyed, Pandu urged her to use the mantra immediately. At his command, Kunti invoked Dharma, the god of righteousness. By his grace, Yudhishthira was born—a prince destined to be just, honest, and the future king. 
Next, Kunti invoked Vayu, the mighty wind god, and gave birth to Bheema, a child of matchless, earth-shattering strength. 
Finally, Pandu asked her to invoke Indra, the king of gods. Kunti chanted the mantra, and Arjuna was born—a prince who would become invincible in battle and the greatest archer of his age. 

(Kunti secretly remembered that years ago, before her marriage, she had carelessly tested the mantra by invoking Surya, the sun god. She had given birth to a radiant son clad in divine armor, but out of fear of social stigma, she had tearfully abandoned the infant in a river. That boy, found and raised by a charioteer, grew up to be the mighty warrior Karna). 

Seeing Kunti blessed with three glorious sons, Pandu asked her to share the mantra with his second wife, Madri. Madri eagerly invoked the Ashwini twins, the celestial physicians, and gave birth to the exceptionally handsome and wise twins, Nakula and Sahadeva. 

The five Pandava brothers grew up in the forest under the loving care of the sages, demonstrating their extraordinary strength and divine lineage from a very young age. 

But tragedy lurked in the tranquil woods. One intoxicating spring day, years later, Pandu found himself alone with the beautiful Madri. Overcome by the romantic beauty of the blooming forest and the sheer force of desire, he momentarily forgot the deer-sage's deadly curse and embraced her. 

Instantly, the curse took effect, and King Pandu fell dead. 

Madri’s agonizing screams echoed through the forest. When Kunti and the children rushed to the scene, they found Madri weeping inconsolably over Pandu's lifeless body. Blaming her own beauty for his death, Madri firmly resolved to join him on the funeral pyre. Despite Kunti's desperate pleas, Madri committed *Sati*, entrusting her twin sons to Kunti's care. 

With their father dead, the forest sages realized it was time for the young princes to return to their rightful home. They gently led the grieving Kunti, the five Pandava boys, and the ashes of Pandu and Madri out of the mountains. Thus began the fateful journey of the Pandavas to Hastinapura, setting the stage for the greatest epic in history.
