# Shringabhuja

King Virabhuja of Vardhamana ruled over a flourishing kingdom of happy subjects, secure in the knowledge of a wise and just rule. 
The king had a hundred wives, of whom Gunavara was the chief queen. However, the king was childless, and felt deeply unhappy about it. 

"I worry about my kingdom. Who will inherit it?" he sighed to Queen Gunavara one day. 
"It is a pity, your majesty, that not one of your hundred wives has been able to give you an heir," she comforted him.
"I care little about the others. But I wish you..."
"It is God's will, my lord."

The king, however, did not resign himself to fate. He sent for the court physician. "You know what is troubling me. Is there no way?"
"There is, your majesty," the physician smiled. "I will concoct a special magic brew which the queens must drink."
"Waste no time. Set about it immediately!" the king ordered.

The physician sent for a skilled royal hunter. "Track down a wild mountain goat. I must have one by tomorrow." Next, the royal chef was summoned. "Let your best cooks stand by. Tomorrow we prepare a special brew, dear friend." 

The next day, the queens excitedly assembled in the royal dining hall. Ayasolekha, one of the jealous queens, noticed that Chief Queen Gunavara was not there. *Good. I hope she arrives late. If she has a son, our children don't stand a chance,* she thought spitefully.

The physician announced, "My brew is ready!" The priest pronounced the blessings: "All you queens of Virabhuja—may you bear him splendid sons!" 
And the queens eagerly drank up the magic potion.

Just as the very last cup had been drained, someone gasped, "Queen Gunavara! How could we have overlooked her?"
"The king will never forgive us!" the physician panicked. 

The king walked in, waving Gunavara into the room. "My queen, you have fasted and prayed for three days. May your devotions meet with special success."
"But sir..." the physician trembled. "...there is no brew left."
"No brew left! But it was for her that all this was done! Send for the cook. He might have some left." 

As the other queens, dreading the king's anger, hurried away, the king sighed, "Gunavara, my proud one, it is too late."
While the physician waited for the cook to arrive, the king hoped, "My lord, if he has saved the creature's horns, there is hope yet."

The cook arrived, looking apologetic. "There is no brew left."
"Never mind. Have you thrown away the horns?" the physician demanded.
"I saved them. They were so majestic, like slender scythes!"
"Good! Crack them open, remove the marrow, and prepare a soup for Queen Gunavara. The son she bears, I predict, will outshine all the rest."

His prediction proved absolutely true. 
When the son was born, the king was overjoyed. "What shall we name him?"
"The horn is what gave him to us. So, we shall call him Shringabhuja," Gunavara smiled.

Shringabhuja grew up to become the unquestioned favourite of the king. The other queens, whose sons were quite ordinary, were fiercely jealous. "The king has no eyes or ears for our sons," they complained. "If we don't do something soon, they will have no share in his affections or his kingdom."
Ayasolekha took the lead. She talked to the ninety-nine other princes, spreading her jealousy like poison. "There are ninety-nine of you. If you join forces, Shringabhuja cannot win." She also vowed, "And as for Gunavara, I will see that she falls from grace."

That night, when the king visited Ayasolekha, she pretended to be worried.
"You are not your usual smiling self. What's worrying you?" he asked.
"I don't know how to say it. Promise you won't be angry with me."
"I promise. Now, what is it?"
"My lord... Queen Gunavara... today it is palace gossip. Tomorrow it will be city gossip. She is unfaithful."

"Gunavara unfaithful! I can't believe it. Who is the man?" the king demanded.
"Surakshita, the superintendent of the women's apartments."

The king stormed out in a furious turmoil. He loved Gunavara too much to ask her straight out, but Ayasolekha had successfully sown the poisonous seeds of doubt. *I must separate them,* he decided.

Surakshita was summoned. "I hear you have killed a man," the king accused him out of nowhere. "You must atone for this grievous sin. Travel to the Himalayas and wash away your sins in the waters of the holy Ganges."
"There must be some mistake! I have not killed anyone!" Surakshita protested, but he was banished anyway.

Then, the king sent for Queen Gunavara. "My dearest, the astrologer says you should be imprisoned in the dungeon for a year. Failing this, I will die and the kingdom will come to grief."
"Is that all, my lord?" the devoted queen smiled. "Prepare the dungeon and I shall go into it only too willingly."

When Shringabhuja heard the news, he was heartbroken. "Father, I can't bear to be away from mother!"
"My son, it is for the good of the kingdom—your kingdom some day," the king reasoned gently.

With Gunavara out of the way, Ayasolekha put the rest of her wicked plan into action. She instructed her son, Nirvasabhuja: "Watch out for an opportunity. See that the king is really displeased with him."

The opportunity soon arose. That evening, the brothers were hunting when a magnificent crane flew low over them. A passing mendicant approached them. "That is no ordinary crane. It is Agnishikha, the fearsome rakshasa (demon) king. If you shoot him, you will be doing the world a good turn."

The ninety-nine princes shot at the bird one by one, but they all missed. The mendicant then pointed to Shringabhuja. "That young brother of yours can do it, I'm sure."

Nirvasabhuja seethed with jealousy. *Even this wandering mendicant singles him out!* Then, a vicious idea struck him.
"Wait, Shringabhuja," Nirvasabhuja said smoothly. "Get father's prized golden arrow. It is beautifully balanced and flies straight to the mark."
"I will," Shringabhuja agreed.

*That should fix him,* Nirvasabhuja smirked to himself. *He will hit the bird, which will disappear with father's arrow. And then he will have to face father's wrath.*

The unsuspecting Shringabhuja returned with the golden arrow, took careful aim, and shot the crane. The wounded bird shrieked and flew away, carrying the golden arrow with it.
"The crane has flown away with it!" Shringabhuja gasped.
"What do we do? We dare not face our father without his golden arrow. You had better bring the arrow back," the brothers told him sternly.

"Never fear. I will not rest till I do so," Shringabhuja promised. He immediately set off, following the clear trail of blood drops left by the wounded crane. 

The trail stopped suddenly, and Shringabhuja found himself in a magical garden city amidst a dense, glowing jungle. "This is fairyland!" he marvelled. 
Suddenly, out of the evening mists, a breathtakingly beautiful maiden appeared. *She must be a woodland spirit,* he thought in awe. *She's too perfect to be mortal.*

"Where am I? Who are you? What brings you here?" he asked.
"You are in Dhumapura," she replied. "I am Rupashikha, daughter of Agnishikha."
"Agnishikha! The very rakshasa who, in the form of a crane, I have just wounded!"

"I know of your extraordinary skill in archery," she smiled gently. "My father is not an easy target. The arrow is with me—I took a fancy to it."
The evening wore on as Shringabhuja told her all that had happened at the palace. "My father has been nursed by magic hands and is very well," she assured him. 
"But I must have the arrow back."
"You will, in good time. But we must first get married. I want you to be my husband," she declared boldly.
"My life, my fortunes, are yours," he agreed happily.
"But my dear Shringabhuja, my father is an old, wily rakshasa and needs to be carefully tackled. You will soon see."

Rupashikha went straight to her fearsome father. "Father, I have met a wonderful man. I must marry him."
"That is strange, coming from a rakshasi," he grumbled. "You should be wanting to eat him."
"I must test him first," she insisted.

When Shringabhuja was brought before him, the rakshasa smiled wickedly. "My daughter is yours, if you promise never to disobey me. Go, bathe, change, and be back in an hour."
As Shringabhuja left, the rakshasa told his daughter, "Rupashikha, bring your ninety-nine sisters here."

Rupashikha hurried out and caught up with Shringabhuja. "I know what father has in mind. When you return, there will be a hundred women looking exactly alike. You will be asked to single me out."
"Whatever will I do?"
"Just look out for the one with a garland over her ears. That will be me."

Shringabhuja easily passed the first test. But the rakshasa had another impossible task up his sleeve. "Take this lot of sesame seeds—all hundred bags of them—and sow them in the massive field outside the town."
"Yes, sir," Shringabhuja agreed.

After he spent all day sowing the field, he returned exhausted. 
"I've changed my mind," the rakshasa grinned maliciously. "Go, collect all the sown seeds and pile them up again perfectly in my granary."

Rupashikha came to his rescue once again. Using her magic, she commanded, "Emerge, you armies of ants, and set to work!" 
Millions of ants scurried about, perfectly collecting every single seed. 

When Shringabhuja produced the filled bags before the rakshasa, Agnishikha was stunned. *He is extraordinary. But a few more tasks will really test him.*
"Just one more errand," the rakshasa said casually. "My brother, Dhumashikha, lives in an abandoned Shiva temple, some miles from here. Go and invite him to your wedding tomorrow."

Rupashikha warned Shringabhuja, "My uncle will just try to kill and eat you! Here—take this magic earth, this magic water, these thorns, and this smouldering faggot. Take my magic horse. Just keep throwing these behind you one by one, each time my uncle catches up with you."

Shringabhuja rode the magic horse with the speed of wind. Soon, the deserted temple loomed before him, dark and menacing. "Dhumashikha! Your brother invites you to the wedding of his daughter, Rupashikha!" Shringabhuja shouted.

Then, turning his horse, he fled, urging it to go faster. A massive, terrifying demon burst from the temple and chased after him with incredible speed. 
"There comes the old demon! Let me throw the earth in his path," Shringabhuja yelled, throwing the magical soil. 

Instantly, the earth grew into a massive mountain, blocking the demon's path. 
A little farther on, the demon broke through and caught up again. "There's life in the old devil yet!" Shringabhuja sprinkled the magic water behind him. It instantly became a huge, swirling, uncrossable river. 

Twice more he blocked the relentless demon's path—once with the thorns, which grew into an impenetrable, tearing jungle, and finally with the smouldering faggot, which set the entire surrounding forest ablaze in a magical fire. 

"I'm going back," the exhausted demon finally gasped. "This human being is too much hard work. I'll just wait for another one to come along."
And he went back, leaving Shringabhuja free to return triumphant.

When he arrived, Agnishikha was stunned. "This impossible man! How did he escape my brother? Well done. Now, you may marry my daughter."

The next day they were married. A month passed in bliss, but Shringabhuja knew he had to return. "Rupashikha, we must get back to Vardhamana."
"That means another battle of wits with my father," she smiled. "Just don't announce your intentions. We'll have to make a quick and quiet exit into the forest."

They packed quickly. "Have you taken the golden arrow?" she asked.
"Yes—and all my jewels."
As they rode out, Rupashikha looked up. "There comes my father on his flying horse. I can feel the air rustle. What now?"

Using her immense powers, she made Shringabhuja and their horse completely invisible, and disguised herself as a simple woodcutter. She joined another woodcutter working nearby.
When Agnishikha landed, he angrily asked the woodcutters, "Did you see a young couple pass this way?"
"We saw no one," Rupashikha answered in a gruff voice. "We have been cutting wood all day—for King Agnishikha's funeral pyre."
"What! Me, dead?" the foolish rakshasa gasped. "I must rush home to check!" 

He flew back in a panic. When he realized he had been tricked, he flew out again. This time, Rupashikha disguised herself as a weeping royal messenger. 
"Did you see a man and a woman go by?" Agnishikha demanded.
"No, but then I am so full of my own sorrow I don't see anything," Rupashikha sobbed. "I'm carrying a terrible message—King Agnishikha is ill and dying!"
"I must hurry back and find out! Am I ill? Am I dying?" the foolish demon panicked again.

This last adventure completely exhausted the old rakshasa. "Let the pair go," he sighed tiredly when he got home. "I'm tired. And I think my people are laughing at me. I wonder what is so funny. The fools!"

At Vardhamana, Shringabhuja arrived triumphantly with the golden arrow and his beautiful, magical bride. Everything that had happened with his brothers was revealed to the king.
"Banish my other sons!" the furious king ordered.
"Father, forgive them," Shringabhuja pleaded gracefully. 

Then, Shringabhuja frowned. "I suspect my mother's imprisonment was also a wicked plot by Queen Ayasolekha. I'll soon find out."
He advised his father, "Tell Ayasolekha you're spending the evening with her."

That night, the king heavily plied Ayasolekha with wine. "Come, drink, my queen." 
Soon she was fast asleep, and mumbling her deepest secrets. "Gunavara... if you were not in that dungeon, would the king be here? Aha! The witch."

The king acted quickly. He immediately freed Queen Gunavara, apologizing profusely for doubting her. 
"The witches! I'll imprison the lot of them!" he raged.
"Forgive them, my lord," Gunavara smiled gently. 

The next day, Shringabhuja was proudly anointed crown prince, and all was well in the royal household of Vardhamana once again.
