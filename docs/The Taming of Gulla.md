# The Taming of Gulla

Bhootayya, the sahukar (wealthy landlord) of Jalahalli, was a merciless tyrant. He ruled the village with an iron fist, showing no compassion for the poor farmers who toiled on his lands. 

One day, Bhootayya impounded cattle belonging to a poor farmer named Bhaira, simply because they had accidentally strayed into his field. Bhaira pleaded with him, but Bhootayya refused to release them. Gulla, a proud and hot-headed young farmer, witnessed this injustice. "Impudent wretch!" Bhootayya sneered at Gulla. "He will have to be dealt with severely!"

That very night, Bhaira's humble thatched house mysteriously caught fire. As the villagers rushed to extinguish the flames, they whispered among themselves, "Poor Bhaira! How could the..." 

"Shh!" an elder warned quickly. "Do you want to suffer the same fate?"

A few days later, desperate and destitute, Bhaira was forced to borrow a hundred rupees from Bhootayya. The cruel landlord smiled as he took Bhaira's thumb impression on a harsh bond. 

A few months later, Bhaira fell seriously ill and died. Bhootayya immediately visited his grieving widow. "I had lent Bhaira some money," he demanded. "Now that he's dead, you will have to repay me." 

"I'm sorry, sir. I don't have even a cowrie," the poor widow wept. 

"Then you'll have to give up this hut and also surrender your land," Bhootayya declared ruthlessly.

Bhootayya's greed knew no bounds. He and his son, Ayyu, soon set their eyes on a large tract of fertile land near the river. "The land gets flooded during the monsoons when the river is in spate," Ayyu pointed out. 

"That's no excuse," Bhootayya replied. "All we have to do is build a dam there. And labour, as you know, comes free."

Bhootayya forced the villagers to work round the clock, building a massive dam to reclaim the land. Once it was ready, he built a grand mansion on the newly secured property for his son. "Ayyu, my son, this is for you," he beamed.

Soon after, however, Bhootayya fell seriously ill. On his deathbed, he offered his son one final piece of advice: "The villagers are all rascals. Show them no mercy. If you are clever, you'll make them work for you for nothing. See that... you recover the... salt... that rogue Kempa had borrowed..." Those were the tyrant's last words.

The villagers heaved a collective sigh of relief. "The devil is dead! This is the day of deliverance," they celebrated. 

But Gulla, the proud young farmer, was not so optimistic. "Not yet, uncle. The devil is no doubt dead. But his son is still alive. A snake begets only snakes. And the young snake must be crushed before he raises his hood."

Acting on his defiance, Gulla openly stopped paying rent for the land where he grazed his cattle. 

Ayyu retaliated swiftly. "I will confiscate his cattle," he declared. When Ayyu's men drove the cattle away, a furious Gulla confronted him. In the heat of the moment, Gulla struck Ayyu. The villagers quickly separated them, but the damage was done. 

"Gulla, you'll pay for this," Ayyu warned. 

Ayyu filed two lawsuits against Gulla—a civil suit to recover the unpaid rent and a criminal suit for assault. Gulla, determined not to yield, engaged a lawyer, paying him five hundred rupees. The case dragged on for months, bleeding Gulla dry. He was eventually forced to sell his wife's gold ornaments just to pay his mounting legal fees. 

Gulla's wife grew increasingly desperate. "This may cost us our house," she worried. Ayyu had already sought the court's permission to auction Gulla's house to collect his dues. 

Taking matters into her own hands, Gulla's wife secretly visited Ayyu's house. "Sir, please leave the village for just one day," she pleaded, tears streaming down her face. "My husband... he says... he'll come here... soon... with a sickle..."

Ayyu was startled. *The Sahukar has a revolver... my husband has a sickle...* her words echoed in his mind. 

Shaken, Ayyu went for a walk by the riverside to clear his head. Suddenly, he heard a splash. "Someone's fallen into the river!" Without hesitating, Ayyu dove into the swirling waters and dragged the drowning woman to the shore. It was Gulla's wife!

When Gulla heard the news, he rushed to the riverbank. "Thank God you are safe!" he cried, holding her. 

"She tried to take her life because of... Gulla... and me!" Ayyu realized with a heavy heart. 

The incident had a sobering, profound effect on Ayyu. He finally saw the destructive cycle of hatred his father had started. "I've had enough of these quarrels, uncle," Ayyu told a village elder. "I want peace... and friendship with all. Hate breeds hate. Gulla hates me for what my father did to him and his father. And after us, our children will continue the feud. This must stop."

Ayyu sought a compromise, offering to withdraw all the suits. "Let's be friends, Gulla," he proposed.

But Gulla's pride was too deeply wounded. "We can never be friends, Ayyu. NO!" he rejected the offer stubbornly. 

Eventually, the judge dismissed the criminal case against Gulla. Ayyu accepted the verdict calmly, even though he had lost hundreds of rupees to his lawyer. "It's all right. I can afford it. But think of poor Gulla," Ayyu sympathized. 

But Gulla remained bitter. "It's all because of that devil, Ayyu!"

A few days later, the police arrived at Gulla's house with an arrest warrant. He had failed to pay his own lawyer's remaining fees. Ayyu, who was passing by, stepped forward. 

"Look, I hate to do this," the police officer said. "Give me my money and I'll let you go."

When Gulla couldn't pay, Ayyu intervened and paid the lawyer's fees in full. He then turned to the humiliated Gulla. "I know how you hate receiving help from me. But I've not given the money as alms. I've given it as a loan. Work for me, and I'll adjust your salary against the loan."

Left with no choice, Gulla agreed and began working on Ayyu's lands. He worked with a fierce, almost possessed energy. A few months later, Ayyu smiled at him. "Gulla, you have cleared off your debt today. We can never be friends, Ayyu... But we don't have to be enemies." Gulla simply walked away in silence.

Then came the annual puja (festival) of Maariamma, the village goddess. As the preparations began, Ayyu's mind flashed back to his childhood, remembering the gruesome animal sacrifices performed in the goddess's name. *No! I must stop it forever,* he decided. 

He sent a message to the village elders, requesting them to stop slaughtering animals at the puja. 

The villagers were outraged by this interference in their ancient traditions. "We will incur the wrath of Maariamma if we listen to Ayyu! We'll die of plague!" they cried. Fuelled by superstitious fear and years of built-up resentment against the Sahukar's family, they decided to teach Ayyu a lesson.

That night, an angry mob, led by Gulla, marched to Ayyu's house. They broke down the door and barged in. Ayyu, armed with a revolver, stood facing them. *There are five bullets. I could kill five. Then what? Will I be the sixth victim? Why six corpses? Let there be only one—mine,* he thought, slowly lowering his weapon.

"Come on. Let's go in," Gulla ordered. 

The villagers ransacked the house, taking papers, silver, and gold. When Ayyu's wife tried to offer her own jewelry to appease them, Gulla stopped his men. "Don't touch them."

But the mob's fury was not easily quelled. Outside, some villagers set the house on fire! "Fire! Fire!" 

As the flames engulfed the building, Ayyu quickly evacuated his family, carrying his old mother to safety. They fled to their new mansion near the dam, outside the village.

The police arrived the next day to investigate the arson. But when they questioned Ayyu, he lied to protect the villagers. "A bullock upset the lantern in the shed. That's how it all began."

The police left, and the bewildered villagers thanked him silently. Even Gulla was conflicted. *Have I misunderstood him? I don't know...*

The very next day, Ayyu left for Mysore to visit his seriously ill sister. 

Then, it began to rain. It poured relentlessly for three days and three nights without a break. The river swelled dangerously. 

Gulla looked out at the raging waters. "It's the dam. The dam must have given way!" he realized in horror. And then, a terrifying thought struck him. "Ayyu's house is in danger. We'll have to rescue his family. He is away in Mysore!"

As the dam crumbled, a massive wall of water rushed towards Ayyu's mansion. The villagers watched helplessly from higher ground. "I can't see Ayyu's house either," one cried.

"Don't waste time. Get some wood. We'll build a raft," Gulla took charge immediately. 

Meanwhile, inside the flooded mansion, Ayyu's wife, mother, and children had fled to the highest terrace. The water was rising fast. "Amma! I hear someone calling," the young boy cried over the roar of the storm.

"HOY! HOY! HOY!" 

It was Gulla, battling the violent currents on his makeshift raft. Ayyu's wife desperately flashed a torchlight to guide him. Gulla skillfully maneuvered the raft to the terrace and rescued the entire family just as the waters threatened to engulf them. 

"Yes, it is me, Tai," Gulla said softly as he pulled them aboard. 

Meanwhile, Ayyu had rushed back from Mysore, hearing of the floods. He stood on the shore, agonizing over the fate of his family. "Gulla has gone in a raft, did you say?" he asked the villagers, his heart in his throat.

"Yes, sir. He left hours ago," a villager replied.

Just as hope seemed lost, a small raft emerged from the gloom. "Look! The raft... Gulla has made it!"

As the raft reached the shore, Ayyu waded into the water. "Appa!" his son cried. 

"Gulla!" Ayyu gasped, overwhelmed with emotion. 

The next moment, the two men, separated by years of bitter hatred and misunderstanding, fell into each other's arms, weeping. The feud was finally over. 

The villagers built a new house for Ayyu. Gulla became Ayyu's most trusted friend and right-hand man. And from that day forward, the villagers of Jalahalli abandoned animal sacrifices, choosing instead to worship the goddess Maariamma peacefully with beautiful, fragrant flowers.
