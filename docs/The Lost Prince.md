# The Lost Prince

Kshemankara, the eldest prince of the land, was hunting in the forest with his younger brother Papankara when Papankara's arrow, aimed at a pair of birds, was deliberately spoiled by Kshemankara. "What a spoil-sport you are!" Papankara complained. "Ask the birds, Papankara. They'll tell you which of us is the spoil-sport," Kshemankara smiled. "I learnt archery to protect, not to kill," he explained. "Well, you are Kshemankara — the good prince. I was born the villain. Why else would our parents have named me Papankara?" Papankara said bitterly. "Don't be childish, Papan. A man's deeds make him what he is, not his name," Kshemankara told him, and the two rode home together.

On the way, they passed a landlord throwing a widow and her belongings out of her home for failing to pay rent. "Is that enough cause to justify your action?" Kshemankara asked, and when the woman explained she had no one to support her since her husband's death, he removed his own necklace and gave it to her, over her protests. The grateful crowd blessed the "generous prince," but back at the palace, their father the King was displeased to learn Kshemankara had given away his necklace to a penniless stranger — "you are far too gullible, Kshema." Papankara, watching, offered a different, harsher read: "He's not gullible but shrewd, father, very shrewd... he's only investing it to earn high praise." The King grew angry at the suggestion his son's generosity was a performance, and Papankara backed down. "I am sorry, father. I didn't mean that."

Soon after, a neighbouring king proposed his daughter's hand in marriage to Kshemankara. "Tell him I don't intend to marry just yet, father," Kshemankara replied — he planned instead to sail off on a voyage to make his own fortune, rather than depend on a bride's dowry or his father's wealth. He recruited volunteers from the city, and Papankara asked to come along too. "Of course I will. I'd love to have you with me, Papan," Kshemankara said. The neighbouring king wrote again pressing for the marriage, and Kshemankara promised to marry the princess as soon as he returned. Then, with a fleet of ships, he set sail.

The voyage carried Kshemankara and his men to several lands, where they made a fortune trading. When one of his men suggested it was time to return home, Kshemankara insisted on visiting one more place first — the Island of Jewels, a mine of diamonds and precious stones — "from there, we'll turn homewards." At the island, his men gathered sackfuls of gems, but Papankara returned to the ship with nothing visible. "Where is your bundle? Didn't you collect any jewels?" Kshemankara asked. "I did. Just a few — but the most precious ones. They're in my waistband, Papan!" Papankara answered. With everyone aboard, the fleet turned for home.

Partway through the return journey, a huge whale rammed and shattered their ship. Men were flung into the sea, and as Papankara struggled, swept away and crying for help, Kshemankara swam to him — "Papankara! Swim close behind me..." — and with the last of his strength, dragged his exhausted brother onto a rocky shore, where both men collapsed. Meanwhile, the rest of the fleet reached the capital safely, and the survivors told the King the terrible news: his sons' ship had been destroyed by a whale, and there had been nothing they could do to help them. "A few might have survived...! Then there is hope!" the King clung on, refusing to give up. Months passed with no word. The King, now gravely ill, kept asking from his sickbed whether his sons had returned — until one day, Papankara alone arrived. "Where is Kshema?" the King asked. "I am sorry, father. He will never see us again," Papankara said. That night, the King died.

Papankara succeeded his father as King, but as he walked up to take the throne, something held him back, and he hesitated at the steps — troubled, the courtiers assumed, by thoughts of his dead brother — before finally taking his seat. Meanwhile, the neighbouring princess who had waited for Kshemankara refused every other suitor. "Father, alive or dead, Kshemankara alone will be my husband," she told her father, though custom eventually forced him to press her to marry someone. "Then permit me to choose the man," she said, and a Swayamvara was held, attended by princes from far and near.

As the princess walked among the assembled suitors, the faint notes of a flute caught her attention, and she followed the sound past the crowd of princes to a blind musician sitting beneath a tree — and garlanded him as her chosen husband. Her shocked companions ran to warn the king that she had chosen a blind man, but he respected her decision: "She will marry the man she chooses."

In the garden, the blind man asked why she had chosen a stranger like him. "I had given my heart to a prince who is dead now," she told him. "Since custom requires that a girl should marry, I have chosen you." "I cannot accept you, young lady. I love another," he said — the woman he loved might not have him now either, but he couldn't bring himself to marry anyone else. "And if you really loved the prince who is dead now, you wouldn't have chosen me," he added, testing her. "Do you doubt my love for the—" she began. "Yes, I do. And I will continue to do so until you prove your love for him," he said. "If my love for Prince Kshemankara be true," she declared, "let the sight of one eye be restored to you." His sight returned in that eye at once. "I am Kshemankara!" he revealed.

"No! You lie. Kshemankara was killed in a shipwreck," she said, refusing to believe it. He told her everything — the shipwreck, swimming to safety with his brother on his back, collapsing from exhaustion, and waking to find himself blinded, with no answer when he called out for his brother. "Without bearing any malice to whoever blinded me, I have been wandering from place to place in search of my brother. I have not found him... but I have found you!" She still needed proof: "What a beautiful story! But how do I know it's true?" "If what I have spoken be nothing but the truth, let the sight of the other eye be restored to me," he said — and it was. "My princess!" "Kshemankara!" She took him to the palace and told her father everything, and that very day, Kshemankara married the princess.

Soon after the wedding, Kshemankara said he wished to return to his own kingdom — to see his brother, since he now knew his father was dead and Papankara was king. "Would that be wise?" the princess asked. "He may think you have come back to claim the throne. He may even try to harm you." "Never! You don't know him as I do," Kshemankara said, trusting his brother completely.

Word reached Papankara that his brother was alive and on his way home. But as his minister spoke to him, Papankara wasn't listening — lost instead in his own memory of what he had actually done: dragging his sleeping, exhausted brother onto the shore, then piercing his eyes and leaving him for dead. "Why did I blind him? Why did I leave him to die? Why?" he thought, tormented by guilt. Unable to face Kshemankara, he ordered the city decorated to welcome the prince home — then quietly rode away before Kshemankara arrived, leaving no trace of where he'd gone.

When Kshemankara reached the city, he was welcomed warmly, but found his brother gone. "We have no idea. He left instructions to decorate the city for you," the people told him. Understanding what his brother must be feeling, Kshemankara did not go looking for him. Papankara never returned, and Kshemankara became king and ruled the land well.
