# Ashoka: The Great Son of India

In the third century B.C., King Bindusara ruled over India from his great capital of Pataliputra. He had a hundred and one sons, but Prince Ashoka excelled them all in the art of war. Therefore, when a rebellion broke out in the distant province of Taxila, Ashoka was sent to suppress it. 

Ashoka easily put down the rebels and was appointed Viceroy of Taxila to keep the peace. However, his hundred brothers were not happy to hear the news. "Ashoka has always been proud," they grumbled. "This success will go to his head and make him more arrogant."
They were unhappier still when Bindusara recalled Ashoka to the capital. The king was growing old, and his sons feared a ruthless struggle for the throne. 

Soon after, another rebellion broke out in the southern province of Ujjaini. Bindusara immediately sent for Ashoka. "The province of Ujjaini needs an able viceroy. You will have to crush the rebels there. You alone can do it," the king commanded. 
"May you emerge victorious!" Bindusara blessed him as Ashoka rode out. 

A greater part of the journey south was on horseback. When they reached the beautiful city of Vidisha-nagar, Ashoka ordered his men to camp and rest the horses. 
While walking around the city, Ashoka was charmed by a beautiful young woman leading a group of ladies carrying offerings. "Excuse me, lady. To which temple are you going?" he asked. 
"We are on our way to the *chaitya* (shrine). Not to a temple," she replied gracefully. 
"Then you are Buddhists," Ashoka realized. He learned her name was Vidisha, the daughter of a reputed local merchant (*shreshthi*). Deeply enamored, Ashoka immediately approached her father and asked for her hand in marriage. The merchant was honored, and after a quick wedding, Ashoka proceeded to Ujjaini with his new bride. 

Ashoka, a great warrior, routed the rebels in Ujjaini in no time. The people of the city gave him a grand welcome. But in the palace gardens later, Vidisha looked deeply saddened. 
"This is an occasion for rejoicing, Vidisha. But you look sad," Ashoka remarked. 
"I dislike killing... even in battles... especially by you," she replied. 
"That's silly. You say it because you are a Buddhist, I am not," Ashoka argued. "I am a Kshatriya king. My duty enjoins killing if need be."
"What about King Bimbisara?" Vidisha countered gently. "He too was a king. But he found peace in the teachings of Buddha."

The differences between them persisted. Months later, Vidisha gave birth to a son. "I am very happy," Ashoka declared. "I will name him Mahindra. He will conquer the entire world!"
Vidisha sighed inwardly, "I wish he conquers the hearts of men as Prince Siddhartha did."
Two years later, they had a daughter. Vidisha named her Sanghamitra—a Buddhist name, which displeased Ashoka. 

One day, a messenger arrived from Pataliputra. King Bindusara was gravely ill. Ashoka had to rush to the capital immediately. Since Vidisha was not fit to travel, Ashoka left her with her parents in Vidisha-nagar. 
"Before I leave, is there anything you want me to do?" Ashoka asked her. 
"Yes," Vidisha pleaded. "I want you to shun violence of all kinds." Ashoka promised he would. 

When Ashoka reached Pataliputra, his father was already dead, and his brothers were viciously fighting for the throne. "The throne! It is mine! I have earned the right to it," Ashoka declared, completely forgetting the promise he had made to his wife. 
Ashoka proved ruthless, brutally killing all his brothers who challenged his right to the throne. Sumanadevi, the pregnant wife of one of his slain brothers, managed to escape the palace through a secret door. She fled into the forest, where a kind old man gave her shelter under a *Nigrodha* tree. There, she gave birth to a son, naming him Nigrodha Kumar. Growing up in the quiet village, the boy became a Buddhist monk at a very tender age. 

Meanwhile, Ashoka had become the undisputed, powerful King of India. Taxila and Ujjaini were quiet, but Ashoka's thirst for conquest was not satisfied. "We should not rest till Kalinga is conquered," he told his commander. 
Ashoka's massive army invaded Kalinga. The soldiers of Kalinga fought bravely, but they were eventually crushed. 
"We have won. Kalinga is ours," the commander announced proudly. 

Ashoka rode out to inspect his newly conquered land, but what he saw horrified him. The battlefield was a desolate wasteland, littered with the mutilated corpses of over 100,000 men. Another 150,000 were captured, and countless innocent civilians were wailing in agony, begging for mercy. Lakhs had died in the brutal campaign. 
Seeing the immense suffering and senseless destruction he had caused, Ashoka was utterly shattered. "Oh! No!" he cried out in horror. 

Ashoka returned to his palace, completely losing his peace of mind. To add to his deep grief, a messenger brought a letter from Vidisha. It read: *"Arya putra, you have broken your promise. I am sending the children to you. Please look after them properly. I plan to join the Sangha of Buddha."*

Though Ashoka drew some comfort from the presence of his children, he continued to brood day and night over the horrific killings at Kalinga. One day, looking from his palace window, he saw a young, serene Buddhist monk walking along the road. Drawn to the boy's calm demeanor, Ashoka sent his guards to invite him in. 
"Venerable one, who are you?" Ashoka asked. 
"My mother called me Nigrodha Kumar," the monk replied. 
Ashoka was stunned. "I know that name... you are my brother's son. You must hate me, don't you? Because I killed your father."
"No. I love all," Nigrodha answered softly. "Hatred is destructive. It should be conquered by love. The teachings of Lord Buddha bring peace to troubled minds."

Deeply moved, Ashoka wept. "My mind has not known peace. Will you accept me as your disciple?"
"Repeat after me," the young monk guided him. 
"I take refuge in the Buddha. I take refuge in the Sangha. I take refuge in the Dharma."

A profound transformation came over Ashoka. He completely renounced war and violence. His entire focus shifted to bringing peace and happiness to his people. He built rest houses and dispensaries for travelers, planted trees, and erected great stone pillars across his empire, carving instructions of *Dharma* and non-violence onto them. He came to be known as *Priyadarshi*, the beloved king of the gods. 

Years later, Ashoka gave away all his worldly possessions, except his children. But soon, his son Mahindra approached him. "Father, I wish to join the Sangha." Ashoka, though with a heavy heart, gave his permission. 
Soon after, his daughter Sanghamitra, whose husband and son had also renounced the world, asked to join the nunnery. Ashoka agreed. 

A few years later, Mahindra left for Lanka to spread the teachings of Buddha. Two years after that, Sanghamitra also came to bid her father goodbye, as she was needed at the mission in Lanka. 
"Sanghamitra, I have grown old. I can bear grief no more," Ashoka wept, realizing he was now completely alone. "Oh! Vidisha, your children have left me just as you did for the sake of Dharma."
"Do not grieve," Nigrodha comforted the weeping king. "By sacrificing your dear ones, you have proved that *Dharma* is dear to you. You are indeed, Dharmashoka."

Thus, Ashoka became known as Dharmashoka. He ruled for many years in peace, looking after the welfare of the subjects of his vast empire as a loving father would look after his children. The five principles of peaceful co-existence he propagated are still respected the world over. The magnificent Ashoka pillar at Sarnath reminds us of his greatness to this day, and the *Dharma Chakra* (Wheel of Law) from that very pillar proudly adorns the national flag of India.
