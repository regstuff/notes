# Thanjavur: City of Brihadeeswara

On the banks of the river Kaveri once lived a wicked asura named Thanjan, who terrorised everyone around him. As the people cried out for help, Lord Vishnu himself heard their pleas and came down to confront the demon. The fight was brief and one-sided; Vishnu vanquished Thanjan easily. Dying, the asura begged one last favour of his conqueror: "Grant me one wish before I die. Let a town of great beauty come up at this spot where you have defeated me." Vishnu granted it, and Thanjavur — "Thanjan's Ur," or Thanjan's town — was born on the very ground where the demon fell. In time it grew so fertile and prosperous that poets sang of farmers using elephants instead of oxen to thresh the abundant paddy, and women flinging away their heavy gold earrings just to scare off the birds pecking at grain left out to dry.

Thanjavur became the capital of the Chola kings in the ninth century. Around 945 CE, a baby boy was born to King Parantaka Sundara Chola and his queen, and they named him Arulmozhi Varman — "one whose words are precious." Legend holds that the many wives of the great serpent Adisesha, who bears the weight of the earth upon his hood, danced with joy at his birth, certain he would one day lighten their husband's burden. Prince Arulmozhi ascended the throne in 985 CE, and after the ritual of the sacred bath was complete, he took a new name: Raja Raja, King of Kings.

He proved himself worthy of it. Under his wise and mature leadership the Cholas grew into a great power — though not without patience. When his ministers pressed him to march to war at once and seize new territory, Raja Raja held them back. "We must first train our soldiers and create a strong army," he insisted. Eight years later, ready at last, he launched a naval assault against the Chera king Bhaskara Ravivarman, destroying the Chera fleet outright, and spent the years that followed conquering steadily outward until his kingdom stretched from Kerala to Lanka, from the Maldives to Orissa.

Then, abruptly, he stopped. "No, I have had enough," he told his minister. "I have realised all my military ambitions. Now I only wish to serve Lord Shiva, like my grand-aunt Sembiyan Mahadevi." That devout queen had built many temples in and around Thanjavur in her own time, including one at Konerirajapuram, where she had once commissioned a bronze Nataraja at least six feet tall. The sculptor charged with the task struggled badly — try as he might, he could produce nothing taller than a small figurine, and the furious king warned him he would be beheaded if he failed once more.

On the very last night before his deadline, as the desperate sculptor prepared the molten bronze for one final attempt, two Brahmins knocked at his door and asked for water. Custom forbade him from serving Brahmins himself, and when he tried to send them to the Brahmin quarter instead, they refused to leave, demanding water all the more stubbornly — until, at his wits' end, he snapped: "You are stubborn! Go drink up that molten bronze which I have prepared for my statue!" To his horror, the two guests walked straight to the cauldron and did exactly that. But where he expected only disaster, he found instead a towering, magnificent bronze Nataraja standing where the molten metal had been — the two Brahmins had been Shiva and Parvati themselves, come down to save him.

The king refused to believe the story the next morning — "What rubbish!" — until, testing the sculptor's claim that the statue itself was somehow alive, he struck it with his sword and blood welled up from the stone. Realising the truth at last, he begged forgiveness, and it's said the mark where he struck the statue can still be seen on it today — the tallest bronze Nataraja in the world.

Raja Raja, like his ancestors, was himself a devoted follower of Shiva. Visiting the temple at Tiruvarur one day, he became so absorbed in a hymn being sung there that when it ended, he begged to hear more — only to be told that the saints who had composed the great body of devotional hymns were long dead, and most of their work already lost, save the few fragments that remained. "Then look for them! This is a royal order!" he commanded, and after many days of searching, an old man was found who knew where to look: the palm-leaf originals lay in a storeroom at the back of the Golden Temple at Chidambaram, a room that — according to the temple's priest — could only be opened by bringing the three saints who wrote the hymns to unlock it in person.

"But they are dead. How can we bring them here?" the king asked, stumped — until his queen had an idea. Life-sized statues of the three saints were fetched and carried to the temple door, and the priest, seeing them "brought" as instructed, had no choice but to let the king in. Inside, the palm leaves were found crumbling, riddled with white ants and mud, and the king wept to see so much lost. A voice — Shiva's own — spoke from the light: "Grieve not, O King! Only those hymns fit for this age are preserved. The rest have been consumed at my will." Raja Raja carried the surviving leaves out and arranged for them to be compiled and sung at every Shiva temple as the Thevaram, the garland of hymns still sung to this day — and in gratitude, the temple authorities at Chidambaram gave him a new title: Sri Raja Rajeswara, greatest of kings.

His triumph didn't last. Soon after, Raja Raja fell gravely ill with a strange, disfiguring disease. "You are cursed with the black leprosy," a holy man told him. "In your past life you were a ruthless hunter, and this is the result of your cruelty." A cure existed, but it was no small task: build a temple with a soaring tower and enshrine within it a linga brought all the way from the banks of the river Narmada. Raja Raja made the vow at once — and swore, besides curing himself, to make the temple "a monument to my military achievements... a measure of my devotion to Lord Shiva," providing employment to his people in the process. He recovered, and the building began.

It was to be no ordinary temple — forty times larger and five times taller than any other, and made entirely of granite, though there wasn't a single granite quarry for miles around Thanjavur. Undeterred, Raja Raja simply had a road built to haul the stone thirty miles from Mammalai. Craftsmen chiselled the hard granite with soft iron tools to cover the whole structure in carving so precise that a broomstick could pass through matching holes placed at equal distances across the walls, and raised gatekeeper statues eighteen feet tall — bigger, the awed queen remarked, than a full-grown elephant.

The temple's massive capstone, tradition says, began as a single boulder belonging to a poor cowherdess, who wished aloud that she could give it to the great temple being built. A voice from the heavens promised to relay her wish to the king, and when it reached him, Raja Raja ordered a long ramp built from her house all the way to the top of the rising temple, with elephants to haul the stone up. (In truth, historians note the capstone is actually built from many smaller stones fitted together without any cement or mortar at all — itself no small feat.) At the entrance stands a huge statue of Nandi, the bull, said to have grown mysteriously larger than intended after its sculptor discovered — at the king's own prompting — that a live toad had somehow been trapped inside the stone; once freed and set loose in a nearby sacred pond, the growing stopped.

The temple, at Raja Raja's own order, was also covered floor to ceiling with inscriptions recording every gift given to it by the king, his family, and his people — a priceless record of the Chola reign for later historians. It was finally completed in 1010 CE, twenty-five years after Raja Raja became king, and he consecrated it himself with water drawn from India's sacred rivers.

The temple walls were further adorned with frescoes, the finest depicting Shiva in his form as Tripurantaka, destroyer of the three cities. That legend told of three asura brothers — Kamaraksha, Taarakaksha, and Vidyunmali — who performed such intense penance that Brahma himself appeared to grant them a boon: three indestructible cities. "Nothing lasts forever," Brahma warned, but agreed to a compromise — the cities would exist in different worlds, built of gold, silver, and iron, meeting only once every thousand years, and could be destroyed only by a single arrow. Drunk on their invincibility, the brothers unleashed a reign of terror, certain nothing could touch them. Shiva alone resolved to end it. With Mount Meru as his bow, the serpent Vasuki as its string, Vishnu himself as the arrow tipped with Agni and winged by Vayu, and Brahma serving as his charioteer drawn by four Devas, Shiva waited a thousand years for the three flying cities to align — and when they finally did, a single arrow destroyed all three at once, ending the brothers' tyranny forever.

Another fresco told the story of Sundarar, a Shaivite saint. Adopted son of a local chieftain, Sundarar was about to be married when an old man burst into the ceremony insisting Sundarar was in fact his hereditary slave, brandishing an ancient palm-leaf deed as proof. Astonishingly, it was genuine, and Sundarar followed the old man away to a temple in bewildered obedience — where the stranger suddenly vanished into a blaze of light. "I was the old man, Sundarar," Shiva's voice announced. "I have chosen you to compose devotional songs for me." Honoured, Sundarar spent his life travelling and singing Shiva's praises, and along the way fell in love and married a woman named Paravayar. Years later he married a second woman, Sangliyar — and was struck blind on the spot for the sin of taking two wives. Sundarar never stopped singing Shiva's praises even in his blindness, and eventually his devotion moved Shiva to restore his sight. Many years afterward, Shiva sent Indra's own white elephant down to fetch the aged saint and carry him up to Mount Kailasa for good.

When Raja Raja died, his son Rajendra Chola pushed the empire further still, marching north and defeating king after king all the way to the sacred Ganga itself, earning the title Gangaikonda Chola — "the Chola who took the Ganga" — and founding a new capital, Gangaikonda Cholapuram, where he built a huge artificial lake he called his "liquid pillar of victory," filled with Ganga water carried back by the very kings he had defeated, along with a temple he pointedly declared should outshine even his father's — diverting funds and gifts away from the Brihadeeswara Temple to build it. Under Rajendra, the Chola empire reached its zenith, its influence reaching as far as distant Java and Sumatra.

The Chola empire itself came to an end in 1279 AD, but the monuments its kings raised across Thanjavur — chief among them Raja Raja's towering Brihadeeswara Temple — still stand, drawing thousands of visitors from around the world to marvel at what a king's devotion, ambition, and sheer stubborn will could build.
