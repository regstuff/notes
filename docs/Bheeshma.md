# Bheeshma: Selflessness Personified

One day, King Shantanu of Hastinapura, whose favorite pastime was hunting, was walking along the banks of the river Ganga in search of deer and bison. Suddenly, a stunningly beautiful maiden appeared in front of him. Shantanu fell in love with the maiden at first sight. 

"O divine maiden! Whoever you may be and wherever you may come from, I want to marry you," the king declared. 
"O King, I shall willingly become your wife," the maiden replied softly. "But on no account must you ask who I am or why I do what I do. And you must never speak an unkind word to me. The moment you do either, I shall leave you forever."
"So be it!" Shantanu agreed enthusiastically. 

Shantanu married the mysterious maiden, and they lived happily together. But his wife behaved strangely for a woman. Each time a child was born to her, she took the newborn and drowned it in the river! "This is for your good," she would simply say. 
Shantanu was deeply troubled and heartbroken. "It saddens me to see each child of mine killed," he agonized in secret. "Yet I dare not question my beloved wife lest I lose her altogether."

However, when the eighth child was born and she moved toward the river, the grief-stricken king could bear it no longer. "Stop! Who are you?" Shantanu shouted. "O wicked woman, how can you murder your own children?"
The maiden sighed sadly. "Alas! You have broken your promise. I am the Goddess Ganga. This child shall be called Gangeya after me. I had to kill our first seven children to redeem them from a curse. The eighth child was to have been mine, and we could have lived together forever. But as you have broken your promise, I will have to leave you now. I shall take the child with me."
And with that, Goddess Ganga and the infant disappeared. 

"Alas! I have lost both a divine wife and a glorious son, because I was impatient and wavered in my faith," Shantanu wept. But Shantanu did not let his personal sorrow affect his duties as a king. He continued ruling wisely and well. The years passed, and his people lamented that their wise and noble king had no heir. 

Then one day, as Shantanu came once again to the banks of the Ganges to hunt, he noticed the mighty river seemed subdued and restrained. Looking closely, he saw a handsome youth shooting an incredible, continuous stream of arrows into the water, forming a dam that blocked the river's flow! 
"An extraordinary feat which only one with divine powers can perform!" Shantanu marveled. The boy was Gangeya, his lost son, but King Shantanu did not recognize him. 

Gangeya, however, knew exactly who Shantanu was. "My father is perplexed. I will play a trick on him," the boy smiled, and suddenly vanished before Shantanu's very gaze. 
A thought suddenly struck the king. "Could he be my son Gangeya?" he wondered. He called out imploringly to the river, "Ganga! Please show yourself and that marvellous youth. Isn't he mine?"

Ganga appeared, holding the youth's hand. "This is Gangeya, the eighth son I bore you. He is unsurpassed in wisdom and the use of weapons. He has earned the title Devavrata. Take him home and make him your heir. He is a mighty archer and has learnt all there is to know about the duties of a king." Handing the boy to Shantanu, Ganga vanished for the last time. 

Shantanu took Devavrata and returned home joyfully. He summoned all his kinsmen, the Pauravas, and proudly announced, "Devavrata shall be the heir to my throne." The Pauravas looked upon the godlike youth and admired him. Devavrata soon earned their love and immense respect. In all branches of knowledge, both worldly and spiritual, his skill was unsurpassed, and his strength was extraordinary. 

Shantanu was happy to have such a worthy son and heir, but he still felt lonely at times. At such moments, he took long walks alone along the banks of the Yamuna. On one such walk, he smelled a divine fragrance. He followed it and saw a beautiful girl approaching. 
"Who are you?" Shantanu asked. 
"I am Satyavati, the daughter of a fisherman. I ferry passengers across this river in my boat," she replied. 
Overcome by desire, Shantanu thought, "Her fragrant perfume shall fill my lonely hours." 

He immediately went to Satyavati's father to ask for her hand. "I wish to take your daughter for my wife," the king said. 
"O King! I can never obtain a husband for her equal to you. But... I have one wish," the fisherman said carefully. 
"What is it that you wish? Name it!"
"That the son born unto my daughter shall reign after you. If you can promise me that, she is yours."

Shantanu was dismayed. "How can I disinherit my noble Devavrata?" he thought. Refusing to make such an unjust promise, he returned to his palace, his heart heavy and his thoughts entirely full of Satyavati. 

At Hastinapur, he passed his days lost in melancholy. Devavrata noticed his father's depression. "Father, why are you sad? You have everything a king can want—prosperity, loyal subjects, an heir..."
Shantanu tried to deflect. "You are my only son. You are an excellent archer and you welcome combats. Supposing you are slain, what will happen to the Bharat dynasty? It is this thought that worries me."
But Devavrata was far too intelligent to be taken in by these words. He sought out an old, trusted minister and asked, "Revered sir, why does my father grieve so?"
The minister explained, "He has set his heart on Satyavati, the fisherman's daughter. Her father will give his consent only if Shantanu promises that her son will be his heir."

Devavrata, accompanied by a few elderly Paurava chiefs, immediately went to the fisherman. "What is your objection to giving your daughter to my father?"
"He already has an heir in you. What chances would my grandson have against you? He would be condemned to a life of subordination," the fisherman argued. 
Upon hearing these words, Devavrata raised his hand and took a solemn oath. "I vow that the son born of your daughter shall reign as the king."

But the fisherman was still not satisfied. "I have no doubt that you will keep your word. But what about the sons *you* may have?"
Devavrata did not intend to go back without winning Satyavati for his father. "Chief of fishermen, I have given up my right to the throne. I now settle the matter of my children," he declared. And then, he took a terrible, ultimate oath: "From this day, I vow to remain a *Brahmacharin* (celibate) for the rest of my life!"

Hearing these words, the gods in heaven showered flowers upon the prince. "He is Bheeshma! Bheeshma of the terrible oath!" they praised him. And from that day on, Devavrata was known as Bheeshma (The Terrible). 

The fisherman happily gave his daughter. Bheeshma brought Satyavati to his father. At Hastinapur, when Bheeshma told Shantanu everything, the king was overwhelmed with emotion. "Son, for this incredible sacrifice, I grant you a boon. You will die *only* when you wish to."

Shantanu married Satyavati and had two sons. Years later, when Shantanu died and the elder son was killed in battle, Bheeshma dutifully set the younger son, Vichitravirya, on the throne and ruled the kingdom as a regent for him. 

When Vichitravirya came of age, Satyavati approached Bheeshma. "The King of Kashi is holding a *swayamvara* for his three daughters. I would like to win them for Vichitravirya."
Bheeshma went in a single chariot to Kashi, defeated all the rival kings who opposed him, and won the princesses—Amba, Ambika, and Ambalika. 

However, back in Hastinapura, just as the wedding was about to take place, the eldest princess, Amba, turned to Bheeshma. "In my heart, I had already chosen another. He too had accepted me. Knowing this, do what is right."
Bheeshma reflected upon dharma and decided, "I give you permission to do as you choose."

Vichitravirya was married to Ambika and Ambalika. Amba joyfully went back to her chosen lord, but he rejected her. "I no longer wish to marry you, as you were carried away by another man."
Amba was furious and humiliated. Blaming Bheeshma for ruining her life, she swore, "Bheeshma is the cause of all my suffering. I will destroy him!"

She went to Sage Parashurama, the fierce warrior who hated Kshatriyas, and told him her tale. "I beseech you, O venerable one. Slay Bheeshma and avenge me," she pleaded. 
Parashurama went to Hastinapura and confronted Bheeshma. "You will marry Amba."
"I cannot break my vow of lifelong celibacy," Bheeshma replied respectfully. "Besides, she wanted to be free."
"Then I will have to slay you," Parashurama threatened. 
Bheeshma and his own former guru, Parashurama, met in single combat and fought a catastrophic duel for twenty-three days. In the end, Bheeshma emerged the victor. 

Parashurama went back to Amba. "I tried my best, but I failed. I advise you to forget your humiliation and seek Bheeshma's protection."
"I cannot," Amba spat. Filled with burning hatred, Amba retreated to the forest and performed severe penances for twelve years to win the grace of Lord Shiva. 
Lord Shiva at last appeared. "What boon do you seek of me?"
"I wish to slay Bheeshma," she demanded. 
"You shall do so!" Shiva granted. 
"But I am a mere woman. How shall I slay him who defeated the mighty Parashurama?"
"You will be reborn as Shikhandin, the son of King Drupada," Shiva promised, and disappeared. 

Unable to wait for a natural death to be reborn, Amba built a massive funeral pyre and leapt into the flames. "I cannot wait to destroy this body so that I might enter the new body that shall slay Bheeshma!" she cried. As Shiva had promised, she was reborn as Shikhandin. 

Meanwhile, Queen Satyavati's bloodline continued. Her granddaughters-in-law gave birth to two royal lines—the hundred Kauravas (sons of Dhritarashtra) and the five Pandavas (sons of Pandu). Bheeshma faithfully supervised the upbringing of all his grand-nephews. 
However, deep-seated enmity grew between the cousins. As adults, the Kauravas refused to give the Pandavas their rightful share of the kingdom. Bheeshma repeatedly advised Duryodhana, the eldest Kaurava, "Give them what is theirs and avoid trouble." But the arrogant Kauravas did not heed him. 

Eventually, the great 18-day war of the Mahabharata broke out at Kurukshetra. On the first day of the battle, Yudhishthira, the eldest Pandava, walked barefoot across the battlefield to Bheeshma. "Venerable grandsire, we seek your blessings and your permission to begin the battle."
Bheeshma loved both the Pandavas and the Kauravas dearly, but he was duty-bound by his oath to protect the throne of Hastinapura, fighting on the side of the reigning Kauravas. 

For nine days, Bheeshma fought relentlessly, slaughtering the Pandava armies and proving utterly invincible. Drupada's son, Shikhandin (the reincarnation of Amba), was an ally of the Pandavas. 

On the tenth day of the battle, Krishna advised Arjuna on how to defeat the invincible grandsire. Shielding himself completely behind Shikhandin, Arjuna approached Bheeshma's chariot. 
Seeing Shikhandin, Bheeshma lowered his bow. "Shikhandin was once a girl. I cannot attack him. How fortunate that I heard the story of Shikhandin last night," Bheeshma thought. He closed his eyes. "The time draws near. I wish to die, but not at the hands of Shikhandin."
From behind Shikhandin, Arjuna fired a volley of devastating, armor-piercing arrows. As they struck deep into his chest, Bheeshma smiled. "The fatal arrows are Arjuna's! I am ready." And the great patriarch finally fell from his chariot. 

He was so pierced by arrows that his body did not touch the ground; he rested on a bed of arrows. Both armies immediately stopped fighting, dropped their weapons, and crowded around their beloved grandsire in tears. 
"I need a support for my head," Bheeshma whispered. 
Arjuna stepped forward and gently shot three arrows into the ground to support Bheeshma's head. "Fine! A pillow befitting a warrior," Bheeshma smiled. "Now I am thirsty."
At once, Arjuna shot a powerful arrow deep into the earth. A cool spring of pure, sweet water erupted and splashed directly into Bheeshma's mouth. Bheeshma drank and was happy. 

"My soul will depart only at the auspicious moment I have chosen for it," Bheeshma declared, using the boon granted by his father so many years ago. "I will lie here and wait."

The brutal war resumed, eventually ending in the total destruction of the Kauravas. The Pandavas won the war. After the victory, Yudhishthira came to the dying Bheeshma for his blessings and his advice on the duties of a righteous king. 
As Bheeshma finished imparting his profound wisdom to the new king, the sun crossed the horizon into the auspicious northern path (*Uttarayaana*). Bheeshma closed his eyes, released his breath, and his great, selfless soul finally passed away into eternal peace.
