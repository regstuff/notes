# The Story of Silk

Many, many years ago, a young Chinese Princess saw some strange worms eating the leaves of a mulberry bush in her garden. 

"See what I have found here!" she called out to her friends. 

"How funny they look!" her friends giggled. 

Every day, the girls would go to watch the worms. "They are becoming fatter day by day," the Princess noticed. 

"That's because they eat so much," a friend replied. 

"I wonder how much fatter they will become tomorrow," the Princess thought. But the next few days were wet and cold, and the children could not go out. When they finally did return to the garden, the Princess gasped. "Oh! What's the matter?"

"Alas! The poor worms have eaten too much and they are dying," her friend said sadly. 

The girls watched in wonder as the worms wove white, oval cocoons round themselves. 

"Those must be their coffins. See, some of them have covered themselves completely. They must be dead already. We will have to bury them," the Princess said softly. 

"Please bring us some tea and rice cakes," she instructed her maid. When the ancient Chinese buried their dead, they used to keep food and drink on the graves. 

Soon the tiny graves were ready. The Princess brought the cocoons... and began to drop them one by one into the grave. As she was doing this... she accidentally dropped one of the cocoons into her cup of hot tea! 

"Oh!" she cried. "What have I done! Its covering has become loose."

The soft-hearted Princess burst into tears and decided to take the ruined cocoon to her mother. Her mother, Empress Si-Ling, was a very clever woman. 

"Don't weep. Leave this worm with me and go back to your cousins," she comforted her daughter. 

"A worm which makes a covering for itself. How strange!" Empress Si-Ling marvelled. She dropped the cocoon into a bowl of hot water... then took it out again... and began to unwind it using a pair of chopsticks. 

The strand broke several times. But she joined it up each time and kept on. After some time, she had a long, glistening strand of thread in her hand. 

"I must see those worms for myself," she decided. 

When her daughter took her to the mulberry tree and showed her the cocoons, she was overjoyed. "You have found something very wonderful, my daughter," she smiled. "You have found the thread from which we shall weave the most beautiful cloth ever seen."

Lady Si-Ling taught her servants how to spin the strands into thread and to weave the thread into the shiny cloth which we now call silk. Ever since, Lady Si-Ling has been called the first maker of silk, though the name of the little Princess who discovered the cocoons has not come down to us.
