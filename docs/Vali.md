# Vali

Vali was the mighty and undisputed King of Kishkindha. Gifted with incredible strength, it was said that the very oceans and mountains trembled before him. Vali's parents were immensely proud of their powerful son, and his younger brother, Sugreeva, absolutely adored him. 

At that time, there lived a terrifying Rakshasa named Dundubhi, who arrogantiy considered himself the mightiest being in all creation. One day, spoiling for a combat, he went to the ocean and challenged it. 

The ocean churned defensively. "I cannot take you on. However, I know of one who should be able to—Himavan, the King of the Mountains. Go to him."

Dundubhi thundered up to the great mountain and angrily broke off huge white cliffs, roaring with glee. Himavan, the mountain king, was alarmed. "Why do you torment me?" he asked. 

"If you don't have the strength to fight me, then tell me who has. I will enter into combat with him," the Rakshasa bellowed. 

"Vali, the mighty King of Kishkindha, is a skilful warrior. In him you will meet your match," Himavan advised.

Transforming himself into a massive, mean-looking buffalo, Dundubhi charged up to the gates of Kishkindha and began to bellow loudly. "GR-R-R!"

Hearing the commotion, Vali, who was relaxing with his wives, rushed out in a fury. "I know it is you, Dundubhi. How dare you bellow at the gates of my city! If you value your life, leave now."

"How dare you speak to me like that in the presence of women!" Dundubhi taunted. "I come to challenge you to a combat, Vali."

Vali offered to fight him the next morning, but the arrogant buffalo refused. Without uttering another word, Vali seized Dundubhi by the horns and violently dashed him to the ground. The fierce battle began. Vali uprooted a massive tree and fought the beast. Finally, with a mighty heave, Vali picked the buffalo up and flung him high into the air with all his might.

Unfortunately for Vali, the heavy, mangled carcass of Dundubhi flew through the air and crashed down four miles away, landing right near the sacred hermitage of Rishi Matanga on the Rishyamuka mountains. 

The Rishi was furious at the desecration. Using his yogic powers, he discovered Vali was responsible. "If that monkey ever sets foot here, may he die!" the Rishi cursed. 

When Vali learned of the irate Rishi's curse, he rushed to plead for forgiveness, but the Rishi refused to relent. 

Sometime later, one dark night, Mayavi, the vengeful son of Dundubhi, arrived at Kishkindha. "O mighty Vali, dare you to fight me!" he roared. 

Vali woke up in a terrible rage. Brushing aside the wise warnings of his wife, Tara, Vali stomped out into the night. Hearing the commotion, Sugreeva woke up and loyally followed his brother. 

When Mayavi saw Vali charging out, closely followed by Sugreeva, he panicked. "I can take on one of them but not two!" he realized, and fled into the dark forest.

Vali and Sugreeva pursued Mayavi through dense forests and narrow mountain paths until the Rakshasa finally ran into a deep, dark cave. 

Vali was furious. "You wait here and guard the mouth of the cave, dear Sugreeva," he commanded. "I'm going after him. I cannot return to the city without killing him."

Sugreeva faithfully took his post at the cave's entrance. Many days passed, and Sugreeva began to worry terribly. "Why hasn't Vali come out? Is he dead?"

Then, almost a year later, Sugreeva heard a terrifying roar from the depths of the cave. "GRRRR!" Suddenly, a thick stream of blood gushed out of the entrance, followed by another horrific roar. 

Sugreeva mistakenly thought it was Mayavi's cry of triumph. "Alas, he has killed Vali! We are without a king. When Mayavi comes out, he will certainly destroy our kingdom. I must do something fast." 

To protect Kishkindha, Sugreeva heavily blocked the mouth of the cave with a huge boulder, sealing it completely. He then returned to Kishkindha, a very sad and grieving monkey. 

But inside the cave, the truth was very different. Vali had finally cornered and killed the scoundrel Mayavi! The blood and the roar had been Mayavi's death throes. 

Vali strode triumphantly towards the mouth of the cave, only to find it completely blocked. "The entrance! It's blocked! Sugreeva has betrayed me for the throne!" Vali concluded furiously, feeling utterly betrayed.

Meanwhile, back in Kishkindha, the nobles insisted that the kingdom could not remain without a ruler. Reluctantly, Sugreeva allowed himself to be crowned King, with the wise Hanuman serving as his chief minister. 

Back at the cave, Vali kicked the massive rock with all his enormous strength until it finally gave way. He stormed back into Kishkindha, only to find Sugreeva sitting on his throne. 

"You hypocrite!" Vali roared, confronting his terrified brother. "It was you who trapped me in the cave."

"Vali, I thought you were dead," Sugreeva pleaded desperately. "I heard Mayavi's roar... I did not want him to come out and destroy Kishkindha. You know it was Mayavi's blood that you saw!"

But Vali, blinded by rage and paranoia, was in no mood for loving reunions. "You lie! Now leave my country. I'll kill you if I see you again, Sugreeva!" 

Vali mercilessly beat his brother and drove him away. To make matters worse, Vali cruelly kept Sugreeva's beloved wife, Ruma, as his own captive. 

Sugreeva, heartbroken and destitute, fled Kishkindha with his loyal friend Hanuman. Remembering the Rishi's curse, they took safe refuge near Rishi Matanga's hermitage in the Rishyamuka mountains, the one place where Vali dared not set foot.

One day, while in exile, Sugreeva spotted two armed youths approaching the mountain. Fearing they were Vali's assassins, he sent Hanuman in the guise of a Brahman to investigate. 

Hanuman discovered they were Rama and Laxmana, the exiled princes of Ayodhya. Hearing of Rama's noble quest to rescue his abducted wife, Sita, Hanuman revealed his true form and carried the princes to Sugreeva. 

The two exiled royals forged a deep bond of friendship. Sugreeva promised to help Rama search for Sita, and in return, Rama promised to help Sugreeva defeat Vali and regain his wife and kingdom.

Sugreeva warned Rama of Vali's terrifying strength, showing him the massive skeleton of Dundubhi. To prove his might, Rama effortlessly kicked the heavy skeleton two hundred bow-lengths away! When Sugreeva still seemed slightly doubtful, Rama drew his bow and shot a single arrow that effortlessly pierced straight through the thick trunks of seven massive Sal trees before returning to his quiver. 

Convinced of Rama's divine power, Sugreeva marched to the gates of Kishkindha and loudly challenged Vali. 

Vali rushed out, burning with rage, and a furious fight ensued. However, Rama, hiding in the trees, did not shoot his arrow. Sugreeva's strength soon waned, and he barely escaped with his life, fleeing back to his mountain hideout. 

"Why did you let him defeat me?" Sugreeva reproached Rama, choking with emotion. 

"My friend, have faith," Rama apologized gently. "I just could not distinguish you from your brother. You look too much alike. I did not want to kill you even by mistake."

Rama asked Sugreeva to wear a distinctive garland of Gajapuspi flowers around his neck and challenge Vali again. 

When Sugreeva roared his second challenge, Tara begged Vali not to go out. "My Lord, our spies tell me that Sugreeva has two friends from Ayodhya on his side. Do not go."

"I know who they are," Vali scoffed confidently. "I know Rama. He is righteous. He will not interfere in a battle between brothers." 

Vali marched out and engaged Sugreeva in a brutal, bone-breaking brawl. As Sugreeva began to tire, he desperately raised his hands—the prearranged signal. 

From his hidden position, Rama took aim. *THWACK!* The unerring arrow found its mark, piercing Vali straight through the chest. Vali crashed to the ground. 

As Rama and Laxmana approached the dying king, Vali looked up at them with bitter reproach. "O Rama, what right did you have to kill me? I have done you no harm. Why did you interfere in our fight?"

"It is the duty of a king to mete out justice—to protect the oppressed and punish the oppressor," Rama answered firmly. "You have wronged your younger brother. You stole his wife, whom you should have cherished as a daughter. For these sins, you deserve to die."

Hearing Rama's righteous words, the anger drained from Vali, replaced by a profound remorse. "I cannot but admit that what you say is true," Vali whispered weakly. "Please forgive me, Rama."

Vali's wife Tara, and his young son Angada, rushed to the scene weeping. Vali looked up at his grieving brother. "Sugreeva, my hour has come. Please forgive me. I did not know what I was doing. It was your love I always wanted, not the throne."

With his dying breath, Vali removed his golden chain of invincibility and handed it to Sugreeva. "Do not grieve, Sugreeva. Take this golden chain. May it bring you success. Please rule Kishkindha properly... and take care of my wife and son."

And with that final act of reconciliation, Vali, the mighty King of Kishkindha, breathed his last, settling his worldly affairs in a manner truly befitting a king.
