# Raj Singh

Based on the famous Bengali novel by Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyaya. 

During the reign of the mighty Mughal Emperor Aurangzeb, a seemingly trivial incident in a small, peaceful Rajput state ignited a massive war. 

In the beautiful, hilly kingdom of Roopnagar, Princess Chanchal Kumari and her clever companion, Nirmal Kumari, were buying portraits from an old, traveling woman dealer. Among the paintings of various Hindu kings, the old woman showed them a portrait of Emperor Aurangzeb. 

Fiercely proud of her Rajput heritage and deeply resenting the oppressive Mughal rule, Princess Chanchal bought the portrait, threw it forcefully onto the floor, and violently crushed it under her foot. "Just to have the pleasure of crushing him under my foot!" she declared fiercely. 

Though Nirmal Kumari immediately bribed the terrified old woman to keep her mouth completely shut, the greedy dealer had no intention of keeping her promise. She traveled straight to Delhi and sold the sensational news to Daria Bibi, the ex-wife of the prominent Mughal commander Mubarak Khan. Daria, in turn, relayed the scandalous insult to Emperor Aurangzeb's proud daughter, Princess Zeb-un-Nisa. 

When the infuriated Emperor Aurangzeb heard how the little Rajput princess had insulted him, his ego was deeply wounded. Seeking a uniquely humiliating revenge, he immediately wrote a royal decree to Chanchal’s father, King Vikram Singh Solanki. The Emperor demanded that Chanchal be sent to Delhi, escorted by a massive army of two thousand Mughal cavalrymen, to become a lowly maid to his queens. 

Terrified of the Mughal's immense military might, King Vikram Singh saw no choice but to comply. 

Absolutely horrified, Princess Chanchal secretly penned a desperate letter to Maharana Raj Singh of Mewar, the greatest and bravest living Rajput hero. "I would rather die than marry a Mughal," she wrote. "Only you can save me. If you do not come, I will consume poison."

A loyal messenger rushed out of Roopnagar with the letter, but was violently ambushed by vicious dacoits on the way. The dacoits stole his belongings, including the royal letter. However, destiny intervened. Maharana Raj Singh, who happened to be hunting in the nearby forests, heard the commotion, charged down the hill, and effortlessly scattered the bandits. 

Only one dacoit, a man named Maniklal, surrendered. Having read the letter, Maniklal handed it over to the Maharana. Deeply moved by the princess's desperate plea and fiery Rajput spirit, Raj Singh immediately agreed to rescue her, generously accepting Maniklal into his service. 

With only fifty men, Maharana Raj Singh could not possibly win an open battle against the two thousand Mughal cavalrymen currently escorting the princess toward Delhi. Fortunately, the clever Maniklal devised a brilliant ambush. 

Disguising himself perfectly as a Mughal cavalier named 'Maqbul Khan', Maniklal successfully infiltrated the heavily guarded Mughal procession led by Commander Mubarak Khan. Gaining their trust, Maniklal deliberately guided the massive caravan into a dangerously narrow, steep rocky ravine. 

Suddenly, a massive boulder crashed down from the cliffs, completely blocking the path forward. Before the trapped, confused Mughals could react, Maharana Raj Singh and his fifty fierce Rajput warriors charged violently down the slopes. 

In the terrifying chaos and sheer panic that followed, Maniklal swiftly slipped through the fighting, rescued Princess Chanchal from her palanquin, and quietly led her to absolute safety. 

Realizing he had been brilliantly tricked and utterly outmaneuvered, Commander Mubarak Khan drew his sword to fight to the death. But the merciful Princess Chanchal intervened, forcing both sides to cease fighting, saving the defeated Mughal commander's life. 

Furious at Mubarak Khan's humiliating failure, Princess Zeb-un-Nisa secretly ordered him to be assassinated via a lethal snake bite. However, Daria Bibi, who still loved her ex-husband deeply, secretly dug him out of his grave and miraculously revived him using a powerful, secret antidote. 

Meanwhile, Emperor Aurangzeb's fury knew absolutely no bounds. Determined to completely crush the rebellious Rajputs once and for all, he personally led his entire, massive imperial army toward Udaipur, the capital of Mewar. 

But the Rajputs were perfectly prepared. This time, Mubarak Khan—now fiercely loyal to the Rajputs who had spared him—disguised himself as a simple local trader. He casually guided the massive, lumbering Mughal army, along with their heavily loaded supply carts and the royal palanquins carrying Aurangzeb's queens, into another dangerously narrow, treacherous mountain pass. 

The moment the Mughal forces were entirely trapped, Raj Singh's hidden troops attacked brilliantly from the high ground. They completely severed the Mughal supply lines, captured all their provisions, and even took Emperor Aurangzeb's queens—including Udipuri Begum and Princess Zeb-un-Nisa—as royal prisoners! 

Trapped, starving, and utterly humiliated, Emperor Aurangzeb was forced to formally beg for a truce. In exchange for the safe return of his royal women, he solemnly promised to completely recognize Raj Singh's absolute sovereignty and end all hostilities. 

But Aurangzeb's promises were entirely worthless. The very moment he was safely out of the mountains, he treacherously broke the treaty and ordered his top general, Diler Khan, to launch a massive, surprise attack on the Rajputs. 

The combined Rajput forces, brilliantly led by King Vikram Singh and the heroic Maniklal, met Diler Khan in a massive, open, and incredibly bloody battle. The fierce Rajputs fought like cornered tigers, ultimately inflicting a crushing, devastating defeat upon the Mughal army. 

During the terrible battle, the tragic Mubarak Khan was fatally wounded, dying peacefully in the arms of his friends. 

With his top general completely defeated, his massive army shattered, and his royal pride utterly broken, Emperor Aurangzeb finally accepted total defeat. He retreated permanently, never daring to attack the brave kingdom of Udaipur ever again. 

That very evening, amidst a grand, joyous celebration of their ultimate victory, the great Maharana Raj Singh formally married the brave and beautiful Princess Chanchal Kumari. Her deepest dream had finally come true—she had married a true Rajput hero, and the mighty Mughal Emperor had been utterly crushed, not just under her foot, but on the battlefield.
