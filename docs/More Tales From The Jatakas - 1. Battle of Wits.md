# Battle of Wits

Long, long ago, Mithila was ruled by a king named Vaideha. One night, he had a strange dream. Four pillars as high as the walls of his fort were burning brightly in the courtyard of his palace. Suddenly, a tiny flame appeared amidst the burning pillars and grew taller and taller till it dwarfed them, the fort, the palace, and all.

The king woke up trembling. "Oh God! What a dream!" 

The next morning he consulted Senak, Pukkus, Kavind, and Devind, the four pandits of his court. 

"Senak, what does my dream signify?" the king asked.

"Only good, Maharaj," Senak replied.

Pukkus stepped forward. "The four burning pillars you saw in your dream represent Pukkus, Kavind, Devind, and me. And the bright flame foretells the advent of a great soul whose wisdom will surpass our combined knowledge."

"When will this great soul be born?" the king asked eagerly.

"Your dream indicates that he entered the womb of a noble mother last night," the pandit answered.

Senak was right. The Bodhisattva had entered the womb of Sumana Devi, wife of Shrivardhana, a wealthy resident of Yavamajjhaka village. Ten months later, Sumana Devi gave birth to a radiant son.

"Our son's face glows with divinity," Shrivardhana marveled. "What is he holding in his hand? I'll open it and see." When the tiny fist was opened, he found a small piece of wood. "It's just a piece of wood."

Suddenly, the newborn baby spoke in a clear, melodious voice. "This piece of wood possesses mysterious powers of healing."

For the past seven years, Seth Shrivardhana had been suffering from frequent bouts of headache. "The next time I have a headache, I'll try this out," he thought.

The very next day, he had one. He quickly brought out the piece of wood, rubbed it on a grinding-stone, and applied the paste to his forehead. And lo!

"It's gone! My headache has gone!" he rejoiced. "I'll mix this in water and distribute the mixture to the ailing."

Fame of the herb spread, and ailing people from near and far flocked to Shrivardhana's house. 

"The pain and agony that tortured me for five long years vanishes with just one dose of medicine! I must be dreaming," a cured man cried out happily. "May the child who brought this divine aushadhi (medicine) live long!"

When the child had to be named, Shrivardhana made a decision. "It is a tradition in our family for the eldest son to be named after his grandfather, but since our son was born with the miraculous aushadhi, we will break the tradition and name him Mahaushadha Kumar."

Mahaushadha Kumar grew up happily in the company of the children of his village, but he was far more intelligent and knowledgeable than any of them. One day, when he was barely seven years old, his friends complained, "Aushadha, the streets are too crowded. How can we play here? Let's go indoors."

"But the rains will soon be here, and then we will have to play indoors all the time," another boy said.

"No, we will not," Aushadha replied confidently. "We'll have a new town with a sheltered playground. We'll ask our parents to donate the money for it."

Aushadha Kumar assembled all the local artisans and spoke to them of his plans. "I want to raise a township which will have a stadium for children, a home for homeless women, rest-houses for travellers and mendicants, and public halls for prayers and settling disputes."

The simple artisans of Yavamajjhaka village were unable to visualize Aushadha Kumar's ideas, so he drew a plan of the township for them. Work began and progressed quickly yet smoothly under his supervision. Before long, the children had an exclusive playground with sheltered sections where they could play when it rained. Destitute women, mendicants, and travellers alike blessed their benefactor, Aushadha Kumar.

The people of Yavamajjhaka were so impressed with Aushadha Kumar that they often asked him to settle their disputes. 

One day, two women were fiercely fighting over a baby. 
"Give me my baby!" one cried.
"It is my son! Not your baby," the other retorted. "I have succeeded in taking the child away from her. It proves that I am its mother."

Aushadha Kumar watched them and proposed a test of strength to pull the child. Naturally, the baby cried in pain. One woman immediately let go of the child because she could not bear to see him suffer. 

"She is his mother," Aushadha proclaimed, returning the baby to the rightful mother. 
"My child! My son!" she wept with joy.

Meanwhile, as several years had passed since the advent of the great pandit was predicted, King Vaideha decided to seek him out. "Ministers, comb the whole kingdom and see if you come across any exceptional child," he ordered.

One of his ministers, who travelled eastward, came to Yavamajjhaka. Looking at the magnificent township, he asked, "Who is the architect of this magnificent township?"

"Aushadha Kumar, the son of Seth Shrivardhana," a villager replied.

The minister thought, "He must be a very learned man. Learned he certainly is, but not yet a man. He's just a boy of seven! I must send a message to the king."

The minister's messenger sped to Mithila. "Maharaj, the minister is convinced that seven-year-old Aushadha Kumar of Yavamajjhaka is the child you are looking for."

"I had my dream about eight years ago!" the king realized.

Pandit Senak advised, "I think we should invite Aushadha Kumar to Mithila."
Another pandit, worried about his position, countered, "What if he is the soul whose wisdom will surpass our knowledge? I am not so sure, Maharaj. Planning a township is no criterion of wisdom or of the greatness of a soul."

"You are right," the king agreed. "Ask the minister to observe Aushadha Kumar for a few more days and to report anything unusual he sees."

The minister did as he was ordered. Then one day, a falcon swooped down and snatched a large piece of meat from a villager. "Hey! Thief! Thief! Oh, God! What shall I do? A whole leg of mutton gone!" the man cried.

"Don't look so dejected. I'll get it back for you," Aushadha Kumar assured him. He rushed like the wind, keeping his eyes on the sky, till he was right below the shadow of the falcon. "Drop that meat at once!" he commanded loudly. To the minister's shock, the falcon dropped the meat.

The minister immediately sent word to the king. "...And, the falcon obeyed him! Maharaj, the minister is convinced that Aushadha Kumar is endowed with mystic powers."

But the jealous pandits still resisted. "His arrival will rob us of our status. I must prevent his coming here," one thought. Senak argued, "Maharaj, I am still not convinced. The falcon could have dropped the meat out of sheer fright."

So the minister was asked to continue his observation.

A few days later, a farmer dozed off under a tree while his robust bullocks rested. A thief came by and quietly led the magnificent animals away. When the owner woke up, he gave chase. "Where are you taking my bullocks?"

"Your bullocks?... They are mine!" the thief boldly lied. "You thief, these are my bullocks."

The villagers gathered and suggested, "Why don't you go to Aushadha Kumar? Let him decide who the true owner is."

Brought before Aushadha, the thief argued, "His bullocks? Ha! How could he raise such fine, healthy bullocks when he himself is so skinny?"

"You are right. The bullocks are healthy," Aushadha said to the thief. "What do you feed them to make them so robust?"

"Oh, Master, I feed them yavagu (barley gruel), sesamum laddoos, and udad (black gram)," the thief confidently lied.

Aushadha turned to the owner. "And you my friend, if as you claim these are your bullocks, what special feed do you give them?"

"I am a poor man. I can't afford special food. I feed them ordinary hay," the owner replied honestly.

Aushadha instructed the villagers, "Friends, grind a paste of mustard leaves and feed it to the bullocks." The paste had the desired laxative effect. 

"Now examine the dung dropped by the bullocks," Aushadha ordered. "...Do you find any grains of barley, sesamum or udad?"

"No! Not a single one," the people confirmed.

"So, you feed yavagu and udad to the bullocks. Do you? You are a thief!" Aushadha declared.

The thief stammered, caught in his own lie. The enraged villagers beat up the deceitful man, and Aushadha returned the bullocks to their rightful owner.

When this tale was narrated in the court of Vaideha, the pandits scoffed again. "Maharaj, anyone with common sense can solve such a problem," they argued.

A few days later, an old woman was spinning cotton in a field. She set her yarn down to drink some water, and another woman stole it. The thief brazenly claimed she spun it herself, despite the old woman's protests that her rough hands proved her hard work. They were brought to Aushadha.

Aushadha studied the women carefully. He saw the old woman's rough hands and the strands of cotton in her hair, while the other woman's hands betrayed she barely did any hard work. 

To trap the thief, Aushadha asked her, "What have you rolled the yarn on?"

Thinking quickly, the thief assumed an old cotton worker would use a cotton seed. "It's rolled on a cotton seed."

Aushadha then asked the old woman, who replied, "On a gourd seed, Master."

When Aushadha unrolled the thread, it revealed a gourd seed inside. The yarn was rightfully returned. 

Yet again, the pandits in Mithila refused to admit Aushadha's greatness, claiming the problem was too simple. In this manner, on seven occasions did the pandits of Mithila dispute the wisdom of Aushadha Kumar to stall his induction into the court. 

But King Vaideha was tenacious. "I shall test Aushadha Kumar myself."

He had the tapering stem of a sapling cut and shaped into a perfect cylinder, so no one could tell the root end from the shoot end. The piece of wood was sent to Seth Shrivardhana's house. "King Vaideha wants the shoot end and the root end of this stem to be identified," the messenger announced.

Aushadha took the wood, tied a string to its center, and placed it in a bowl of water. "In a plant, the root end is heavier than the shoot end," he explained. "So the end that dips into the water is the root end and the other, of course, the shoot end."

The king was delighted, while the pandits were downcast. "He is certainly knowledgeable and observant," the king said. "Now let me see if he is clever and has his wits about him."

The king's men went to Aushadha with a bizarre request. "The king wants you to send him a rope, as the rope of his swing suddenly snapped. But his rope was not an ordinary one. It was made of sand. And he wants an identical one."

Seth Shrivardhana was baffled, but Aushadha smiled. "I know how. But I have a small problem... I do not know how thick the snapped rope was. If he could provide us with a sample, we'll make an identical one for him."

When the king heard the counter-demand, he exclaimed, "Remarkable! The boy is extraordinary! Aushadha Kumar is beyond doubt a great soul. I shall now go and fetch the great pandit here myself."

The next morning, the king left his capital for Yavamajjhaka. However, on the way, his horse tripped and broke its leg. 
"This is a bad omen, Maharaj," a minister warned. "You should give up the idea."

"It is not a bad omen," the king replied. "Let us return to the capital and send another cryptic message to Aushadha Kumar. Let us see if he stands this final test."

A messenger arrived at Shrivardhana's house. "The royal horse is injured. Please send a mule or its superior for the king."

Shrivardhana was indignant. "What kind of a demand is this? Shall we send a donkey which is superior to the mule by virtue of being its father?"

"Father, it is neither mule nor donkey that the king wants, but you and me," Aushadha deduced. "I must put an end to this battle of wits. By showing disrespect to you in the court, father."

"But son... you have always respected me."

"And I always will, father. What I am going to do is only a part of a scheme. You go to the court first. I will follow you. When the king asks me to take a seat, you get up and I will sit in your place."

When Seth Shrivardhana reached the court, the king asked about his son. Soon after, Aushadha Kumar arrived... riding a donkey! 

"Salutations, Maharaj," Aushadha said.
"Please choose a seat that befits your status, O Pandit," the king offered.

Aushadha walked straight to his father, pointed at him, and ordered him to get up. Then, he sat in his father's seat. 

The pandits erupted in mocking laughter. "Aushadha Kumar has no respect for his father! He is no better than the donkey he brought with him!"

The king looked deeply disappointed. "I never dreamt that one whom I had held in such high esteem would let me down so badly. How could you make your father get up and occupy his seat!"

"Do you always consider the father to be superior to the son?" Aushadha asked calmly.
"Always!" the king declared.

Aushadha pointed outside to his mount. "Who is superior? A donkey or a mule?"
"The mule, of course," the king replied. "It is a better animal, sturdier and capable of harder work."
"But the donkey is the father of the mule," Aushadha pointed out gently.

The court fell silent as the profound wisdom sank in. 

"And so it sometimes is with man," Aushadha concluded. "A young person can sometimes be wiser than his elders. And one, to whom the welfare of the state is entrusted, should select his counsellors guided by this fact."

King Vaideha smiled broadly. "Seth Shrivardhana, I wish to adopt Aushadha Kumar as my son. Do I have your permission?"

"You do, Maharaj," Shrivardhana agreed proudly. "It would be selfish of me to refuse when the people and the state would benefit so greatly by this adoption."

In appreciation of his selfless gesture, the king granted Seth Shrivardhana the village of Yavamajjhaka. And thus, Aushadha Kumar was formally adopted by King Vaideha of Mithila, emerging victorious in the ultimate battle of wits.
