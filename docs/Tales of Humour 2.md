# Tales of Humour 2 - 1. The Money-Lender Meets His Match

One sunny afternoon, a poor, hard-working woodcutter was chopping wood deep in the forest. Suddenly, he heard a loud, terrifying snorting sound. Looking up, he saw a massive, angry wild buffalo charging aggressively right towards him!

At once, he dropped his heavy axe and quickly scrambled up a tall tree, narrowly escaping the beast's sharp horns.

Furious at losing its prey, the buffalo rammed the thick tree trunk again and again. *THUD! THUD!*

"Help! Help!" the woodcutter cried out, clinging tightly to the swaying branches.

After some time, the stubborn buffalo grew very tired from its useless efforts and finally fell fast asleep right at the base of the tree. 

"Saved!" the woodcutter sighed in immense relief. *Now I'll quietly climb down and escape,* he thought, looking down carefully at the sleeping beast. 

But just as he began to quietly climb down...

"...Huh! Here comes Dayaram, the greedy money-lender!" the woodcutter muttered, spotting the cruel, wealthy man walking casually down the forest path. 

Dayaram was known throughout the village for his ruthless, cheating ways. Seeing him, the woodcutter smiled mischievously. *I'll play a trick on him... hee hee hee!* he thought.

"Sssh... up here," the woodcutter whispered loudly from the branches.

Dayaram stopped, looking up in confusion. "?"

"Oh, it's you..." Dayaram sneered, recognizing the poor woodcutter. 

"Hush! Lower your voice!" the woodcutter warned him urgently, putting a finger to his lips.

"But, why...?" Dayaram asked loudly.

"That is the king's royal buffalo sleeping down there. You mustn't wake him up!" the woodcutter lied smoothly. Then he leaned down, whispering conspiringly. "Come up here quickly if you want to make some easy money."

Dayaram's greedy eyes immediately lit up. "Money? Oh, good!" 

When the greedy money-lender had climbed up and settled uncomfortably on a branch, the woodcutter began his grand tale. 

"Now listen closely," the woodcutter whispered. "For a whole month now, I have been the official keeper of this royal buffalo. My job is merely to sit quietly on this tree and mind it while it rests. When I take it safely back to the palace in the evening, the king gives me a thousand gold coins."

Dayaram's jaw dropped open in shock. "A thousand gold coins? For only a day's work?"

"Yes—that's exactly how incredibly precious this buffalo is!" the woodcutter nodded seriously. "Having done this extremely easy job for so long, I have obviously become a very rich man."

The woodcutter sighed, looking at Dayaram kindly. "Now I feel I should step aside and let somebody else make some easy money too."

"How incredibly thoughtful and kind you are!" Dayaram quickly grasped his arm, completely blinded by his greed. "I will gladly take up the job! A very wise decision!"

"The brute is still fast asleep. Good!" the woodcutter whispered, looking down at the snoring buffalo. As quietly as he could, the woodcutter carefully climbed down the tree, leaving the greedy money-lender perched high in the branches.

"I must simply sit all day on this tree, you said?" Dayaram asked softly from above.

"Sssh... ssh... yes, yes," the woodcutter nodded solemnly from the ground. "Or you certainly won't get your thousand gold coins."

And with that, the woodcutter happily hurried home.

So, all afternoon, the miserable money-lender sat uncomfortably on the hard tree branch, terrified to make a sound. 

"Oooh! How terribly stiff and sore my limbs have become," Dayaram groaned quietly, rubbing his aching back.

Finally, in the evening, seeing the buffalo still sleeping, he stiffly climbed down. He approached the beast confidently. 

"Get up, you!" Dayaram yelled, kicking the buffalo. "I want my gold coins."

The wild buffalo awoke with a start. *GRRAH...!* it bellowed, instantly enraged at being violently kicked.

"OW!" Dayaram screamed as the massive beast bucked and chased him fiercely. 

"Aaah! He'll gore me to death. Help!" Dayaram shrieked in absolute terror, running wildly through the forest. *RAM!* The buffalo chased him relentlessly, repeatedly charging at him as he frantically scrambled back up the nearest tree.

It was a very, very long time before the angry wild buffalo finally gave up and left the forest... and the bruised, terrified money-lender could carefully climb down again. 

"I... I never want to see a buffalo again!" Dayaram whimpered, limping painfully back to the village without a single gold coin.


# Tales of Humour 2 - 2. Sundar and the Seth

One morning, Sundar saw his friend Sonu sitting idle by his hut, looking absolutely miserable. 

"Hey, Sonu! No work today?" Sundar called out cheerfully.

"I've stopped working for Sethji," Sonu muttered darkly, hugging his knees. "I'd rather starve to death than work for him."

"Why? What happened?" Sundar asked, sitting down beside his friend.

Sonu touched his nose nervously. "He did not pay me a single paisa and... he was about to... c...cut off my nose."

"How awful! But, why?"

"Because that was his cruel condition when he hired me," Sonu explained. "He said that if I ever left his service of my own free will, he could legally cut off my nose. And if *he* sacked me, I could cut off his nose. But he works his servants so incredibly hard that they always quit first... and then he keeps their wages and threatens their noses!"

"How strange!" Sundar smiled, rubbing his chin thoughtfully. "Your Sethji sounds like an incredibly interesting man. I'll go and offer my services to him today."

Soon after, Sundar marched up to the wealthy man's large house. "Sethji, do you need a strong, hard-working servant?"

The Seth looked at him shrewdly. "I do. But... I have certain strict conditions..."

"I already know all about them, Sethji," Sundar interrupted with a wide smile.

The Seth looked surprised, but pleased. "You do? Excellent! Then you can start immediately."

A few days later, the Seth was talking to his wife. "He does the work of three men!" the Seth bragged happily. 

"And he eats as much as three men!" his wife complained.

"Let him eat!" the Seth chuckled evilly. "Remember, because of our special agreement, he won't be getting a single paisa from us in wages! Eventually, he will beg to leave, and I will keep all his money."

One day, as Sundar was passing by a deserted spot in the forest, he stumbled upon the rotting carcass of a dead wild horse. "A dead horse..." Sundar smiled. "That gives me a brilliant idea!"

Sundar quickly cut off the dead horse's tail. Then, running back to the Seth's house, he secretly untied the Seth's beautiful, expensive mare, led it deep into the forest, and tied it securely to a hidden tree there.

Afterwards, he planted the severed horse's tail firmly in a deep, muddy marsh near the village... and ran home screaming in mock terror.

"Sethji! Sethji!" Sundar yelled, bursting into the courtyard. "Your expensive mare is sinking in the marsh! Hurry! Hurry!"

"Oh!" the Seth cried, dropping his ledger and running as fast as he could.

When they reached the marsh, Sundar pointed to the tail sticking out of the mud. "Look! It's completely gone in!"

"Oh, the poor mare!" the Seth wailed, grabbing the tail and pulling with all his might. 

*SPLOSH!*

The tail came completely loose, and the Seth fell flat on his back into the foul-smelling mud.

Sundar gasped loudly in fake horror. "Sethji, you pulled too hard! You have tragically killed your own mare. Now you'll have to give a massive religious feast to the entire village to atone for your terrible sin!"

"A... feast?" the Seth choked, imagining the horrifying cost of feeding hundreds of people. His incredible greed instantly took over. 

"Sundar, let's keep this strictly to ourselves!" the Seth begged, grabbing Sundar's arm. "Don't breathe a single word of this to anybody in the village. Please!"

"But, Sethji, you brutally killed the poor beast..." Sundar argued loudly. 

"Shh! Sundar, not so loud!" the Seth shushed him frantically. "I'll personally pay you a hundred rupees right now to keep quiet."

Sundar frowned. "Only one hundred? A proper feast to wash away your sins would have easily cost you..."

"All right! All right! Take two hundred!" the Seth cried desperately. 

"It's a deal!" Sundar agreed instantly.

The two returned home, and the terrified Sethji paid Sundar the money in secret. 

The very next day, the Seth had an important meeting. "Sundar, fetch the important legal documents from my desk. I have to take them to the Zamindar immediately," the Seth ordered.

"Yes, sir," Sundar obeyed. He went into the study, found a pair of dirty old *chappals* (slippers), carefully wrapped them in a neat piece of cloth, and gave the official-looking parcel to his master.

At the Zamindar's house, the Seth bowed deeply. "I have brought the very important papers, sir," he said, dramatically opening the cloth parcel before the Zamindar and all his noble guests. 

"HA! HA! HA!" the Zamindar and his guests roared with laughter at the sight of the dirty chappals.

The Seth returned home, fuming with sheer rage. "You... you...!" he screamed at Sundar.

"Yes, Sethji?" Sundar asked innocently.

"You rogue! How dare you publicly insult me!" the Seth roared. 

"I didn't do a thing Sethji..." Sundar shrugged casually. "...Yet you can legally sack me right now if you like!"

The Seth opened his mouth to fire him—"I will!"—but suddenly remembered the cruel condition. If he sacked Sundar, Sundar got to cut off his nose!

"You're... you're...!" the Seth sputtered, his face turning red. "Never mind!" 

A few days later, when Sethji had to go visit his father-in-law's house in a neighbouring village, Sundar followed him quietly. Taking a shorter route, Sundar reached the house well ahead of his master.

"Namaskar, sir!" Sundar greeted the father-in-law. "I am a new servant of your son-in-law. Sethji is on his way here right now."

"Welcome, my boy," the man smiled.

"Sethani (the Seth's wife) has urgently sent me to tell you that only raw green chillies and black salt should be given to him as food during his stay," Sundar lied smoothly.

The father-in-law looked shocked. "Green chillies and salt?"

"A strictly special diet, sir. He is not well at all, you know," Sundar sighed sadly.

"What is wrong with him?" the mother-in-law asked, rushing out with deep concern.

Sundar tapped his head. "He is... tragically suffering... from brain-fever... he behaves very strangely and aggressively at times."

"Oh, my God!" the mother-in-law gasped in horror.

Sethji arrived a little later, tired and extremely hungry from his journey. At lunch time, his mother-in-law carefully placed a small plate before him.

"Here is your special lunch, dear son-in-law," she whispered nervously.

Seth stared at the plate. "Special lunch... this?" he asked, looking at the pile of raw chillies and salt. 

"Yes. Your servant has told us all about your terrible condition," she sniffed.

"My servant?" Seth roared, looking around the room. He spotted Sundar grinning in the corner. "You!"

"That's the terrible trouble, mother," Sundar said loudly to the in-laws, shaking his head. "He forgets so soon..."

"What do you mean?" the mother-in-law asked.

"Remember the dead mare, Sethji?" Sundar whispered menacingly to the Seth. "Or should I loudly remind you about the sin?"

The Seth instantly paled, terrified the whole village would find out about his supposed crime. "You... you...!" he stammered helplessly. "Well... I'll eat this... special food!" 

And for supper, too, the miserable Sethji had to eat the burning "special food" of raw chillies and salt. 

At midnight, his stomach was completely empty and his mouth was on fire. "Oh... that rascal! I'll... I'll brutally punish him..." the Seth groaned, tossing in his bed. "But... first... I need some real food... oh!"

He tiptoed quietly into the dark kitchen, fumbled around, and finally opened a large tin of sweet *laddoos*, greedily stuffing them into his mouth.

Suddenly, in the dark, his foot knocked over a large metal pot. *THANN! NOOO!* the loud clatter echoed through the silent house.

"Who... who is there!!" the mother-in-law cried, rushing out of her room. 

The Seth froze in terror and quickly hid behind a door.

"It's a thief! Help! Help!" she screamed. "Get a light someone!"

"I am coming!" Sundar yelled, running into the kitchen with a heavy stick. In the darkness, he purposefully began thrashing the hidden figure.

"What! You!" the mother-in-law gasped as the light finally revealed the battered "thief." "I... I..."

"I'll explain!" the bruised Seth cried pitifully. "But please let me eat first. I am completely famished!"

The very next morning, the battered and broken Sethji called Sundar aside in secret. 

"Please leave me! I beg you! I have had enough," the Seth sobbed, falling to his knees. "Sundar! Cut off my nose! Do anything but just leave me alone!"

But Sundar did not cut off the Seth's nose. Instead, he forced the Seth to hand over five hundred rupees, ending the cruel contract. Sundar returned home a rich man, and generously gave the Seth's hidden mare to his friend, Sonu, who never had to worry about money again.


# Tales of Humour 2 - 3. The Reward

One day, a poor, hard-working farmer went out to his small field to tend his crops. As he walked among the vines, he suddenly stopped and rubbed his eyes in sheer disbelief.

*Are my eyes playing tricks on me?* he thought, staring at the ground.

Lying in the dirt was a truly massive, gigantic pumpkin. It was larger than a boulder!

"I've never seen a pumpkin as large as this in my entire life," the farmer gasped, struggling to lift the incredibly heavy vegetable onto his shoulders. "I'll give it to our noble king as a rare gift."

He carried the giant pumpkin all the way to the palace. 

The king was absolutely amazed and immensely pleased with the unique gift. "This must surely be the biggest pumpkin in the entire world!" the king laughed, admiring its size. He turned to his royal treasurer. "Give this good man a thousand gold pieces as a reward."

The farmer returned to his village a very rich man. "The farmer is a lucky man indeed!" the villagers gossiped, amazed at his sudden wealth.

Soon, news of the farmer's incredible good fortune reached the ears of a wealthy, greedy miser in the village. 

The miser was stunned. *A thousand gold coins for a mere, ordinary pumpkin?* he thought greedily, his mind racing. 

He rushed to his large safe and pulled out his most expensive, glittering jewel. *I wonder what the generous king would give me if I offered him this incredibly costly diamond necklace?* the miser plotted. *He gave gold for a vegetable... for diamonds, he will surely give me cartloads of gold!*

Blinded by his own greed, the miser went straight to the palace... and presented the priceless necklace to the king.

"It's absolutely beautiful!" the king exclaimed, admiring the sparkling diamonds. 

The miser smiled eagerly, waiting for his massive reward.

*What can I possibly give you in return for such a priceless gift?* the king thought to himself. *Gold? Diamonds?* 

The king looked closely at the wealthy miser's fine clothes. "No, you are already a very rich man and would certainly not care for such common things as gold or money," the king decided aloud. 

The miser's smile faltered. 

"Ah, I know exactly what I can give you!" the king beamed, suddenly clapping his hands together. "Something truly rare and unique!"

The king ordered his guards to bring out his prized possession. "Here! You may have the giant pumpkin!"

The miserable miser, clutching the ridiculously heavy pumpkin in his arms, stumbled out of the palace in an absolute daze, having traded his priceless diamonds for a vegetable.


# Tales of Humour 2 - 4. A Matter of Rights

A wandering sadhu, making his daily rounds to beg for food in the village, knocked gently on a solid wooden door.

"Praise be to God..." the sadhu began softly as a young woman opened the door.

The young daughter-in-law of the house frowned at him. "I'm sorry. I've nothing to offer," she said curtly.

"Please, charity in the name of..." the sadhu pleaded.

*BANG!* 

The young woman viciously slammed the door right in his face.

"She has absolutely no respect for sadhus," the sadhu muttered angrily to himself, walking away from the house in a huff. "That arrogant daughter-in-law behaves as though..."

"Did you say daughter-in-law?" a sharp voice interrupted him.

The sadhu turned around to see an older woman carrying a heavy bundle of firewood. 

"Yes," the sadhu complained. "The young daughter-in-law of that house just turned me down when I politely asked for alms..."

The older woman dropped her firewood in sheer outrage. "What! *My* daughter-in-law refused alms to you!"

"Yes, mother!" the sadhu nodded. "Who does she think she is—the absolute mistress of the house?"

The fierce mother-in-law picked up her umbrella like a weapon. "Come with me, sir. I'll clearly show you whose writ actually runs in that house."

The sadhu smiled happily, expecting a good meal. "You're very kind, mother. Let me carry that heavy firewood for you."

"No... no..." the mother-in-law said, marching towards the house. 

"I insist," the sadhu said eagerly, picking up the heavy bundle and following closely behind her. 

They reached the door. *KNOCK! KNOCK!* the sadhu banged loudly. 

The young daughter-in-law opened the door and gasped. "!"

"You insolent girl!" the mother-in-law roared, pointing her umbrella at the terrified young woman. "How dare you arbitrarily drive away people who come here begging for alms!"

The daughter-in-law trembled. "I... I..."

"Go to the kitchen at once!" the mother-in-law commanded fiercely. "I'll have something much more to say to you later!"

The frightened young woman quickly scurried away into the kitchen without another word. 

The sadhu grinned broadly, dropping the heavy firewood on the porch. "That's exactly the way to talk, mother," he praised the older woman. He held out his begging bowl eagerly. "Now..."

The mother-in-law turned to him, her eyes still blazing. "Now go away! There's neither flour nor bread for you here!"

The sadhu's jaw dropped. "Mother!"

"It is *I* who refuse to give you anything!" the mother-in-law declared proudly, thumping her chest. "I am the only mistress here! Not her!"

*BANG!* 

She slammed the door violently in his face, and the poor, exhausted sadhu fell completely over backwards in shock.


# Tales of Humour 2 - 5. Buddhiraj

One bright morning, a clever little turtle named Buddhiraj was slowly making his way back to the safety of his lake when a hungry fox suddenly pounced from the bushes. 

"Caught you!" the fox cried triumphantly, pinning the poor turtle down with his sharp paws.

Buddhiraj didn't panic. Instead, he simply sighed. "But what's the real use, my friend? You can't eat me."

The fox glared at him, licking his lips. "Why? Who'll possibly stop me?"

"My incredibly hard shell, of course," Buddhiraj replied calmly, tapping his solid back. 

The fox scratched his head. "Oh, I didn't think of that!" he admitted, looking disappointed. He tried to bite the shell, but nearly broke a tooth. "But... there must be a way."

Buddhiraj looked up at the fox with innocent eyes. "It will become quite soft, if you simply hold me underwater in the lake for a little while."

"Brilliant!" the foolish fox grinned. He picked up the turtle, carried him to the water's edge, and held him submerged in the shallows.

Soon...

*I wonder exactly how long I'll have to wait,* Buddhiraj thought, comfortably submerged in his natural element. 

After some time, the fox grew impatient. He lifted Buddhiraj slightly above the surface. "Has your shell become soft yet?"

"It has, indeed," Buddhiraj lied smoothly. "Except..."

"Except what?" the fox demanded.

"...Except for the very small parts where your heavy paws are holding me," Buddhiraj explained patiently. "If you just let go for a single second, the water will soften those spots too."

The hungry fox nodded eagerly. "Only for a single second, mind you," he warned.

But the moment the foolish fox let go...

*SPLASH!*

...the incredibly clever Buddhiraj slipped deep into the dark waters of the lake, never to return to the hungry fox!


# Tales of Humour 2 - 6. The Boy and His Coins

A young boy named Anand used to make a hard living by chopping and selling firewood. One particularly hot day, he was carrying a massive bundle of wood on his head.

*This load is very heavy,* Anand groaned, sweating profusely as he walked towards the market. *But it will fetch me a lot of good money.*

And sure enough, a wealthy merchant bought the entire bundle. As Anand stood by a wall, happily counting his well-earned coins, an old woman approached him.

"Young man, you must never count your money in the open like that," she warned him gently. "Someone may see it and steal it from you."

Anand quickly put his coins away. "You are right, mother."

The old woman smiled warmly. "You look terribly tired and hungry from your hard work. Come, I will gladly give you something to eat."

Anand's stomach rumbled at the thought. "Thank you, kind lady," he smiled. "Food would be most welcome."

Some time later, after eating a huge, delicious meal in the woman's home, Anand patted his stomach happily. "No more... I am completely full!"

"I'll take a quick stroll through the town before I go home," Anand announced, standing up to leave.

"A word of warning, son," the old lady said, her face suddenly serious. "There are many dangerous pickpockets in the town... Why don't you keep your hard-earned money safe with me? You can safely collect it on your way back home."

Anand, trusting the kind woman who had just fed him, nodded. "Yes, I'll do that." He handed over his small pouch of coins. "Here."

"The houses here look so similar!" Anand realized as he stepped out the door. "How will I find your house again in the dark?"

"Don't worry," the old lady smiled kindly. "I'll keep a bright lamp outside my door so you can find it easily."

*What a wonderfully kind lady she is...* Anand thought as he happily strolled into the town square.

But when the boy returned to the street in the dark evening...

*There's no lamp outside any door!* Anand panicked, frantically searching the completely dark street. *Now how will I ever find her house?*

He realized the terrible truth. *I have been cruelly tricked! She was only after my money.*

The devastated boy sat down in the dirt and began to cry loudly. His loud sobs soon attracted attention, and a curious crowd of villagers quickly collected around him. 

"What's the matter, boy?" an old man asked gently.

"Lost your way?" a woman asked.

"Don't cry! Tell us what happened," another offered.

Anand suddenly had a brilliant idea. Wiping his fake tears, he spoke loudly enough for the whole street to hear. "I found ten large gold coins on the road!" he lied. "And I desperately want to keep them safe with the kind lady who helped me earlier today, but I simply can't find her house!"

Suddenly, a door flew open. The greedy old lady rushed out into the street, completely fooled by the promise of more gold. 

"Here I am, dear boy! Here I am!" she yelled eagerly, pushing through the crowd. "Quickly, give me the gold coins!"

Anand's tears vanished instantly. He smiled broadly. "The only coins I ever had are already safely with you. Now, may I please have them back?"

The old lady's face fell in sheer horror. *Tricked!* she thought, realizing her terrible mistake.

With the entire village crowd watching her expectantly, the greedy old woman could do absolutely nothing else but quickly return the clever boy's money.


# Tales of Humour 2 - 7. The Locked Postbox

The wealthy merchant, Popatlal, was exceptionally busy one morning at his shop. He finished writing an important document and quickly sealed it in an envelope.

"This urgent letter simply must go today," Popatlal muttered to himself.

He called out to his simple-minded new servant. "Here, Kallu! Take this important letter immediately and post it for me."

Kallu took the letter carefully but looked utterly confused. "B... but where exactly do I post it, sir?"

Popatlal rolled his eyes impatiently. "In a postbox, of course! Where else?"

Kallu scratched his head. "But sir, I've never even seen a postbox in my entire life!"

*Good grief!* Popatlal thought in utter despair. He pointed out the window towards the main road. "Just go straight down that street. You will eventually find several postboxes standing there. You can't miss them—they are all bright red in colour."

"Yes, sir!" Kallu said eagerly, taking the letter and running out.

So Kallu went out into the bustling city. He wandered up and down the streets, staring intently at everything painted red. He saw the big, red iron pillar boxes, but he just stood there scratching his head.

Kallu did not return the whole day. 

Finally, in the late evening, Popatlal was preparing to close his shop. "Where on earth is that foolish fellow?" Popatlal fumed. "I must lock up the shop now... Ah! Finally, here he comes!"

Kallu came running up the street, panting heavily. "Sir! Sir! Here's your letter," Kallu said proudly, handing the slightly crumpled envelope back to the merchant.

Popatlal stared at the letter in absolute horror. "What?! You didn't post it?!"

"No, sir," Kallu replied earnestly, shaking his head. "I carefully followed your instructions. I've seen several of those bright red postboxes all day long..."

"Then why didn't you put the letter inside one?" Popatlal screamed.

"...But they were all securely locked with a little brass padlock on the front!" Kallu explained defensively. "How could I possibly put your important letter into a locked postbox? I didn't have the key!"

Popatlal violently slapped his forehead in sheer despair. "Oh, God!"


# Tales of Humour 2 - 8. The Show-Off

Once there was a very wealthy man who just couldn't help showing off his expensive possessions to everyone he met. 

One evening, he invited all his friends over to his grand mansion just to boast about his latest purchases.

"This intricate cuckoo clock is imported directly from Switzerland," the rich man bragged, pointing to a beautiful wooden clock on the wall. 

"And this delicate, beautiful toy is a genuine Japanese doll," he continued, holding up a small porcelain figure.

His friends politely admired his collection, though they were secretly getting quite bored of his endless boasting. 

Suddenly, one of his friends smirked. "These are all very nice, indeed," the friend said casually. "But... do you happen to have a *talking parrot*?"

The rich man froze, his smile vanishing. 

"My other friend has a talking parrot, and it's absolutely fascinating," the friend added innocently, walking away.

The rich man seethed with jealousy. *A talking parrot?* he thought in a panic. *I must get one immediately! Wait for me...*

Without excusing himself, he ran out of his own house and rushed straight to the bustling market-place. He went directly to the exotic bird seller's shop.

He pointed to a bright green parrot in a brass cage. "Can this particular parrot talk?" the rich man demanded anxiously.

"There's no doubt about it!" the shopkeeper answered confidently. 

"Perfect! I'll take it," the rich man beamed. He pulled out a crisp banknote. "Here's a hundred rupees for this parrot."

The rich man rushed excitedly back home with the cage to finally impress his friends. 

"Look everyone!" the rich man announced grandly, bursting into the living room. "Here's my amazing talking parrot!"

His friends gathered around the cage with keen interest. 

"Can you talk?" one of the friends asked the green bird.

"There's no doubt about it," the parrot replied immediately, its voice surprisingly clear.

"Remarkable!" the friends gasped in sheer amazement.

"What is the day today?" another friend asked the clever bird.

"There's no doubt about it!" the parrot squawked brightly.

The friends looked at each other in sudden confusion. 

"Are you hungry?" a third friend asked.

"There's no doubt about it!" the bird repeated loudly.

The first friend burst into uncontrollable laughter. "Your incredibly expensive parrot only knows a single sentence!" he roared. 

"What a complete fool I've been!" the rich man groaned, realizing he had been completely swindled by the clever shopkeeper. He angrily opened the cage door to shoo the useless bird away.

As the green parrot gladly flew out the window to its freedom, the rich man's friends all howled with laughter at his great expense. 

"HA HA HA!" the friends laughed.

And from high up in the sky, they heard the parrot's final reply: "There's no doubt about it!"


# Tales of Humour 2 - 9. The Considerate Shopkeeper

One busy afternoon, a smart little girl went to the sweet shop to buy some fresh *jalebis* for her family.

She held out a coin to the fat shopkeeper. "Here! Two rupees worth, please."

The greedy shopkeeper, Lalajee, weighed out a very small portion of sweets, wrapped them in a tiny leaf packet, and handed it to her.

The little girl frowned, looking at the small packet in her hand. "But my mother brings home many, many more jalebis for two rupees!" she protested.

Lalajee waved his hand dismissively with a fake, sweet smile. "Oh, that! I did it solely for your own sake, child," he lied smoothly. "You are so small, I didn't want you to strain yourself. Now you'll have a much smaller, lighter packet to safely carry all the way home!"

The clever little girl stared at the cheating man for a moment. Then, a wide smile spread across her face. "Oh! I see," she said innocently. 

She reached into her pocket, pulled out a single coin, and placed it firmly on the counter. "Well, here's your money!"

Before the shopkeeper could say anything, she turned around and began to quickly walk away. 

Lalajee picked up the coin and frowned. He counted it. "Hey! Listen! Come back here!" he yelled angrily.

The little girl paused and looked back over her shoulder.

"There's only a single one-rupee coin here!" Lalajee shouted, waving his fist. "You owe me another rupee!"

The little girl smiled sweetly at him. "Oh, that! I did it solely for your own sake, Lalajee..." she called back. "...Since you are so very busy, now you'll have much less money to count!"

And before the furious shopkeeper could catch her, the clever little girl ran happily home with her sweets.


# Tales of Humour 2 - 10. The Fat Man

During the rush hour traffic, an incredibly fat man was slowly and painfully trying to squeeze his large body through the narrow door of a crowded city bus. A long queue of frustrated, impatient people was quickly forming behind him on the street.

"Oh, Lord! How long is he going to take?" a man complained loudly from the back of the line, checking his watch.

The fat man continued to struggle, blocking the entire entrance. 

Suddenly, a frustrated passenger directly behind him lost his patience entirely. 

*WHAM!*

The impatient passenger shoved the fat man violently from behind with both hands. 

*WHOOSH!* 

The fat man flew straight through the doors and tumbled headfirst down the aisle of the bus. The rest of the relieved people quickly got in behind him, and the bus sped away.

Sometime later, after he had finally recovered his breath and found a seat, the fat man stood up with a furious glare.

"Who was it that pushed me so violently as I was trying to get in?" the fat man roared, glaring menacingly around the bus.

While the two greedy drivers were busy crawling around on the floor of the dark cabin, desperately searching for a non-existent gold ring, the clever old man simply turned around and casually walked away into the night. 

*And I've safely reached my home without having to pay them a single paisa for the ride! Ha ha ha!* the old man chuckled to himself, patting the heavy gold ring safely hidden in his pocket.

# Tales of Humour 2 - 26. Two Tricks

There was once a massive, incredibly strong man who lived in a certain king's court... who was also incredibly arrogant and always showing off his muscles.

One day, while standing before the king and a large crowd of courtiers, the strong man began his usual boasting.

"I can easily carry that big rock over there on my back," he bragged, pointing out the window to a huge boulder. "And I can easily drink all the water in that massive royal clay pot in one single breath! Can anyone else here do it?"

The crowd remained silent, intimidated by his size.

"No? Not one of you weaklings?" the strong man mocked, puffing out his chest.

Suddenly, a very thin, ordinary-looking young man stepped forward from the crowd. "I can!" the young man announced loudly. "In fact, I can do much more than that! I can carry a whole *hill* on my back. And I can drink all the water in the *river*!"

The strong man glared down at the skinny youth, his face turning red. "If you are joking, boy, I'll break your head with my bare hands!" he threatened.

"And if I am not joking?" the young man asked calmly, completely unafraid. "What will you give me if I actually do it?"

"I will personally give you a hundred gold coins," the strong man sneered confidently, knowing it was completely impossible. 

"Good! Then it's a bet. I'll do it next Sunday," the young man agreed smoothly. "But to build up the necessary strength, I'll absolutely have to eat extremely well for the next seven days."

Certain he was going to win the bet, the arrogant strong man actually paid to have the very best, most expensive food in town sent to the youth every single day that week. 

"Ah! This is the life!" the young man smiled happily, feasting like a king all week long.

When Sunday finally came around, the strong man knocked loudly on the youth's door. "Shall we go?" he grunted.

The entire town had gathered at the base of the nearest hill to watch the incredible show. 

The young man walked up to the base of the hill, squatted down slightly, and braced his hands on his knees. "I'm completely ready, sir," the young man announced to the strong man. "You can put the hill on my back, now."

The strong man stared at him in utter confusion. "What? I can't lift the hill! You do it yourself!"

The young man stood up, shaking his head. "Oh, no, no! I specifically said I would *carry* the hill, not *lift* it! If I am to carry it, someone else has to lift it and put it on my back first!"

"HAHAHA!" the watching crowd erupted into loud cheers and laughter at the clever trick. 

"Come on, big man," the villagers mocked. "Lift the hill... and put it on his back!"

The strong man turned red with intense embarrassment. "All right, all right, you win this round," he grumbled. "But you still have to drink absolutely all the water in the river!"

"Very well," the young man smiled. He walked down the bank and waded waist-deep into the flowing river. 

"Where exactly am I standing right now, sir?" the young man asked loudly.

"In the river..." the confused strong man answered.

The young man dipped a small clay jug into the rushing water right in front of him, filled it to the brim, and drank it all down in one gulp. 

He wiped his mouth. "What did I do just now, sir?" the young man asked.

"You drank all the water inside that jug," the strong man replied impatiently. 

The young man grinned broadly. "Exactly! And since that water was currently physically located *in the river*... There you are, sir! I successfully drank *all the water in the river*!"

The crowd went wild. "What an incredibly clever fellow!" they cheered. "HA! HA!" "CLAP! CLAP!"

The strong man clenched his massive fists, furious at being humiliated. "You... you dirty trickster! I-I'll..." he roared, rushing forward.

The young man held up his hands, still smiling. "Please don't be angry, sir. I did it just for fun to prove a point. I don't really want your hundred gold coins."

The strong man stopped, surprised by the boy's generosity. His anger slowly faded into genuine respect. He sighed heavily. "Well, you are indeed very clever, my young friend," he admitted humbly. "You have certainly taught me a very valuable lesson about boasting."

The king, who had been watching the entire exchange, was also immensely pleased by the boy's sharp wit. 

"The strong man may keep his gold, but I will not let such cleverness go unrewarded," the king announced. He handed a heavy, clinking bag to the youth. "Here are a hundred royal gold coins for your trouble, young man!"
