# The Deadly Feast

According to legend, the Bodhisattva was once born as Aushadha Kumar, a young man endowed with celestial knowledge and superhuman powers. Dazzled by his intellectual feats, Vaideha, the King of Videha, adopted him and brought him up in his royal court. Soon, the King was so impressed by Aushadha Kumar's wisdom that he appointed him as his Chief Minister.

"Maharaj Vaideha," Aushadha Kumar said one day, "we must fortify our capital to prepare for any emergency."

"Do what you think appropriate, Aushadha," the King agreed.

Aushadha made elaborate arrangements to protect Mithila, the capital, from invaders. First, he built a massive wall with watchtowers for sentries. Then he built three moats around the wall. The first he filled with water, the second with treacherous slush, and the third was left dry. In the fortified city, he constructed large tanks of water and stocked sufficient food grains.

Then he selected a hundred faithful warriors. "Go to the hundred kingdoms of Jambudveepa," he instructed them. "Win the confidence of the kings, but do not reveal your real identity... and send me regular reports of their plans." 

The men disguised themselves and scattered across the land to carry out Aushadha's bidding.

Meanwhile, while Aushadha was securing Videha, Kevatta, the minister of Panchala, was plotting to expand the domain of his King, Brahmadatta. 

*King Brahmadatta is very generous to me,* Kevatta thought to himself. *I must help him to become the overlord of Jambudveepa. My status also will rise with him.*

And so, Kevatta worked out a cunning plan. "Maharaj Brahmadatta, we must increase our forces," he advised the King. 

One of Aushadha Kumar's spies, who was present in the court of Brahmadatta, immediately sent a secret letter. When the letter reached Aushadha Kumar, he grew concerned. "King Brahmadatta is up to something... but I have no means of knowing what his exact plans are."

He smiled, looking at his pet parrot. "Well, I have the means to know. It is time for my parrot to act. Fly to Panchala, my pet, and find out the plans of Brahmadatta for me."

"As you wish, Pandit," the parrot squawked, for Aushadha Kumar knew the language of birds. 

The parrot flew swiftly to Panchala and alighted on the windowsill of King Brahmadatta's chamber. 

"Maharaj, I want to discuss a secret plan," Kevatta was saying. 

"Go ahead, Minister Kevatta."

"Not here, Maharaj! Even walls are said to have ears... let us go into the garden."

The two men went into the royal garden. Aushadha's parrot silently followed them, hiding in the branches of a large tree right above them. 

"We have built a fairly large army," Kevatta began. "You should now become the overlord of Jambudveepa."

"But, we are not powerful enough to accomplish that," Brahmadatta protested.

"We can, Maharaj! By deceit!"

"Deceit?!"

"Yes! We shall surround one small kingdom at a time and then inform the king that we will spare his life and let him rule his kingdom if he accepts your overlordship. The king will have no option but to surrender. Thus, one by one, we shall annex all the hundred kingdoms without shedding a drop of our blood."

"But I will be overlord only in name, as the kings will rule their own kingdoms," Brahmadatta pointed out. 

"They will not, Maharaj!" Kevatta grinned wickedly. "After the campaign is over, we shall invite them to a feast... where all of them will be poisoned."

"Excellent!" Brahmadatta exclaimed. 

High up in the tree, the parrot was horrified. *Rascal!* it thought. *I must inform Aushadha Pandit immediately.*

While taking off, the angry parrot deliberately dropped its excreta right on Kevatta's head. "Drat that parrot!" Kevatta cursed, wiping his turban as the bird flew away. 

The parrot flew straight back to Aushadha Kumar. Aushadha was deeply perturbed by the information. "I cannot allow so many kings to die... I shall protect them from untimely death. But before that, I must arrange to protect my own people."

Aushadha Kumar swiftly moved the citizens from the outskirts into the fortified city of Mithila and stocked the granaries with ample quantities of food grains. 

Meanwhile, King Brahmadatta marched with his army to carry out the nefarious plan conceived by Kevatta. He surrounded the fort of a neighbouring king and sent a messenger: "Accept our friendship and prosper, or invite our wrath and perish."

As envisaged by Kevatta, the enemy king surrendered without putting up a fight. In seven years, seven months, and seven days, Brahmadatta successfully forced the surrender of all one hundred kings through his clever scheme. 

Aushadha Kumar received timely reports of each surrender. "Kevatta is crafty," Aushadha mused, "but I am ready for him."

With all hundred kings subdued, Brahmadatta turned to Kevatta. "We should now turn to Videha. His is the only kingdom not under us."

"Maharaj, Videha is a very small kingdom. It will be demeaning for an overlord of a hundred kings to attack a tiny state."

"All right, but what about the remaining part of our scheme... when do we get rid of the kings?"

"I shall send invitations of death to the kings right now," Kevatta sneered. 

The hundred kings received identical messages: *Emperor Brahmadatta, the overlord of Jambudveepa, invites you to a feast to celebrate his Digvijaya (conquest of the world).*

When Aushadha Kumar learned that the deadly feast had been scheduled, he decided to act. He summoned his men. "Take a hundred trusted warriors disguised as Panchalas... enter the venue of the feast a little before its scheduled time... and destroy all the food and drinks. But before you leave, announce yourselves as men of the King of Videha."

His warriors infiltrated the grand kitchen preparations in Panchala. "Where is the seat for King Vaideha?" one of them demanded loudly. 

"He is not invited," the head cook replied. 

"That is an insult to our King!" the warrior shouted. "Since our King is not invited, we will not allow anyone else to enjoy the feast!"

With that, Aushadha Kumar's men smashed the pots, overturned the cauldrons, and destroyed all the poisoned food before escaping on swift horses. 

Brahmadatta was furious when he heard the news. "Rascals! They have ruined my plans. I shall attack Mithila and behead Vaideha."

"Maharaj, it will not be an easy task... Mithila is protected by Aushadha Kumar," Kevatta warned. 

"What can he do against the overlord of Jambudveepa?" Brahmadatta scoffed. He incited the hundred kings, "The King of Mithila has insulted all of us by ruining our feast. We shall punish him!" 

Accompanied by the hundred kings and a massive army, Brahmadatta marched to Mithila. Soon, they laid a fierce siege to the city. 

Inside the fort, King Vaideha was terrified. "Oh God, Brahmadatta will storm my fort and kill me," he whimpered. 

But Aushadha Kumar, unafraid like a lion, calmly inspected the security arrangements. "Do not feel anxious, Maharaj. I have a solution," he reassured the panicking King. "I shall scatter the enemy just as crows are dispersed with a stick... or just as monkeys are put on the run by a handful of stones... Be relaxed, Maharaj, for the enemy will run away leaving even their weapons behind."

While Aushadha Kumar allayed Vaideha's fears, Brahmadatta launched a massive attack on the fort. However, the approach to the fort was made nearly impossible by the three deep moats. When the enemy soldiers finally managed to come near the walls, Vaideha's soldiers rained down a devastating shower of stones and missiles. 

Brahmadatta's attack failed miserably. 

"What shall we do? Shall we lift the siege and go back?" Brahmadatta asked Kevatta in despair. 

"No, that will mean a loss of face. You cannot afford that as the overlord of Jambudveepa."

"But how can I save my face?"

"I shall use a trick to gain a token victory," Kevatta said slyly. "By resorting to Dharmayuddha (a battle of wits). I, as your chief minister, shall challenge Vaideha's chief minister Aushadha Kumar to match his wits with mine. Whoever wins, his side will be considered victorious."

"How will we know who has won?" Brahmadatta asked.

"The vanquished will bow to the victor," Kevatta explained. 

"But you yourself admit that Aushadha is wiser than you," Brahmadatta frowned. 

"Yes, but not craftier than me," Kevatta chuckled. "Since he is younger, he will instinctively bow to greet me out of respect for his elders. The moment he does that, I shall announce that he has admitted defeat."

Aushadha Kumar was promptly warned of the conspiracy by his spies. "Ah! So that is your game, Kevatta. We will see who wins this battle of wits," he smiled. 

On the day of the Dharmayuddha, Aushadha carried a priceless jewel with him to the meeting ground between the two armies. 

"I am sorry Pandit Kevatta, that I could not greet you earlier..." Aushadha said humbly. "...But now kindly accept this gem as a token of my respect."

As Aushadha offered the sparkling gem, he deliberately let it slip from his fingers and fall to the ground. Unthinkingly, greedy Kevatta bent down to pick up the gem. 

As soon as Kevatta bent down, Aushadha Kumar placed his hands firmly on Kevatta's shoulders and pressed his head down hard onto the earth. The head struck with force, and a deep gash appeared on Kevatta's forehead. 

"No! Pandit Kevatta, do not touch my feet..." Aushadha announced loudly for both armies to hear. 

Then Aushadha whispered fiercely to Kevatta, "Stupid fool! You expected me to bow to you." He pushed Kevatta away with such force that Kevatta tumbled backward. 

Immediately, at a pre-arranged signal, Aushadha Kumar's armed soldiers rushed out of Vaideha's fort with a terrifying battle cry. Kevatta took one look at the charging soldiers and scrambled to his feet, blood dripping down his face. "Kevatta has been defeated! Run! Run!" he screamed in panic. 

And so did Brahmadatta, the hundred panic-stricken kings, and their massive army. Just as Aushadha Kumar had foretold, the terrified enemy fled, leaving even their weapons behind. 

The wound on Kevatta's forehead left a permanent scar. The mark constantly reminded Kevatta of his utter humiliation. "Oh, Aushadha Kumar, I will not forgive you for this," he seethed, looking in his mirror. 

Finally, Kevatta hatched another deadly scheme. "Maharaj, I have a plan to avenge our insult," he told Brahmadatta. 

"Your plan did not work out last time, Kevatta."

"This time it will. For you shall offer your daughter to Vaideha."

"Are you out of your mind?!" Brahmadatta exploded. 

"No, Maharaj. This scheme will work. Because... your daughter Panchala Chandi is the most charming maiden in Jambudveepa. Vaideha will easily be lured by her beauty... and at the time of the wedding, we shall kill both Vaideha and Aushadha Kumar."

Brahmadatta's eyes gleamed. "Are you sure the scheme will work?"

"I will make sure that it does, Maharaj."

Kevatta prepared a meticulous plan. He sent bards to Videha who sang enchanting verses praising the unmatched beauty of the Panchala princess. Soon, a bard gained an audience with King Vaideha. A painter from Panchala presented a stunning portrait of the princess to the King. 

"Ah! What exquisite beauty," King Vaideha gasped, utterly captivated. "What is the use of my being a king if I can't make her my wife."

So, when Kevatta arrived with the formal marriage proposal, Vaideha eagerly agreed. "This alliance will unite the people of Panchala and Videha," Kevatta smiled smoothly. 

"I'm happy to accept the proposal," Vaideha said enthusiastically. 

"Brahmadatta and Kevatta are up to some trick," Aushadha Kumar realized, watching the proceedings. He immediately sought information from his spy in Panchala. 

The spy replied with a message: *Clear information is not available. Kevatta and Brahmadatta regularly confer in a closed room. No other soul was present except a caged mynah bird.*

"I must send my parrot again," Aushadha decided. 

Again, the green parrot flew... and reached Brahmadatta's palace. It landed by the caged mynah bird. "Greetings, O beautiful mynah," the parrot chirped. 

"Greetings, O parrot! Where do you come from? And what brings you here?" the mynah asked. 

To extract the vital information, the clever parrot lied to the mynah. "I am the royal parrot of Shivi. I'm looking for a suitable bride for myself. Well, have you found one?"

"Why should I agree to marry you?" the mynah fluttered coyly. 

"Because marriage leads to eternal happiness."

"Not always," the mynah sighed softly. 

"What do you mean?" the parrot asked. 

"The marriage of the Panchala princess to King Vaideha is going to cause utter misery," the mynah whispered. 

"How?"

"Vaideha will be killed when he comes for the wedding."

When Aushadha Kumar learned of the dastardly plan, he tried to dissuade Vaideha from the marriage. But the smitten King was in no mood to listen. 

"Your parrot is a stupid bird!" Vaideha yelled angrily. 

"But, pardon me, Maharaj—"

"No, I'll not discuss the matter any further. We are going to Panchala."

*The King is blinded by his desires,* Aushadha thought worriedly. *But as his minister, it is my duty to protect him.*

Aushadha Kumar travelled with an advance party to the Panchala capital. "Maharaj Brahmadatta, I have come to make arrangements for a grand wedding," he announced in the royal court. 

"Let us know if you need any help," Brahmadatta smiled deceitfully. 

"I do, I need a house and some land to build a lodge for the wedding party."

"That will be arranged."

Aushadha was given a house near the palace to stay in, and a piece of land outside the city walls. 

"Why did you allow him to stay near the palace?" Kevatta asked Brahmadatta in private. 

"So that we can keep a watch on him."

"And why did you give him land outside the city?"

"Because it will be easy to surround the wedding party and finish them off there."

Once Aushadha was settled, he secretly started two massive construction projects simultaneously—the construction of a palatial house for the wedding party outside the city, and the digging of a large underground tunnel from the house where he was staying near the palace. 

"The tunnel will have two outlets," Aushadha instructed his trusted men. "One in the palatial house that we are building outside the city... and the other hidden in thick bushes near the river."

Soon, the tunnel was completed, linking the King's new house to the river, with a branch leading straight under Brahmadatta's own palace. 

When all arrangements were made, King Vaideha was summoned to Panchala. He arrived with great pomp and was settled into the newly built house outside the city. 

Unaware of the treachery, and tired from the long journey, Vaideha went straight to sleep. 

Soon after Vaideha arrived, his house was heavily surrounded by Brahmadatta's armed men. 

But Aushadha Kumar was alert. He immediately put the next part of his scheme into operation. He called a few of his best men. "Disguise yourselves as Panchala soldiers and go to Brahmadatta's palace through the tunnel."

As instructed, the disguised men sneaked through the tunnel into Brahmadatta's palace and entered the Queen's chambers. 

"O Queen! Vaideha has been killed..." they announced urgently. "King Brahmadatta has asked all of you to join him in the feast celebrating this victory. Please follow us."

Thus, the Panchala Queen and the Princess were tricked into accompanying Aushadha Kumar's soldiers down into the secret tunnel. 

Meanwhile, back at the house outside the city, Vaideha was violently awakened by the noise made by the surrounding Panchala army. 

"Block all routes of escape!" Brahmadatta's commanders were shouting outside. 

"What is that?" Vaideha gasped, looking out the window. "Oh God, we shall all be butchered now."

"When desires cloud the senses they lead to misery, Maharaj!" Aushadha Kumar said calmly. 

"What shall we do now?" Vaideha wept, turning to his other ministers. 

"Senaka and other pandits understand politics better than I do, Maharaj. Let them guide you," Aushadha said, stepping back. 

When consulted, all the terrified pandits could do was lament. "Brahmadatta will torture us to death... Let us kill ourselves before that... Yes, death will be less painful this way."

"Oh, God! Is there no way to escape?" Vaideha cried, realizing his terrible mistake. "The King is terrified... I should reassure him," Aushadha thought. 

"The way to safety lies under the ground, Maharaj," Aushadha finally spoke up. 

"Underground!" Vaideha gasped in relief. 

"Yes! Please step this way." Aushadha lifted a heavy trapdoor hidden under a rug. 

Through the tunnel, Aushadha Kumar took the King and his pandits to the junction where the Panchala Queen and Princess were being held by his men. 

"Maharaj, your bride and her mother are waiting for you here..." Aushadha presented the bewildered royal women. "...Please take them with you to Mithila. The boats are waiting at the river exit."

A much-relieved Vaideha rushed towards the river exit where the boats were moored. He jumped in and turned to his minister. "Come on Aushadha, let us not waste time."

"I am not coming, Maharaj..." Aushadha replied firmly. "...my men are trapped outside by the enemy. I cannot leave them behind."

With the King and the royal hostages safely away, Aushadha returned to the house to face the enemy. 

The next morning, Brahmadatta barged into the house, his sword drawn. "Where is Vaideha?" he roared. 

"He has gone to Mithila with your wife and daughter," Aushadha replied calmly. 

When Brahmadatta learned the truth, he became livid with rage. He turned to his guards. "Cut off his nose, cut off his hands, cut off his head!"

"Think carefully before you do anything rash, O King..." Aushadha warned, not moving an inch. "...Because, if you cut off my nose, my King will cut off the nose of your Queen. Whatever you do to me, the same will be done to your wife."

Brahmadatta hesitated, his sword trembling in the air. 

"You are bluffing!" Kevatta shouted from behind him. 

"Then call my bluff," Aushadha challenged coldly, staring Brahmadatta in the eye. 

Brahmadatta did not dare to punish Aushadha at the risk of his own wife and daughter's lives. Defeated once more, he let Aushadha and his trapped men go free. 

Soon, Aushadha returned safely to Mithila with all his men. 

"How did you manage to escape certain death?" Vaideha asked in awe, throwing his arms around his brilliant minister. 

"Maharaj, the brain is mightier than brawn," Aushadha Kumar smiled gently. "Wisdom always triumphs."
