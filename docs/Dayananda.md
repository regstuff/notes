# Dayananda: His Strength Was His Virtue

It was the night of the Maha Shiva Ratri festival of the year 1839. Devotees from far and near had gathered at the Shiva temple in Tankara (near Morvi in Gujarat state). They had fasted since morning and were chanting the name of Shiva. 
"Om Namah Shivaya... Om Namah Shivaya..."

By midnight, many of them began to doze; but not Mool Shankar. The fourteen-year-old Brahman boy was absolutely determined to keep himself awake. 
Suddenly, he noticed mice running over the Shiva lingam. "What's that! Rats! How dare they!"
This set the boy thinking. "Why does God tolerate these pests? He can certainly protect himself."

He woke his father. "Father, father, wake up! The rats... they are eating the offerings!"
"Father, why should we worship one who is helpless against mere rats?"
"Stop asking foolish questions. Repeat the mantra," his father scolded him. 
Mool Shankar tried to obey his father, but he could not concentrate. "This idol cannot be God - the creator of the universe. No! I can never worship that idol. I had better go home."

As soon as he reached home, he broke his fast. 
His mother was shocked. "Moola! You've broken the fast before dawn. But how could I stop you? You are only a hungry child. I shall ask your father to forgive you."
"But mother, this is not a matter of my hunger. I broke the fast because the idol is not God."
"Moola! You must never say such things again. Would so many people worship it if it weren't? Now go to bed."
But Mool Shankar could hardly sleep. "Why should I do something simply because others are doing it?"

The next morning, Mool Shankar tried to talk to his father. But Karshanji Tiwari was the Tahsildar (revenue collector) of Tankara and was too preoccupied with his official duties to spare much time for his son. 
His uncle, however, noticed his distress. "Come, Moola. Come, tell me what's bothering you."
"Uncle, isn't it foolish to worship an idol as the creator of this universe?"
"Well, oh... there are many things we don't understand," his uncle replied evasively. 
Mool Shankar was not satisfied with the answers and he soon lost faith in idol-worship. 

Two years later, Moola's sister died of cholera. His seeming indifference shocked his father. 
"Moola has not shed a tear for his beloved sister," his father complained. 
"Poor boy. This must be too much for him to bear," his uncle reasoned. 
By the time Mool Shankar was nineteen, he began to think deeply about life and death. 
"Uncle, what are we living for? Is death the end of it all?"
"No, son. The soul never dies. It is born again."

His father noticed his distraction. "He is always lost in thought. I don't know what has come over him. I think I do. Let's get him married. I have almost accepted one of the many proposals for Moola!"
Suddenly, Moola's beloved uncle too had a fatal attack of cholera. "Moola... my boy... I won't live through... this. I bless you. Let God's grace... be on you."
Moola wept bitterly. The cruel hands of death had struck again. 

Because of the uncle's death, Moola's father did not bring up the topic of his marriage for a while. Then, a year later, his father decided to go ahead with it. 
"But father, I don't want to marry."
"Son, I have already given my word; and your mother has approved of the girl. It's not fair to delay any longer. You can't refuse now, Moola. It will hurt the family's prestige, and the girl's future."

"Marriage is not what I want now. I must escape while there is yet time," Mool Shankar decided. 
Bidding a silent farewell to his parents in his heart, he left the house at night. He kept away from the main road, knowing his father's mounted guards would hunt him down. 
He met a group of sadhus on the way. Seeing his golden rings, they eyed him greedily. "Where are you going?"
"I have renounced the world. I am in search of a guru," he said. 
"Of what use is gold to one who would renounce the world? Give it away," they said. Moola gave away all that he had and went on. 

Later, he was initiated as Naishthik Brahmachari (one who takes the vow of lifelong celibacy) by a wandering monk, who named him Shuddha Chaitanya. After this, he moved about wearing only a saffron loin cloth. 
But he had not yet found a guru. Hearing of a religious festival in Sidhpur, he set out. On the way, however, an acquaintance recognized him and informed his father. 

That evening as he was about to enter a Shiva temple in Sidhpur, his father caught up to him. 
"What's the matter with you? Enough of this nonsense! You are coming back with me to live a normal, householder's life," his furious father yelled. He locked him in a room. "Guards, keep a close watch over him tonight. Let him not play his pranks any more. He shall on no account stir out of this room. We will leave tomorrow."
But luckily for Moola, the guards fell asleep in the night. He quietly escaped, climbed up a tree, and hid in the foliage. The guards frantically searched for him the next morning but could not find him. 

After that, Moola wandered through Ahmedabad, Baroda and other places. He then proceeded to Kashi, a great centre of learning in those days. There he learnt Yoga. 
But he soon grew disillusioned. "Yoga brings peace to the Yogi. How does it help the people in the care-worn world outside?"
He was deeply distressed by the poverty, hypocrisy and intolerance he saw all around him. 

Then one day, on the bank of the Narmada, he came across Poornananda Swami's party. After much entreaty, Poornananda agreed to initiate him as a sanyasi. 
"This cuts off your past status and relations. Henceforth, you shall be called Dayananda Saraswati."
Dayananda remained with Poornananda for a couple of years. He wandered about in Kashmir and in the Himalayas seeking enlightenment. For days together, he had nothing to eat. One day he ate ice while crossing the freezing river Alaknanda and fainted from exhaustion. 

When he came to his senses, he walked to the edge of a steep cliff. "Let this body of mine perish on these high Himalayas. What good is it without enlightenment."
As he was about to jump, a thought struck him. "No! My death in desolation serves no purpose. Why not use this body to lighten the misery of at least a few! But first I must find a true guru."

His relentless search brought him to the prosperous Okhi Math. The chief priest offered to make him his chief disciple and heir to the math's wealth, but Dayananda refused. "It is not wealth and glory that I seek."
He wandered for nearly twelve years in search of a true guru. At last he came to Mathura and the blind Guru Virjananda's ashram. He was now thirty-six years old. 

"I seek true knowledge," Dayananda told the blind sage. "I have learnt a little of Vedanta and Yoga. I have also studied Sanskrit grammar. But such knowledge does not bring me peace."
"Then throw those books into the Yamuna and come back to me," Virjananda ordered. 
Dayananda did not hesitate to throw his precious manuscripts away. 
"The Vedas are the only source of true knowledge. Ignorance is the source of all the misery and strife in the world," Virjananda taught him. 
Dayananda served his guru devotedly for two and a half years. 

"You have learnt all that there is to be learnt. You are now fit to go out and teach others," Virjananda finally said. 
"Master, I have nothing but these few cloves to offer you as Guru Dakshina," Dayananda said humbly. 
"Dayananda! Go and spread the message of the Vedas in the weary world. That would be the most befitting Dakshina," his master blessed him. 

Dayananda set about his task in all seriousness. In 1867 he went to Haridwar to attend the Kumbh Mela and began preaching. 
"The so-called religious teachers want to keep you in ignorance so that they can exploit you. Don't listen to them," he urged the masses. 
While many were convinced, orthodox priests were furious. They threw stones at him, but Dayananda withstood the attacks with immense courage. 

He fearlessly started giving the sacred thread to untouchables and women. "All are children of God. No one should be barred from initiation on grounds of caste or sex. Women must be treated with equality and respect."
At Anupshahar, conservatives tried to poison him by offering him a poisoned 'pan', but Dayananda survived using his Yoga skills. When the culprit was arrested by a British official, Dayananda had him released. "I have come to set people free, not to throw them into bondage."

The growing popularity of Dayananda was resented by orthodox Hindus throughout the country. A great debate was arranged in 1869 at Kashi. On one side were twenty-seven distinguished scholars... on the other was a lone figure - Dayananda. 
"I will prove that idol-worship is not sanctioned by the Vedas," Dayananda declared. 
After a long session, he silenced all opposition. But the orthodox pundits deceitfully claimed victory and hired rowdies to break up the conference by throwing stones at him. The Maharaja of Kashi later apologised to Dayananda for the shameful conduct of the scholars. 

Dayananda's reputation spread far and wide. He met Keshub Chandra Sen in Calcutta, who advised him to wear conventional clothes and speak in Hindi instead of Sanskrit to reach the masses. Dayananda readily accepted. 
On April 10, 1875, Swami Dayananda established the Arya Samaj in Bombay. "Our objectives are to impart true knowledge, to bring about social justice and to achieve freedom from alien rule," he declared. In the same year, he wrote his great work, Satyartha Prakash. 

He won unprecedented ovations in the Punjab. During his tour he befriended many Muslims, openly eating food given by them - a revolutionary act in those days. 
He also proved he possessed immense physical strength, once effortlessly holding back the heavy horse carriage of a skeptical nobleman with his bare hands. 

Two years later, he went to Jodhpur, where the Maharaja became his disciple. One day, the Swami scolded the Maharaja for his association with a dancing girl named Nanhijan. 
The British, who were highly suspicious of the Swami's role in the rise of Indian nationalism, sent spies to Nanhijan. Bribed by the British, Nanhijan made the Swami's cook, Jagannath, add powdered glass to his daily milk. 

The Swami became seriously ill with oozing sores breaking out all over his body. At last, the cook, unable to bear his guilt, broke down and confessed. 
"Forgive me, Swamiji! What a heinous crime have I committed!"
"Never mind Jagannath! Take this money. Run for your life. If the Maharaja finds out, you will not be spared," the dying Swami told him. 

When his condition worsened, Dayananda was forced to leave Jodhpur for treatment elsewhere. At Ajmer, on 30th October 1883, he knew his time had come. 
"It is time for me to go, my sons! Be brave. Remember only truth prevails. All else is transitory. Om!" he chanted, and breathed his last. 
At Tankara, a monument stands today, raised to the memory of this great son of the motherland.
