# The Gita: The Song of the Divine

Long, long ago, King Yudhishthira ruled at Indraprastha. He had four brothers, and together they were known as the Pandavas. The middle brother, Arjuna, was considered the finest archer of his time. 

When Yudhishthira performed the grand Rajasuya sacrifice, kings and princes from far and near acknowledged him as the supreme suzerain. However, one prince felt deeply jealous of their triumph: their cousin, Duryodhana. 

"I alone deserve to be the emperor," Duryodhana fumed to his brother Duhshasana as they returned to Hastinapura. 

Their maternal uncle, the cunning Shakuni, offered a sinister suggestion. "I know a way of getting possession of the immense riches of Yudhishthira. We will invite him to a game of dice. I know a trick by which I can win."

Reluctantly, the blind King Dhritarashtra yielded to his son Duryodhana's request and invited the Pandavas. The stakes at the dice game were heavy. Using his deceitful tricks, Shakuni ensured that Yudhishthira not only lost his entire kingdom but was also compelled to go into exile for thirteen long years. 

"Now rule as the supreme king," Duryodhana mocked. "The Pandavas will roam about in the jungles as vagrants."

After completing their grueling period of exile, the Pandavas reached Upaplavya. Having fulfilled all conditions, they were ready to demand their kingdom back. To negotiate from a position of strength, they rallied their allies. Lord Krishna, their maternal cousin, arrived with his army. "We will fight for you," Krishna declared. "Your cause is just."

However, Duryodhana fiercely spurned every peace offer. Hoping to avoid bloodshed, Krishna personally went to the Kaurava court in Hastinapura on a mission of peace. 

"O King! Give the kingdom of the Pandavas back to them. Let there be peace," Krishna urged Dhritarashtra. He then turned to Duryodhana. "Dear brother, the Pandavas will accept you as the crown prince. Moreover, by their unmatched prowess, they will make your father the supreme king. You must all live together in peace."

Even the grand old men of the Kuru family, Bheeshma and Drona, tried to persuade him, but Duryodhana stood up angrily. "Krishna, I will not give back to the Pandavas even as much land as is covered by the tip of a pointed needle!" He stormed out of the assembly, plotting with Shakuni to seize Krishna by force.

When Dhritarashtra learned of this wicked plan, he summoned Krishna. Undeterred, Krishna suddenly assumed a terrifying, blinding divine form. Flashes of lightning blazed out of him as he laughed, revealing all the gods within his person. The kings and counsellors shut their eyes and bowed in fear.

Resuming his normal form, Krishna told Dhritarashtra, "I take leave of you with a sad heart. I will tell Yudhishthira that the peace offer has been rejected."

With peace no longer an option, the two massive armies marched towards the battlefield of Kurukshetra. The Kaurava army, numerically far superior, was arrayed in a formidable needle-like formation. In the center of the Pandava army stood Arjuna in his glorious chariot, drawn by four white horses, with Lord Krishna acting as his charioteer.

"Krishna, drive the chariot ahead," Arjuna commanded with great self-assurance. "Let me have a clear view of my enemies and their battle plans."

Krishna drove the chariot to the center of the battlefield, right between the two opposing armies. But as Arjuna looked closely at the faces of his enemies, a sudden, overwhelming change came over his mind. Gone was his self-assurance.

"Krishna, my whole body trembles. My grip on the bow is loosening," Arjuna gasped, sinking dejectedly into his seat. "I find it difficult even to stand erect."

"Why, Arjuna, what weakness has suddenly come over you?" Krishna asked.

"No! I cannot slay my own kinsmen—not even for all the three worlds, much less a kingdom!" Arjuna cried, tears filling his eyes. "To kill my own grandsire's brother, the great Bheeshma? To kill my venerable teacher, Drona? No! I would rather beg for my food than do such a thing."

"This is your hour of trial, Arjuna!" Krishna rebuked him firmly. "Such base despair does not become you. It is inappropriate. The call to battle has been sounded. Arise! Take up your arms!"

When Arjuna still refused to fight, Krishna began the profound philosophical discourse that would become known as the Bhagavad Gita. 

"Arjuna, everyone depending on his station in life has a certain dharma (duty) to perform," Krishna explained. "You are a warrior. Your dharma is to fight for a righteous cause. If you—a warrior—shun fighting after the call to battle has been sounded, you will be setting a bad example."

"But Krishna, how can I be the cause of their deaths?" Arjuna pleaded.

"Arjuna, you grieve because you think you are the doer," Krishna replied. "Think of God as the doer. You are but an instrument in His hands. You are but carrying out His will. Resigning all your work to God, you must act. This is true renunciation."

Krishna explained the eternal nature of the soul. "It is only the body of man that is subject to changes like birth, childhood, youth, old age, and death. The soul in man is neither born nor does it die. Weapons cannot cut it. Fire cannot burn it. Water cannot wet it. Wind cannot dry it. Just as a man discards old clothes and wears new ones, the eternal soul sheds a dead body and enters another. What makes you think you can kill the soul?"

As Krishna detailed the paths of *Jnana Yoga* (knowledge), *Karma Yoga* (action), *Dhyana Yoga* (meditation), and *Bhakti Yoga* (devotion), Arjuna listened, sometimes bewildered, sometimes enlightened. 

"I had revealed these great truths at the beginning of this age, thousands of years ago, to Vivaswat," Krishna stated.

"Thousands of years ago! How do you mean?" a dumbfounded Arjuna asked. "You were not born then. How could you have done it?"

"Arjuna, you don't know the truth about all this," Krishna smiled gently. "You have had many lives before, without knowing it. As for me, I am the Lord of all that exists. Whenever there is a decline of righteousness and a rise of unrighteousness, I incarnate on this earth to protect the good and to destroy the evil."

"Krishna, can I have a glimpse of this real form of yours?" Arjuna asked in awe.

"It is a form you cannot see with ordinary naked eyes," Krishna said. "You need divine vision. I bestow it on you."

As Arjuna looked up, a dazzling light filled the sky. He saw within Krishna all beings, the whole of creation, the past, the present, and the future all at once. He saw the twelve suns, the moon, the stars, and the infinite, boundless universe. Struck with profound amazement, Arjuna bowed before the Lord.

Krishna then assumed his normal form. "Do not be afraid, O Arjuna. He who works for me, he who looks upon me as his goal, and he who worships me, soon becomes free from attachment to the body and comes to me."

"What are the qualities of the man who has reached you?" Arjuna asked.

"The one who has received enlightenment is the same in pleasure and in pain; in victory and defeat; in honour and in dishonour. He is the same to friend and foe. A piece of gold, a clod, and a stone are the same to him. Even such a man continues to perform his actions. Behold, Arjuna, I have nothing to seek in this world. Yet I engage myself in ceaseless activity. If I don't, others, following my example, may sink into indolence."

Krishna pointed towards the battlefield. "Arise, Arjuna. Your duty as a warrior is to do battle for a righteous cause. Viewing alike victory and defeat, gain and loss, happiness and misery, get ready for the battle. Whatever you do, do it as an offering to me."

Listening to these divine words, Arjuna's apathy and despair completely vanished. He saw clearly where his duty lay. 

"Lord, I stand here with all my doubts cleared. I will do as you bid," Arjuna said resolutely. He lifted his mighty Gandiva bow, and its terrifying twang echoed across the battlefield as far as Yudhishthira stood. 

Arjuna had taken up arms! The epic battle of Kurukshetra commenced, ultimately ending in the total annihilation of the Kauravas and the victory of dharma. But more memorable still was the immortal discourse that Lord Krishna gave to Arjuna on that battlefield—the Bhagavad Gita—which has instructed, enlightened, and inspired humanity down the ages.
