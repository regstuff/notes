# Chandrahasa: Faith Cannot Be Vanquished

King Medhavi of the Keralas had no children. He fervently prayed to Lord Vishnu to grant him a son. His sincere prayer was finally heard, and the queen gave birth to a beautiful baby boy. 

But a month later, a messenger rushed into the king's chambers. "Maharaj! Our enemies have surrounded the fort!"
"Rally our men," King Medhavi commanded, drawing his sword. "We will give them a fitting reply."
In the fierce and bloody battle that followed, King Medhavi fought valiantly but was ultimately killed. 

When the tragic news of his death reached the queen, she collapsed in tears. "No! No! My son!" She held her infant close. "Oh, my darling son, I must join your father. May Lord Vishnu protect you from harm."
Then she sent for her old, trusted nurse. "I entrust my son to you. Take him and flee the palace."
"The prince is safe with me. I shall protect him as long as I live," the nurse promised. 

As was the custom in those ancient days, the queen observed *sati*, joining her husband on the funeral pyre. 
Meanwhile, a loyal guard led the nurse carrying the baby prince. "Go out through that secret tunnel. Be quick!"

The faithful old nurse took the prince and fled far away to Kuntala, a distant town. There, they lived in poverty on the streets. 
"Here's some food for your grandson," a kind woman offered. 
"May God bless you, mother," the nurse wept. "What a fate!" she thought bitterly. "Were you born a prince only to live on alms?"

When the young prince was just five years old, the old nurse suddenly fell ill and died. 
"Ma, get up. Get up, ma, I'm hungry," the little orphan cried, shaking her lifeless body. 
After that tragedy, the kind women of the neighbourhood fed the endearing orphan prince. "Come, drink this milk." "Here is a juicy mango for you."
"Thank you, mother," he would smile brightly. 

One day, he watched the other neighbourhood boys playing. "I too would like to play," he thought. "May I join you, please?"
"Only if you have a marble," the boys said. 

That night, the orphan prayed earnestly. "Lord Vishnu, I want a marble. Please give me one."
The next day, while playing near a riverbed, he found a smooth, perfectly round dark stone. "Ah, a marble!"
What the orphan prince did not know was that it was a *shalagram*—a sacred black stone, usually round in shape, considered to be a divine representation of Lord Vishnu. 

From then on, the boy's luck turned miraculously. Armed with his sacred black marble, he always won the games. The other boys admired him immensely, and very soon, he became their leader. "Come on, boys!" he would rally them happily. 
Though the orphan prince had neither home nor family, he was the happiest boy in the neighbourhood, protected by his pure faith. 

One day, the orphan prince and his gang were playing in front of the grand mansion of Dushtabuddhi, the powerful chief minister of Kuntala. Dushtabuddhi was seeing off his guest, a learned Brahman astrologer. 
When the wise Brahman saw the prince playing, he stopped. "Who is that boy, Dushtabuddhi?"
"A street urchin, I suppose. Why do you ask?"
"Mark my words," the Brahman prophesied solemnly. "One day he will be the king of Kuntala. Take him under your care and give him a proper royal education."

Dushtabuddhi was secretly horrified. "The king of Kuntala! Hm..." he thought darkly. "Madana, my own son—not this street urchin—shall be the king of Kuntala."
Dushtabuddhi had long harbored designs on the throne of Kuntala, for the current king had no son. "I must act fast," he decided. 

Soon afterwards, Dushtabuddhi hired two ruthless assassins. "Do you see that boy in the centre?"
"Yes, I do."
"Take him to the deep forest and kill him. You will be well rewarded."
"Leave it to us, sir. We will do the job."

The killers chased the boys. "Run! Run! They mean to kill us!" the boys screamed, scattering. 
But the assassins grabbed the young prince. "You are the one we want."
"Where are you taking me?" the boy cried. 
"To the house of Yama, the God of Death! Ha! Ha! Ha!"

The killers took the boy deep into the dark forest. "I'll hold him. You cut off his head," one assassin said, raising his heavy axe. 
The brave little boy closed his eyes and began chanting, "Hari, Hari! Hari, Hari!" (Another name of Lord Vishnu). 
"I will hold him. You do the killing," the second assassin said, shivering slightly. 
"All right. I will."

But as the axe came down, the first killer suddenly dropped it. "No, I can't. Not this innocent child... somehow my hands refuse."
"Neither can I," the second killer admitted in wonder. "I don't know why, but I cannot harm him."
Then, one killer noticed the boy's foot. "Look! He has six toes on his left foot. We will cut off the extra toe and show it to the minister... and he will believe that we have killed the boy."
As the boy chanted "Hari, Hari," they quickly sliced off his extra sixth toe, bandaged his foot, and left him in the forest. 

The assassins returned to Dushtabuddhi. "We have killed him, sir. Here is the proof."
"Very good. And here is your reward," Dushtabuddhi grinned, handing them gold. "Let's hope the jackals have a fine feast on his body," he thought wickedly. 

But the prince was very much alive. He was being tenderly cared for by the gentle animals of the forest. 
One day, King Kalindaka of Chandanavati, a loyal vassal of the king of Kuntala, came to that exact forest to hunt. 
When he saw the radiant boy sitting peacefully among the deer and peacocks, he was amazed. "What are you doing here alone, my child? Whose son are you?"
"I have no father or mother," the boy smiled. "...And I don't have a son," the King realized sadly. "From now on I shall be your father and you, my son."
And King Kalindaka joyfully took the orphan prince back to his palace. 

Later, King Kalindaka told his queen, "When this child smiles, it seems as though the whole palace is flooded with moonlight."
"Ah, my queen, you have just given him his name," Kalindaka smiled. "We will call him *Chandrahasa*." (Chandra = Moon, Hasa = Smile). 

Kalindaka brought the best tutors. "O venerable one, I leave Chandrahasa in your care. Please teach him all that a prince should know."
Under the guidance of his wise teacher, Chandrahasa soon became an expert in the use of weapons, scriptures, and in statecraft. 
When he was eighteen years old, King Kalindaka formally installed the brilliant Chandrahasa as his successor. 

A few days later, Kalindaka sent for Chandrahasa. "Dushtabuddhi, the chief minister of Kuntala will be visiting us today. I want you to receive him."
"As you wish, father," Chandrahasa obeyed. 
Chandrahasa greeted the minister warmly at the gates. "Welcome to Chandanavati, sir."

Later, Dushtabuddhi asked Kalindaka, "Kalindaka, who was that impressive young officer who received me at the main entrance?"
"It was my son, Chandrahasa."
"Your son? But you don't have a son. How did you come by this one?"
"Some thirteen years ago I found him alone in the forest. I adopted him."

Dushtabuddhi's blood ran cold. "Thirteen years ago? Chandrahasa can be none other than that street urchin! The killers must have deceived me. Will the Brahman's prediction come true? Not if I can help it."
Dushtabuddhi immediately hatched another deadly plot to kill Chandrahasa. He smiled falsely at the young man. "Chandrahasa, you are a dependable young man. Ride to Kuntala and give my son, Madana, this sealed letter. It is highly confidential."
"It is my privilege to be of service to you, sir," Chandrahasa said, taking the letter. 

Chandrahasa rode as fast as he could towards Kuntala. On the outskirts of the city, he saw a beautiful, fragrant garden. He suddenly realized that he was deeply tired from the long ride. "I may as well rest here for a while."
He tied his horse to a tree, lay down on the soft grass, and was so tired that he soon fell fast asleep, the letter sticking out of his waistband. 

Just then, Dushtabuddhi's beautiful daughter, Princess Vishaya, came to the garden with her maids. As they were gathering flowers, she strayed away from her friends. Suddenly she chanced to see the sleeping prince. 
"How handsome he is! Who could he be?" she blushed. Then she noticed the scroll. "He has a letter with him! The letter will surely reveal his identity."
Overcome by curiosity, she carefully slid the letter out and read it. 

The letter said: *"Dear Madana, The bearer of this letter is destined to become the king of Kuntala. Therefore, without waiting for my return, give him Visha."* (Visha meant poison). 

Vishaya gasped in horror. "Visha? Poison? There must be some mistake. I know... in his hurry father has mis-spelt my name! Oh, how kind and thoughtful of father! What a handsome man he has chosen for me."
But then she frowned. "But will my brother Madana understand that the word is Vishaya and not Visha? Suppose he takes it to be Visha and poisons him. Oh God, no!"
Thinking quickly, she picked up a sharp stalk and using her *kaajal* (greasy black eye cosmetic), she carefully added the syllable "ya" to "Visha". 
The letter now read: *"...give him Vishaya."*

Pleased with her cleverness, she replaced the letter in his waistband and walked quietly away. "How fortunate that I read the letter!"

That evening, Chandrahasa woke up and rode to the minister's estate. He handed the letter to the minister's son. "You must be Madana. I have a letter for you from your father."
Madana read the letter and his eyes widened in joy. "Vishaya, father has found Manmatha (the God of Love) himself for you!"
Since Dushtabuddhi was a secretive man who had never confided his dark plots to his son, Madana saw absolutely nothing strange in his father's orders to marry off his daughter to such a noble prince. 
So, with great royal pomp, Chandrahasa was immediately married to Vishaya. 

Dushtabuddhi returned a few days later, expecting to hear news of Chandrahasa's death. Instead, Chandrahasa and Vishaya bowed before him. "Bless us, father."
Dushtabuddhi nearly fainted. He dragged his son aside. "You fool! Why did you disobey me?"
"I... I... don't understand," Madana stammered. "I have obeyed you to the last letter, father. Look at the letter... *Give him Vishaya...*"
Dushtabuddhi stared at the altered letter. "Impossible! How did I make such a careless mistake?" he fumed. 

Then, driven mad by ambition and hatred, Dushtabuddhi made a cruel decision. "Chandrahasa shall die tonight, even if it makes my own daughter a widow."
He called his son. "Madana, ask Chandrahasa to see me."
When Chandrahasa arrived, Dushtabuddhi said smoothly, "I want you to visit the temple of Goddess Kali on the edge of the forest tonight and offer worship to the mother alone."
"That I will father, gladly," Chandrahasa agreed piously. 
Dushtabuddhi then secretly dispatched his assassins to wait in the dark temple and kill whoever entered to worship that night. 

Meanwhile, at the royal palace, the old king of Kuntala sent for the royal priest in a panic. "When I looked into the mirror, I saw a headless reflection of my body. What does that mean, revered sir?"
"It signifies that your end is drawing near, O king. Think of Shri Hari in your last days on earth," the priest said sadly. 
"But sir, my beloved daughter is not yet married," the king worried. 
"Don't despair, O king. Why don't you marry your daughter, too, to Chandrahasa, the noble son-in-law of Dushtabuddhi?"
"A brilliant proposal! Send for him at once, sir."

The priest hurried to Dushtabuddhi's mansion. "Madana, where is Chandrahasa? The king would like to see him, immediately."
"I'll go and tell him, sir," Madana said. 

Madana intercepted Chandrahasa just as he was saddling his horse for the temple. "Chandrahasa! The king wants to see you at the palace immediately."
"But I am on my way to the temple to fulfill your father's vow."
"Let me go to the temple in your place," Madana offered. "You must hurry to the palace. Take my horse."
"All right, Madana, if that is your wish," Chandrahasa agreed, riding to the palace. 

As soon as Chandrahasa reached the palace, the desperate King married him to the royal princess. Soon after, the dying king formally crowned Chandrahasa as the new King of Kuntala. The Brahman's prophecy had finally come true!

The next morning, King Chandrahasa and his second bride visited Dushtabuddhi's mansion. "Bless us, father," the new King smiled. 
Dushtabuddhi's jaw dropped. "Chandrahasa! Why haven't you gone to the temple?"
"The king sent for me. So Madana went in my place, sir."

"What! Madana gone to the temple! Oh! God! No!" Dushtabuddhi screamed. 
Dushtabuddhi hurried to the temple, running as fast as he could. "God, let me be there in time to save my son!"
But he was too late. The killers, who were ordered by him to blindly kill the lonely young worshipper visiting the temple that evening, had brutally assassinated Madana. 

Seeing his son's bloody corpse on the temple floor, Dushtabuddhi was overcome by immense grief, guilt, and repentance for his lifelong wickedness. Drawing a dagger, Dushtabuddhi stabbed himself in the chest and fell dead beside his son. 

A few minutes later, Chandrahasa arrived at the temple to check on Madana, only to find both father and son dead in pools of blood. 
Devastated, Chandrahasa fell to his knees and prayed to Goddess Kali to restore their lives. When his fervent prayer remained unheard, Chandrahasa drew his own sword. "Then accept my life too, Mother Kali, and let the father and son live!"

He raised the sword to his neck. Just then, a blinding light filled the temple, and Goddess Kali manifested before him. 
"Wait! I will not allow one as pure and devoted as you to die," the Goddess smiled. "I'll bring them back to life."
With a divine blessing, Madana and Dushtabuddhi gasped and sat up, their wounds completely healed. 

Dushtabuddhi wept, falling at Chandrahasa's feet. "I have sinned greatly against you, Chandrahasa; forgive me."
"But father! Your sin has already been washed away by your blood and repentance," Chandrahasa smiled, lifting him up. "You are a new man now. Come, let us return home."

And so, King Chandrahasa ruled wisely and justly over the twin kingdoms of Kuntala and Chandanavati, living happily with his two wives, proving that pure faith can never be vanquished.
