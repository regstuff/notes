# Tachcholi Othenan: A Legendary Hero of Kerala

Othenan belonged to the aristocratic Tachcholi family of North Malabar in Kerala, a matrilineal society where martial prowess defined a man. His parents were estranged, and he lived with his mother and elder brother, Koma Kurup, in a humble homestead.

"That scoundrel, Kelappan, has usurped my lands," his mother wept one day, "because I have no maternal uncle or brothers to protect me. My grain and the cash from the sale of my spices fill his granaries and coffers. If only my sons were older."

"Mother, I am going out with Chaapan and Chandu," Koma Kurup said gently. "Keep away from the Tampuran's orchard."

But the mischievous young Othenan had other plans. He and his friends snuck into the very orchard Koma had warned them about to steal mangoes. 

"Run! The Tampuran's men!" his friends yelled as guards suddenly appeared. Chandu and Chaapan escaped, but Othenan was caught.

"Let me go! Let me go!" Othenan kicked and struggled fiercely.

"We will, when we reach the Tampuran's house," the guards grunted, dragging the boy.

"Sir, he was stealing mangoes. We caught him with great difficulty. He is tough," a guard panted before the wealthy Tampuran.

"What! It took three of you to catch him?" the Tampuran frowned. "He will be a threat to us when he grows up. He must be hanged."

Fortunately, Chandu and Chaapan had rushed to Othenan's wealthy, estranged father. "The good-for-nothing boy," his father scoffed. "Here, take this gold to the Tampuran and buy his release."

Othenan was freed, but he seethed with humiliation. "The bullies! I shall specialize in Payattu and beat them all!" he vowed. 

Later that day, Othenan accompanied his mother to the temple. As they prayed, she suddenly began to sway. "Othenan! I am possessed by the spirit of Bhagawati. I am about to die," she gasped. "Trust in the goddess and all will go well with you." She collapsed and died right there on the temple floor.

With no maternal uncles, Othenan and Koma Kurup were now orphans. "Father should have come for this at least," Othenan cried as the funeral pyre burned.

During the harvest festival of Onam, Othenan decided to visit his father's house for his rightful share of family gifts. 

"Don't go! He might insult you," Koma Kurup warned.

"I must go. Please let me go," Othenan insisted. 

When he arrived, his father barely looked at him. "You may collect a mundu and four measures of rice as anybody else."

Furious, Othenan spurned the rice and tore the cloth to shreds. "Who do you think you are to expect any special treatment?" his father roared.

"A member of the house of Tachcholi!" Othenan fired back. 

Suddenly, terrified screams echoed from inside the house. "Sir! Sir! Your nieces are possessed by the spirit of Bhagawati!" a servant cried in panic as the women laughed hysterically. The goddess was avenging the insult to her devotee's son.

As he walked home, Othenan made up his mind. *No uncles; an indifferent father; a peace-loving brother; I will have to rely on myself to regain the wealth of the family.* 

He began rigorous training under Cherayi Panikker, and later Mathiloor Kurikkal. Finally, he went to the great master Ramappan of Tulunadu. Ramappan taught him the fatal *marmas*—the vulnerable nerve centers of the body. 

"Othenan, I shall make you invincible," Ramappan said proudly, handing him an amulet. "So long as this amulet is on your person, no firearms can harm you."

"To you, O Kurikkal, I dedicate all my future victories," Othenan promised.

Othenan's fame as a fighter spread. One day, a neighboring chieftain sent him a desperate plea. The notorious upstart, Kelappan of Fort Ponnapuram—the very man who had stolen the Tachcholi lands—had kidnapped the chieftain's daughter, Kunhi Kanni.

"I must fight him and take what he has illegally grabbed," Othenan declared. Accompanied by his loyal friend Chaapan, Othenan scaled the impregnable fort with a single massive leap. 

"Turn the cannons towards the palace and fire," Othenan ordered.

"It's Othenan! Othenan!" the guards shrieked in panic. 

Kelappan, a coward at heart, hid in a tower, but Chaapan quickly found him. "Kanni is safe," Othenan commanded. "Wait! Don't kill him. We must take him to her father."

They bound Kelappan and delivered him to the chieftain. "I condemn you to the gallows," the old chieftain declared. Overjoyed with gratitude, he turned to Othenan. "Honour us by accepting Kanni as your wife." Othenan gladly accepted.

But the rivalry with Fort Ponnapuram was not over. Months later, Kelappan's nephew, Kelu, returned from Tulunadu. He ambushed Othenan, chaining him and throwing him into the dungeons to be hanged. 

The resourceful Chaapan disguised himself as a holy man and brought Kelu a hollowed-out watermelon. "Take this melon. It is consecrated. Let this juice be his last drink," Chaapan smoothly lied to Kelu. 

Kelu laughed and unchained his prisoner to drink. Othenan drank the juice—and found his hidden sword inside the melon! In a matter of seconds, he fought his way out, vanquishing Kelu and his men. Othenan was only twenty-two.

In the eventful decade that followed, Othenan fought and defeated many tyrants, regaining his family's lost glory. Yet, an old grudge still haunted him: a local master, Mathiloor Kurikkal, had publicly insulted the Tachcholi family by occupying their sacred stone platform.

At a temple festival, Othenan intentionally picked a fight. He mocked the Kurikkal's low caste, which specialized in extracting peacock oil. 

"All right, Othenan. I challenge you to a fight. We will meet at Ponniyam," the furious Kurikkal roared.

The duel at Ponniyam lasted three long days. Like a man possessed, Othenan fought and ultimately killed his former master.

Triumphant, Othenan and his friends began the journey back to Tachcholi. Suddenly, Othenan stopped. "I have forgotten my *urumi* (flexible sword) at Ponniyam. I shall go back for it."

"It is said that one should never return to the field of fight," a friend warned.

But Othenan was obstinate. As he bent to pick up his weapon, a sniper aimed from behind a tree. It was Mayan, a vengeful disciple of the Kurikkal.

Othenan reached for his chest. "My amulet! Alas! I failed to keep it with me," he realized.

*BANG!* The bullet struck Othenan squarely in the forehead. With his last ounce of strength, Othenan flung his *urumi* and killed the sniper.

Chandu ran to the scene. "I'll get a stretcher to take you home," he wept.

"That would be a fine triumphant entry for a famous warrior! No, thank you, Chandu. I'll walk," Othenan said proudly.

Sustained only by a bandaged head and sheer willpower, Othenan walked all the way back. "Let 101 shots announce my arrival," he commanded at the village temple. 

When he reached his home, he lay down and calmly instructed Koma Kurup, "Now I am ready to die. You may remove the bandage."

Resting flat on his back with his glorious weapons laid out on either side, the valiant Tachcholi Othenan breathed his last.
