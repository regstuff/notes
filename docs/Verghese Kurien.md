# Verghese Kurien

What does it take for a milk-deficient country to become the world's largest milk producer? Nothing short of a revolution. And revolutions do not always start with a bang. Some are born quietly, often in the unlikeliest of places, spearheaded by the unlikeliest of people. 

India's milk revolution began in a small, dusty town of Gujarat, sparked off by a man who had absolutely no interest in dairying and had, in fact, landed there by a simple twist of fate. He ended up dedicating his best years to the cause of Gujarat's poor milk farmers and spending his whole life ensuring that our land flowed with milk. This is the story of Verghese Kurien—the man with the 'Billion-Litre' idea—the creator of Amul.

Our story begins far away from Gujarat. On November 26, 1921, Verghese Kurien was born to a wealthy Syrian Christian family in Kozhikode, Kerala. Blessed with a remarkable memory, Kurien excelled in academics as well as in sports, and joined college at the young age of fourteen. 

After completing a degree in engineering, Kurien started an apprenticeship with the Tata Iron and Steel Company (TISCO) in Jamshedpur. His uncle, John Mathai, was a powerful director there. However, the fiercely independent Kurien hated being known merely as the "boss's nephew" and receiving special treatment. 

Desperate to carve his own path, he applied for a government scholarship to study metallurgy and nuclear physics abroad. During his selection interview, the panel unexpectedly asked him, "What is pasteurisation?"

Kurien bravely ventured a guess. "Er... I'm not sure of the process, sir. But I think it may be something to do with sterilising milk?"

"That's right. Congratulations. You have been selected for a scholarship to study dairy engineering," the interviewer announced. 

Kurien was in a dilemma. He wanted to study nuclear physics, not dairy engineering. But he realized, "If I don't take this scholarship, then I'm stuck working here. I'm between the devil and the deep blue sea!" 

He chose the scholarship. He went to the U.S., but cleverly managed to major in his preferred subjects of nuclear physics and metallurgy at Michigan State University, taking just a few token courses in dairy engineering to "keep the government happy."

When he returned to India after his studies, the government reminded him of his obligations. Since they had funded his education, he had to accept the job they assigned him. The disappointed, highly educated engineer was posted to a tiny, dilapidated government creamery in a place he had never heard of: Anand, in the Kaira district of Gujarat. 

He arrived in Anand on May 13, 1949. The place was awful, and he was forced to live in the dirty garage of the dairy, using corrugated iron sheets to build himself a makeshift bathroom. He was bored to tears by the inefficient government job and immediately started waiting for his release orders so he could move to Bombay (Mumbai). 

Unknown to Kurien, a fledgling milk cooperative society was struggling to survive right next door. The milk farmers of Kaira had long been severely exploited by a wealthy middleman named Polson, who bought their milk at incredibly low prices and sold it in Bombay for huge profits. 

Inspired by Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel and guided by Morarji Desai, a local leader named Tribhuvandas Patel had organized the farmers. They realized that "alone, you are powerless against the crafty middlemen. Together, you can crush them and seize the profits of your labour." 

In 1946, the Kaira District Cooperative Milk Producers' Union Limited (KDCMPUL) was registered. They famously went on a strike, pouring their milk onto the streets rather than selling it to Polson, eventually forcing the Bombay government to buy milk directly from the farmers' cooperative. 

However, running a dairy meant operating complex machinery, and their equipment was ancient. Tribhuvandas Patel frequently visited the government creamery next door, asking the bored young engineer, Verghese Kurien, for help fixing their constantly breaking pasteuriser. 

Kurien, thrilled at the prospect of doing some actual work, happily helped them. Eventually, he convinced Tribhuvandas to buy a modern pasteuriser and even helped him raise the massive sum of 60,000 rupees for it. 

Just as the new machine was arriving, Kurien's long-awaited release letter finally arrived from the government. He was free to leave Anand! 

But Tribhuvandas pleaded with him. "The farmers don't know how to operate the new machinery. They need you, Kurien. Stay for two months. We'll even pay you a salary."

Kurien couldn't bring himself to refuse the earnest man. Those "two months" extended into a lifetime commitment. Kurien realized, "I don't think I want to get away from this place anymore. The satisfaction of working for a greater good... I don't think I can find that anywhere else." 

In 1951, Verghese Kurien became the General Manager of KDCMPUL. By 1952, under his dynamic leadership, milk collection grew from 200 litres to a staggering 20,000 litres a day! 

To handle the massive expansion, Kurien was sent to New Zealand for specialized dairy training. Upon his return, he married Molly in a brief trip to Kerala, and brought his new bride back to his humble home in Anand. 

With the help of his brilliant friend and dairy expert, H.M. Dalaya, Kurien achieved a historic, impossible milestone. They successfully converted buffalo milk into milk powder! Until then, Western experts had insisted that milk powder could only be made from cow's milk. 

This breakthrough allowed them to handle surplus milk effectively. With help from UNICEF, KDCMPUL built a massive new dairy, inaugurated by Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru. 

As the cooperative expanded to sell butter and other products, Kurien realized they needed a strong brand identity to compete with established Western brands like Polson. During a brainstorming session, someone suggested the Sanskrit word 'Amulya', meaning priceless. 

"I do like the sound of it," Kurien agreed. It was smartly shortened to the catchier "AMUL," which also served as the perfect acronym for Anand Milk Union Limited. The brand was officially registered in 1957.

To market their new butter, Kurien hired an advertising agency led by Sylvester daCunha. They created the iconic, utterly adorable 'Amul Girl' mascot and the legendary tagline, "Utterly Butterly Amul!" The campaign was a massive, enduring success. 

Amul's success revolutionized the local economy. Milk collection centers operated strictly on a first-come-first-served basis. "Milk knows no caste, no religion, no boundary," Tribhuvandas had declared, and Kurien enforced it. Women, who traditionally handled cattle, received direct daily payments, granting them unprecedented financial independence. 

In 1964, Prime Minister Lal Bahadur Shastri visited Anand to inaugurate a cattle feed factory. Refusing the luxurious guest house, the Prime Minister insisted on spending the night in a humble farmer's hut to understand their lives. Deeply impressed by the prosperity and empowerment the cooperative had brought to the villagers, Shastri told Kurien, "You have not just changed the dairy industry, you have transformed lives. I want the Anand pattern to be replicated everywhere in the country. And I want you to head the operations."

In 1965, the National Dairy Development Board (NDDB) was set up, with Verghese Kurien as its founder-chairman. He firmly insisted it be headquartered in Anand, close to the farmers, rather than in bureaucratic Delhi. 

To fund this massive national expansion, Kurien devised a brilliant, audacious plan called 'Operation Flood'. He convinced the World Food Programme and European nations to donate their massive "milk mountains" (surplus milk powder) to India. Kurien's NDDB sold this donated powder in Indian cities to generate the enormous funds required to build dairy cooperatives across every state in India, cutting out middlemen entirely. 

Launched in 1970, Operation Flood—the 'Billion-Litre' idea—was wildly successful. Milk production skyrocketed, and India soon stopped importing milk altogether, eventually becoming the world's largest milk producer. 

In 1973, Kurien set up the Gujarat Cooperative Milk Marketing Federation (GCMMF) to bring all the cooperatives together to compete powerfully against the private sector. 

Kurien was also a visionary institution builder. Realizing the need for professionally trained managers who understood rural India, he founded the Institute of Rural Management Anand (IRMA) in 1979. 

He even found innovative ways to tell the farmers' story. When filmmaker Shyam Benegal wanted to make a movie about the dairy cooperatives but lacked funding, Kurien had a unique solution. Every single milk-producing farmer in Gujarat donated just one rupee. The result was the critically acclaimed feature film *Manthan*, the only film in history produced by half a million farmers! 

Verghese Kurien was known for his unshakeable integrity, strict punctuality, and a "mad passion" for his country. He lived a surprisingly simple, unostentatious life in Anand, refusing luxuries that his farmer-employers could not afford. 

Dr. Verghese Kurien, the 'Father of the White Revolution' and recipient of the Padma Vibhushan and the Ramon Magsaysay Award, passed away at the age of 90. When asked about his greatest legacy, he did not point to the massive factories or the billions of litres of milk. His greatest achievement, he proudly stated, was that he managed to get a Harijan (untouchable), a Brahmin, and a woman to stand together as equals in the same queue. 

His life remains a towering example of how a brilliant mind, driven by dedication to the common man, can truly transform a nation.
