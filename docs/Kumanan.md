# Kumanan: The King Who Loved Poetry

In the first century AD, during the glorious Sangam age in Tamil Nadu, the kingdom of Mudiramalai was ruled by King Kumanan. He was a deeply cultured and generous monarch, beloved by his people and widely celebrated by the poets of his time. However, his younger brother, Ilankumanan, was a stark contrast—ambitious, aggressive, and fiercely practical.

One day, Kumanan and Ilankumanan were discussing an impending state visit from a neighboring ruler, King Veliman. Just before Veliman's arrival, Kumanan’s face lit up. "Our librarian has secured a rare manuscript," he announced. "I want to have a look at it. You do it on my behalf and bring him to the library." 

Ilankumanan frowned in disbelief. "But, brother, you should receive Veliman!" As Kumanan hurried away, Ilankumanan muttered to himself, "Poets! Parchments! Palm-leaves! That is the musty world of my brother the king!"

When King Veliman arrived at the library, he was surprised to find his host surrounded by dusty texts. "Come, my friend," Kumanan welcomed him warmly. "Share the wealth of this classic treasury of manuscripts. Such a classic should reach all those who love poetry. Have this work copied and distributed." 

Veliman smiled politely, but inwardly, he thought, *Veliman will get the idea that we are idle scholars here—an easy prey to any aggressor.* 

To correct this impression, Ilankumanan later escorted Veliman to the hall of exhibits. "This is my collection of arms," Ilankumanan boasted. "We are not all scholars here. We are soldiers too." He pointed proudly to a display of animal heads he had hunted. "I am itching for fights, battles, conquests! No enemy can withstand the onslaught of Ilankumanan and his men."

On the following day, during the festival of Saraswati Puja, Kumanan hosted a grand conference of poets. When a poet finished a beautiful song in praise of the motherland, Kumanan was so moved that he presented the man with a heavy bag of gold coins. 

"Please accept this," Kumanan said. "It is but a meagre reward."

"Can anything give keener pleasure than poetry, Ilankumanan?" Kumanan asked his brother. 
"Yes!" Ilankumanan snapped, gripping his hilt. "The sword!"

Later in private, Ilankumanan confronted the king. "Was the reward adequate?" he mocked. "The thought that the poem deserved much more worries me." 
"You worry needlessly, Kumanan. There is no greater judge of poetry than you! The reward was proper. I say it was far too much!" 
Kumanan shook his head. "Was it? Grain we can measure. Gold we can weigh. But genius? How can we set a price on a poem of great merit?"

A few days later, a renowned poet named Perunchittiranar visited Mudiramalai. Kumanan treated him like royalty. The king spent many happy days in the poet's company, discussing literature and philosophy. But soon, the poet grew restless. 

"I must now return home. I seek your leave," Perunchittiranar requested. 
"Don't talk of leaving so soon! Aren't you happy here?" Kumanan asked in dismay. 
"Even as I feast here day and night, I am restless because my family is almost starving!"

Deeply moved, Kumanan ordered his minister, "Send three fully laden carts to the poet's house! Food, clothes and ornaments, everything they need should be sent!"

A couple of years later, Perunchittiranar returned, and Kumanan once again showered him with extravagant gifts, including an elephant. This was the breaking point for Ilankumanan.

"Take the keys of the treasury, Ilankumanan, and count out ten thousand pons," Kumanan ordered to complete the poet's gift.
"Doesn't he deserve more?" Ilankumanan sneered. "It is not wise to squander all our wealth on poets. It will lead to ruin."
"On the contrary," Kumanan argued softly, "when a country's culture is not nourished, its life-giving springs run dry. And that will lead to ruin!"
"I disagree. So much of music and poetry will lull us into laziness and make us an easy prey to any aggressor," Ilankumanan shouted. "I am trying to protect the interests of our kingdom. But our ideas differ. Divide the kingdom and give me my share, to rule as I think best."
"If you insist, so be it!" Kumanan sighed. "It pains me, but there is no other way."

The partition of Mudiramalai broke the people's hearts. Ilankumanan shrewdly demanded the richer regions and the capital city. Unwilling to argue, Kumanan gave it all up and moved out.

Under Ilankumanan's rule, conditions rapidly deteriorated. He increased taxes mercilessly while the people were already burdened. When the crops failed and famine struck, the citizens grew desperate. "The gods are angry with us," they whispered. "Only a good king on the throne can have God's grace. We must bring Kumanan back."

A secret plot was formed to overthrow Ilankumanan on a new moon night. However, before the conspirators could act, Ilankumanan's guards discovered the plot. The rebels fled into the dead of night. 

Paranoid and furious, Ilankumanan decided to eliminate the root cause of the unrest. "The real threat is Kumanan," he concluded. "You can only be secure when Kumanan is no more!"

He issued a shocking royal decree: "Hear ye! The king has offered a reward of a hundred thousand pons for Kumanan's head!"

A desperate delegation of citizens rushed to Kumanan's humble forest dwelling. "We cannot tolerate this any more. Let us persuade Kumanan to return," they pleaded. "A hundred thousand pons for this head! Rather over-priced, isn't it? Please, Maharaja. You must return. The country and the people need you."
But Kumanan shook his head. "The country and the people have a king already! Besides I will not take back what I have given away. I cannot return."

A few days later, Kumanan received another visitor in the forest—the great poet Perunthalaichattanar. The poet sang a beautiful, soul-stirring song, and Kumanan listened, completely enthralled. 

"Ah! How exquisitely beautiful!" Kumanan praised. "But now I am strangely sad. How I wish you had come earlier when I could have sent you back with your hands full of gifts you so richly deserve! Poor though you be, you are not so helpless that a reward you cannot bestow!"

"What will I do with a sword?" the poet asked, confused, as Kumanan handed him his weapon. 

"My brother, the king, has placed a price on my head," Kumanan said gently, kneeling before the poet. "Behead me, and take your reward from Ilankumanan. A hundred thousand pons I thought was too much for this head. But if it is to reward your work, my head should have been valued at many times the figure! Well, go on. Don't waver."

The poet dropped the sword in horror. "Heaven forbid! How do you expect me to commit such a heinous crime, O king? Nay, nay! Some ruffian some day will want to claim the reward. Why not a deserving poet like you?"

Meanwhile, in the capital, guilt had begun to gnaw at Ilankumanan. "Nothing seems to work. The kingdom is facing ruin. People blame me and curse me for it," he muttered. "I will change my order! Kumanan should live!"

Just then, the guards ushered Perunthalaichattanar into the throne room. The poet carried a blood-stained sack. "Here is the head," he announced grimly, pulling out what appeared to be Kumanan's severed head.

Ilankumanan collapsed onto his throne, his face pale with horror. "No! It cannot be! My brother dead! This is a black day!" he wept, staring at the lifeless face. Filled with sudden, blinding rage, he turned on the poet. "You, common murderer... You will die in the public square at sunrise tomorrow! I will snuff out your life!"

"I crave your pardon," the poet said calmly, peeling away the disguise. "This is not Kumanan's head. I had this clay model made to shock you... to awaken you."

Tears streamed down Ilankumanan's face as the truth washed over him. "You have succeeded! I have been a blind, unseeing fool. You, noble poet, have opened my eyes!"

Without wasting another moment, Ilankumanan ordered his palanquin and rushed to the forest. He fell at his brother's feet, weeping bitterly. "Dear brother, can you forgive this sinner? The kingdom needs you. And I need you. Please forgive me and come back."

Kumanan embraced his brother and agreed to return to the capital. They ruled together in harmony, and the noble poet Perunthalaichattanar received his reward and departed with full honours.
