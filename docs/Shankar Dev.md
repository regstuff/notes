# Shankar Dev: Preaching the Religion of Love

Prag-Jyotish was the ancient name of Assam, a beautiful land of mountains, valleys, and the mighty river Brahmaputra. In these lush mountains nestled the little town of Alipukhuri. 

Here, in the year 1449, a son was born to Kusumavara, the Shiromani (overlord) of the local Bhuyans or chieftains. 
"Lord Shiva has granted us our wish at last," his mother, Satyasandha, whispered happily.
"What a beautiful child he is, Satyasandha! Let us call him Shankaravara," Kusumavara decided, naming him for a boon from Shiva.

Shantaram, the family priest, was called in to read the stars. "Shiromani Kusumavara, your son's horoscope foretells a great future for him," he announced joyfully.

But tragedy struck early. A few days later, Satyasandha died. Not long after that, Kusumavara too passed away, leaving the child an orphan. 
Under the strict but loving care of his grandmother, Khersuti, Shankaravara grew up into a healthy, highly intelligent boy.

He possessed a deep, natural compassion. Once, he caught a beautiful bird. "Ai (grandmother), look! I caught this bird. Isn't it beautiful?" he showed her proudly.
"It is," she nodded. "But a bird is more beautiful when it flies free in the open sky."
"I didn't think of that," the boy realized. He opened his hands. "Go! Fly away, my beauty!"

Shankaravara loved sports and games. He could outrun all his friends and effortlessly swim across the mighty Brahmaputra, even when the dangerous river was in spate. But his greatest love was to tend the family cows and take them out to graze in the quiet pastures.

When Shankaravara was twelve years old, his grandmother scolded him. "Games are all very well. But, tell me, have you done your lessons today?"
"No, Ai. It... it was so bright and sunny..."
"It pains me to see you neglect your studies," she sighed. "You are the son of a Shiromani! Your father and grandfather and those before him were great scholars. Don't disgrace them, Shankara!"

The young boy took her words to heart. "I won't, Ai. No, never! And I'll make you proud of me! I'll make the family proud of me!"
Shankara immediately joined the village school conducted by the respected Mahendra Kandali. Here too, he was easily the best. 
"I have never had a student as brilliant as Shankara!" his teacher marvelled.

Shankara's pastimes now took a deeply scholarly turn. He read classical poetry and composed his own poems in simple Sanskrit. 
One hot afternoon, he fell asleep under the hot sun while studying. His teacher, walking by to wake him, froze in shock. A large cobra was hovering over the sleeping boy, its hood fully expanded to shield him from the hot rays of the sun!

As the teacher approached, the snake slithered away. Shankara woke up. "Oh... forgive me, Gurudev! It was so warm I went to sleep in spite of myself. Why do you stare at me, Gurudev?"
"Shankara, you are not an ordinary boy," the awed teacher replied. "From today, you shall be known as Shankar Dev."

***

Within a surprisingly short time, Shankar Dev mastered Sanskrit and the sacred texts. His school-days were over. 
"You now possess the most valuable gift—knowledge," his teacher blessed him. "Let it benefit not only you, but others too."

When he returned home, his grandmother was waiting. "You will soon be the Shiromani, Shankara. It's time you became a householder."
"A householder? Please Ai, not so soon. I have other things to do. I want to be free..."
"Free to do what? No, Shankara. You must marry and have sons to carry on the family name."
Shankara bowed his head. "All right. I shall do it to please you, Ai."

Shankar Dev was married to a beautiful girl named Suryavati. Soon, a daughter was born to them, whom they named Haripriya. 
But soon after, Suryavati died. 

Shankar Dev fell into the depths of depression. "Marriage... children... attachment... death... misery... is that all there is to life?" he agonised. 
Though he was a successful, respected ruler, he felt completely discontented. He began to spend more and more time in quiet contemplation. 

His worried uncles approached him. "Shankara, confide in us."
"I long to free myself from the bonds of leadership and go away—on a long pilgrimage," he told them.
"If you must, please wait till your daughter Haripriya is married," they reasoned. "We can take on the burden of ruling the land; but to see your daughter well settled is your duty."

So Shankar Dev patiently waited. He raised Haripriya, and when she came of age, he married her to Hari, the son of a noble Bhuyan. 
"I have at last fulfilled my duties as a father," he told himself. "I may now fulfil my long-cherished dream of visiting the sacred places of my motherland."

***

With seventeen companions, including his old teacher Mahendra Kandali, Shankar Dev left Assam. For twelve years, they roamed the length and breadth of India. They visited Varanasi, Badrikashram, Gaya, and Vrindavan.

At Vrindavan, Shankar Dev witnessed the passionate Bhakti (devotion) of the locals. "There is a gentleness in the fervour of these devotees; an infectious rhythm in their devotional songs!" he noted. "They sing and dance with the careless abandon of a lover before the beloved! Love! Yes! They worship God out of love, not fear."

What impressed him most, however, was the simple language of the devotional songs. "A child could understand and sing those songs! Yet they fill my heart with a peace I have not known. What my people need is this simple religion of love! Bhakti!"

He also watched musical plays detailing the lives of Krishna and Rama. "What a delightful way to bring Lord Krishna closer to the people!" he marvelled.

Finally, they reached the holy city of Puri, home of the eternal shrine of Lord Jagannath. 
At the temple, a priest read the opening chapters of the Bhagawat Purana. "Listen carefully, Shankar Dev," his companion whispered. "This is that great book which is not available in our own region." (In those days, copies had to be painstakingly handwritten on palm leaves).

As the priest read on, Shankar Dev drank in every word. *If only my people could listen to this! If only some day I could give my people the Bhagawat in the language they know.*

Filled with deep inspiration and a life's mission, Shankar Dev returned home to Alipukhuri. 

To please his aging grandmother, he married again and had three sons, but he did not let marriage interfere with his spiritual life. At Bardowa, where he now lived, he selected a secluded place and built a *Namghar* (a house of worship). This became the wellspring of his new religion.

"Come, my brothers," he preached. "Let us cast aside the veil of ignorance. Open your eyes. See God in your enemy. Love him. See God in animals. Love them. See God in yourself. For he is in all. Shun the strange deities that will be pleased only with the blood of innocent animals. Worship the one who is content with your loving devotion."

And so, the *Ekasharana Dharma*, Shankar Dev's Vaishnava movement, took root in Assam. 

One day, a stranger named Jagdish Mishra arrived from distant Puri, carrying a priceless gift: a complete copy of the Bhagawat Purana!
"You must have come from the Lord Himself," Shankar Dev wept, holding the holy book. "How I have longed for this work!"

Shankar Dev made the teachings of the Bhagawat the basis of his Dharma, and translated it into the local Assamese language. "Now, the word of the great Purana can reach the homes of common people."

His poetic genius blossomed as he composed beautiful devotional songs. He even wrote and staged a grand musical play called *Chihna Yatra* (drama with paintings) which took seven days to perform. He painted the backdrops himself and even played the role of Vishnu. 

People flocked from all over to see this unprecedented spectacle. "I have never seen anything like this before!" they gasped, feeling transported to another realm.

The Namghars were thrown open to everyone. People from various tribes—Kachari, Miri, Garo, and Khasi—flocked to hear the versatile preacher. "Why do you hesitate? Come in," he welcomed them. "We are all equal in the eyes of the one God."

***

But every great movement invariably faces fierce opposition. The orthodox priests of various old cults were up in arms. 
"Shankar Dev reviles our gods and our sacrifices! He fills the heads of the out-castes with ideas about worshipping one supreme God! We must do something to stop him."

The Vaishnavas began to be violently harassed. They were stoned on the streets and abused. The priests even managed to turn the local Kachari King against Shankar Dev. 

"We must protect our Dharma at all costs," Shankar Dev decided. "Let us go north to the Ahom kingdom."
The fleeing Vaishnavas crossed over and settled on the beautiful river island of Majuli on the Brahmaputra. 

Here, a brilliant man named Madhav Dev came to debate Shankar Dev. They argued intense theology for over four hours. Finally, Shankar Dev quoted a line from the Bhagawat Purana:
*"As the branches and leaves of a tree are nourished by the pouring of water only at the root; as the limbs of the body are nourished by putting food only in the stomach; so all gods and goddesses are propitiated by the worship of Vishnu."*

Madhav Dev was silent. Then he bowed low. "I accept defeat. Devotion to Vishnu is the only truth. Accept me as your disciple, master."
"I take you in with open arms," Shankar Dev smiled. "You shall be a pillar of the new religion."

Under both Shankar Dev and Madhav Dev, the Dharma flourished. They established *Satras* (religious centres) across towns and villages. 

But misfortune struck again. The Ahom king ordered the local Bhuyans to join a dangerous royal elephant-trapping expedition. After the hunt, Shankar Dev and his followers were ordered to guard the captured wild elephants under pain of death.
Unfortunately, the wild elephants broke through the wooden enclosure and escaped. 

"Master, you must escape before the king's men arrive," his followers pleaded.
Shankar Dev reluctantly escaped, but Madhav Dev and Shankar Dev's son-in-law, Hari, were captured. Madhav Dev was released because he was a holy man, but Hari was cruelly beheaded.

"We dare not live here any longer!" Shankar Dev grieved. Leaving Majuli, the Vaishnavas moved again, finally settling safely at Barpeta, in the Koch kingdom of King Naranarayana.

***

In Barpeta, Shankar Dev continued his teachings. His compassion was boundless. Once, he saw a deer caught in a hunter's trap in the jungle. He immediately released it, but left a coin behind. "We have deprived the hunter of his food. I'll leave this coin here for him to buy food for his family."

But soon, orthodox critics in Barpeta began to poison the mind of King Naranarayana against him. "Your majesty, this Shankar Dev is bent on breaking all the religious laws of the kingdom. He is a dangerous man."
The King summoned Shankar Dev to court to answer the charges. 

Shankar Dev arrived and answered every hostile question with profound wisdom and deep humility. 
"What kind of a holy man are you?" a priest mocked. "You don't even have a spiritual guru."
"Lord Jagannath is my guru," Shankar Dev replied simply. "Isn't that enough?"

King Naranarayana was deeply moved. "How hollow the charges levelled against him are! He is a great man!" The king declared him innocent and offered his protection. "Make Koch your home and let my people benefit by your wisdom."

From then on, Shankar Dev spent many peaceful, highly productive years in the Koch kingdom, spreading the message of love and devotion. When he was 120 years old, having fulfilled his great mission on earth, he nominated his trusted disciple Madhav Dev as his successor and peacefully gave up his mortal body.
