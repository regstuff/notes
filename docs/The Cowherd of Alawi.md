# The Cowherd of Alawi and Other Buddhist Tales

It was the eve of the day Buddha and his monks were to pass through the village of Alawi. A cowherd of the village was out searching for one of his cows, Gowri, that had strayed from the herd. He searched all through the night but couldn't find her, and by morning he was in a quandary. "I won't be able to listen to the words of the Enlightened One," he thought. "And yet... Gowri will have to be found before I go to the teacher."

Meanwhile, Buddha arrived in the village with his disciples. The villagers served the monks food, and afterward gathered to hear Buddha speak. But Buddha would not begin. "I will begin only when the man for whom I have come is here," he said. "But all of us are present," a villager protested. "No, not all. That cowherd is not yet here," Buddha replied. So Buddha waited.

The cowherd, still searching, finally found Gowri far off among the rocks and hurried her home. "He must have ended his discourse by now," he thought, "but I could at least pay my respects to him." Without stopping even for a cup of water, he went straight to the meeting — hungry and thirsty, but relieved to find Buddha still there, waiting. Buddha asked if there was any food left, and had the cowherd fed before beginning his sermon. The cowherd listened in rapt attention, and afterward begged to be admitted to the Sangha. "I will, my child," Buddha told him.

As Buddha and his monks walked on, one of the disciples grumbled about the fuss their teacher had made over a mere cowherd — the teacher, after all, never even asked whether they themselves had eaten. Buddha explained: he had gone to Alawi only to bring the cowherd into the fold, and the moment he saw the man, exhausted and starving, he knew he was in no frame of mind to receive any teaching. "You cannot teach a person who is troubled by hunger," Buddha said. The monks understood, and walked on.

**The Path of Moderation.** The chase was the favourite sport of King Prasenajit of Kosala, but he had grown too fat of late to enjoy it as he once had. Tired after barely entering the forest, he ordered the hunt cut short, to his men's disappointment. Back at the palace, his queen noted happily that talk had turned, as always these days, to food alone. At dinner, his nephew Sudarshana broached the subject of Princess Vajira's marriage — to himself. "I am not going to give my daughter to him," the king snapped, though he was clearly fond enough of his nephew to feel guilty about the refusal, covering his discomfort by calling for more curry and more rice.

Called away to hear Buddha's discourse at Jetavana, the overfed king struggled to stay awake throughout, pinching himself repeatedly. Afterward, Buddha asked why he seemed to always feel uncomfortable after meals. "You must be overeating," Buddha told him. "A man who eats too much, spends his time in sleep... and will never be freed from the chain of rebirth." He gave the king a verse on moderation to recite before every meal, and — since the king's memory wasn't what it used to be — asked Sudarshana to memorize it and recite it to him at the table.

From then on, every time the king reached for another helping, Sudarshana dutifully recited the verse, and the king would set down his food, saying "no more helpings." In time he no longer needed the reminder at all — much to Sudarshana's mock disappointment at losing his role, until the king promised him a greater responsibility instead: caring for Vajira as her husband, with the king's own blessing at last. Later, out hunting again, Prasenajit found the chase enjoyable once more, and told his queen happily that the path Buddha had shown him had given him back his greatest possession — health; his greatest acquisition — contentment; and his dearest relative — confidence.

**The Witness.** Thieves once tunneled into the house of Dhananjaya, a wealthy citizen of Shravasti, and made off with his valuables through the same tunnel. Fleeing afterward, one spotted the city's Kotwal and, in a panic, ducked into a drain, only to lose his bag of gold along the way without noticing. The Kotwal, hearing Dhananjaya's cries of "Thief!", chased the fleeing men into the same drain himself.

The next morning, a farmer setting out to plough his field crossed paths with Buddha and his monks, and saluted them. As they walked on, Buddha suddenly said to his disciple Ananda, "A poisonous snake! Do you see it, Ananda?" "I do, revered sir. A deadly snake!" Puzzled, the farmer went back to check his field for the snake — and found instead a heavy bag of gold, which he assumed the "snake" must have referred to. Just then the Kotwal, still hunting for the missing loot, appeared and demanded to see what was in the farmer's hand. Finding it full of gold, he accused the innocent farmer of theft on the spot, and the king, without giving him any chance to explain, sentenced him to death.

As he was led away to be executed, the farmer kept muttering, "A poisonous snake! Do you see it, Ananda?" The guards, baffled, reported the strange words to the king, who had the farmer brought back and asked why he kept repeating them. "Because I am not a thief," the farmer said, "and the Teacher and the Elder Ananda will bear me out — this man has named Buddha himself as his witness."

The king took the farmer to Buddha, who confirmed that he and Ananda had indeed passed the field that morning, and that what he had said was "a poisonous snake" — because he already knew that the bag contained gold the farmer would sooner or later stumble upon and be blamed for. By repeating the teacher's words when accused, the farmer had proven his innocence and saved his own life — and spared the king the sin of executing an innocent man. The real thieves were later found and punished, and the grateful king thanked Buddha for saving them both.

**The Three Editions of the Sutras** (a Zen tale from Japan). When the Buddhist sutras existed only in Chinese, a young devotee named Tetsugen resolved to publish them in Japanese — a task requiring a fortune he did not have. He spent ten years travelling from village to village, collecting whatever alms people could spare, until at last he had enough to begin. But on his way home, passing through a village devastated by the flooding of the river Uji, he gave away every coin he had gathered to the starving villagers instead. "I must begin all over again," he said simply, and did.

Years later, having collected the sum once more, an epidemic swept the land — and again Tetsugen gave away the entire fortune to care for the sick, rather than keep it for his sutras. Undaunted, he started collecting a third time, and twenty years after he had first set out, his dream of printing the sutras in Japanese characters finally came true. The wooden printing blocks he made can still be seen today at the Okaku monastery — where visitors are told that these are the blocks of Tetsugen's *third* edition of the sutras. To the Japanese, his first two acts of selfless generosity are themselves considered sutras, surpassing even the printed sutras he finally left behind.
