# The Twelfth Year

The Pandavas spent the twelfth year of their exile in the Kamyaka forest, devoting themselves to acts of charity and the performance of religious sacrifices. 

One day, the great Sage Vyasa visited them. "O virtuous Yudhishthira," the sage advised, "life has its ups and downs. Neither is it filled with joy nor with grief. When one is blessed with good fortune, one must know how to be happy, and when misery strikes, one should bear it with fortitude. Practice charity, for there is nothing more difficult than parting with hard-earned wealth."

Taking his words to heart, the Pandavas spent their days distributing charity and performing austerities. Draupadi faithfully served the many sages who visited them, using the wondrous copper Thali granted by Surya to ensure the food never ran out until she herself had eaten. 

Meanwhile, in Hastinapura, Duryodhana was filled with envy upon hearing of the Pandavas' spiritual success. When the notoriously ill-tempered Sage Durvasa arrived at his court with hundreds of followers, Duryodhana saw an opportunity. He served the sage with lavish hospitality, earning a boon. 

"O King, I wish to grant you a boon," Durvasa offered.

"Please visit my brothers in the forest," Duryodhana requested with feigned innocence, "but make sure to arrive after Draupadi has finished her meal." He secretly hoped that the hungry sage would curse the Pandavas when they failed to provide food. 

As expected, Durvasa and his hungry disciples arrived at the Pandava hermitage just after Draupadi had eaten. Yudhishthira welcomed the sage with due honor. "We will return shortly," Durvasa said. "Let Draupadi have a good meal ready for us." They went to the river to bathe. 

Draupadi was terrified. The Thali was empty. In her despair, she prayed to Lord Krishna for protection. 

Instantly, Krishna appeared at the door. "Draupadi, I am very hungry. Give me something to eat first."

"O Krishna, you must save me from the wrath of Durvasa!" Draupadi wept. "The Thali remains full until I have eaten. But I have already eaten, and there is no food left."

Krishna asked her to fetch the vessel. Examining it closely, he found a tiny bit of vegetable stuck to the side. Putting that small morsel into his mouth, Krishna said, "May all the souls in the universe be satiated with this."

At that exact moment at the river, Durvasa and his disciples suddenly felt their stomachs stretch as if they had eaten a massive feast. Realizing they were completely full and could not eat another morsel, they panicked. "If provoked, the Pandavas could consume us in their wrath," Durvasa worried. Fearing the insult of refusing a meal, the sage and his disciples quietly fled from the forest. 

Sometime later, while the Pandava brothers were out hunting, King Jayadratha of Sindhu passed by their hermitage. Seeing Draupadi standing alone, he was utterly smitten by her beauty. He approached her, offering her a place as his queen. When Draupadi angrily rejected him, the wicked king forcibly abducted her in his chariot. 

When the Pandavas returned, they discovered the abduction and immediately gave chase. A fierce battle ensued. The Pandavas effortlessly scattered Jayadratha's soldiers. Seeing his men dying, Jayadratha abandoned Draupadi and fled for his life. 

Bheema and Arjuna pursued him relentlessly. Bheema caught the fleeing king by his hair, dashed him to the ground, and beat him mercilessly. "I would kill you if it were not for Yudhishthira's mercifulness!" Bheema roared. To add to the humiliation, Bheema shaved Jayadratha's head, leaving only five tufts of hair, and forced him to publicly declare himself a slave of the Pandavas. 

Only at Yudhishthira's intervention was Jayadratha released. Deeply humiliated, Jayadratha retreated to the banks of the Ganga and performed severe penances to Lord Shiva, eventually securing a boon to defeat all the Pandavas in battle for one day—except for the invincible Arjuna. 

As the twelfth year drew to a close, a minor incident led to a profound test. A deer accidentally tangled its antlers in a Brahmana's fire-sticks and ran away. The Pandavas chased the deer to retrieve the sticks but lost track of it. Exhausted and parched, they sat under a tree. 

Nakula climbed a tree to look for water and spotted a nearby lake. He hurried to quench his thirst, but as he bent down, a mysterious voice echoed: "Wait! This lake belongs to me. First answer my questions and then you may drink."

Disregarding the warning, Nakula drank the water and instantly dropped dead. When he failed to return, Sahadeva went to look for him. He too ignored the voice, drank, and died. Arjuna and Bheema followed sequentially, both fiercely threatening the unseen voice, but they too drank the water and fell lifeless. 

Finally, Yudhishthira approached the lake. Seeing his invincible brothers lying dead, he was stricken with grief. As he approached the water, the voice spoke again. "I am a Yaksha. I have caused the death of your brothers. Answer my questions, or meet the same fate."

Yudhishthira agreed to answer. The Yaksha fired a series of profound questions. 
"What is heavier than the earth, higher than the heavens, faster than the wind, and more numerous than straws?"
"One's mother is heavier than the earth; one's father, higher than the mountains. The mind is faster than the wind, and our worries are more numerous than straws," Yudhishthira answered wisely. 

"What enemy is invincible? What constitutes an incurable disease?"
"Anger is the invincible enemy. Covetousness constitutes an incurable disease."

Pleased with Yudhishthira's immense wisdom and righteousness, the Yaksha revealed his true form: Yama, the Lord of Justice and Yudhishthira's divine father. 

"I am pleased with you," Yama smiled. "I grant you your brothers' lives. Furthermore, ask for another boon."

"We have spent twelve years in the forest," Yudhishthira requested. "Lord, please grant that in the thirteenth year of our exile, we will not be recognized by anyone."

"So be it," Yama granted. "Even if you wander the earth in your true forms, you will remain unrecognized. Through my grace, you will spend this thirteenth year secretly and undiscovered."

With their brothers revived and the critical boon secured, the Pandavas prepared for the most challenging year of their exile: the year of living incognito.
