# The Bear Comes to Dinner

Mukhia was a lazy man, while his wife was hardworking and practical. 

"Mukhia, get me some firewood!" his wife called out from the kitchen one morning. Mukhia simply closed his eyes and ignored her. 

"Mukhia! O Mukhia! What are you doing?" she asked, exasperated.

"Praying," he answered simply.

"Praying? For what?" 

"Yes, praying! For a storm," Mukhia nodded solemnly. 

His wife pointed out the window. "Can you see that tree laden with pears in our neighbor's yard? How can the fruit fall into our yard unless there is a storm?"

"Do you remember what our kind neighbour said?" Mukhia smiled lazily. "All pears which fall into our courtyard are ours."

Suddenly, a delicious smell drifted from the kitchen. "Are you making khichri?" Mukhia asked, his mouth watering. 

"Who will make the khichri then, if I don't?" his wife retorted. "But I need wood. Let me not stop you from your 'praying'. You do your work, I'll do mine. Go to the forest and get some wood."

"Who? Me? I'm sorry, I can't," Mukhia made another excuse. "What if the storm breaks out when I'm not here to gather the pears?"

Furious, his wife went out and returned with some heavy wood herself. "Shh... place the bundle down, don't drop it," Mukhia whispered, pointing at a squirrel in their yard. "You'll scare the squirrel away. The little rascal is nibbling at a pear. He'll drop it any..."

*CRASH!* His angry wife threw the bundle down loudly. The startled squirrel bounded away over the wall.

"See what you've done! The squirrel has gone and took the pear with him," Mukhia complained. 

Seeing his wife genuinely upset by his laziness, Mukhia thought, *Never mind! I'll give her a ripe pear when I get one. That will cheer her up.* 

Later, Mukhia leaned against a post, staring at the pear tree. "Why do those pears have to cling to the branches? Why can't they fall down?" He then caught a strong whiff of cooking. "Sniff... sniff... I could tell by the aroma. It's your khichri! I knew it! Only one person in the world can make khichri that has such an aroma."

He eagerly approached the pot. "Now!" 

"Stop! Don't touch it!" his wife slapped his hand away. "You won't get even a tiny morsel of it... until you go to the forest and get some wood."

"You want me... to work... on an empty stomach?" Mukhia asked indignantly. 

"Yes. If you want the khichri you'll have to."

"All right. Here I go," Mukhia grumbled, picking up his axe. *I hate to send him out on an empty stomach...* his wife thought guiltily, *but I'll have to... for his own good.*

At the door, Mukhia turned back. "My dear... I... er... I want to sniff the khichri just once before I go. I want to carry its aroma with me. It will act as a tonic."

His wife sighed. "All right. You may sniff it once. But just once, mind you." 

Mukhia took a deep sniff, smiled in satisfaction, and finally walked to the forest. "Mm-m-m- It's so good... m-m... Wah! What a khichri!" he mumbled to himself as he walked.

Deep in the forest, he met a large brown bear. "Where are you going, uncle?" the bear asked.

"To the forest, to get wood," Mukhia replied. 

"To fell trees and gather wood? You'll have to work very hard," the bear sympathized.

"I don't mind. If I carry home a big bundle of wood, my wife will give me some khichri," Mukhia explained.

"Khichri?" the bear asked, curious.

"And what a khichri! Mm-m-m..." Mukhia exaggerated the aroma. "My friend, I wish you could have smelt it..."

The bear licked his lips. "Will your wife give me some?"

"She might. But you'll have to work for it," Mukhia proposed cunningly. "Give me the axe, uncle! I will get you the wood... You'll get me the wood... for a share in the khichri."

"How much will you bring?" Mukhia asked. 

"A large bundle," the bear promised.

"Would four hundred kilos do? Make it five hundred," Mukhia negotiated. The bear agreed eagerly. 

"All right! It's a bargain! You get back home and keep the khichri ready... I'll come with the wood," the bear said, taking the axe and marching off to fell trees.

When Mukhia returned home empty-handed, his wife was furious. "So you are back empty-handed! You won't get any food!"

"Have patience, woman, patience," Mukhia grinned. "You will be getting five hundred kilos of wood... thanks to the shrewd business sense of your husband." 

When he explained the bargain he had struck with the bear, his wife was impressed but worried. "Oh, yes. You have been clever. But... the bear is a glutton. He will gobble up all the khichri before we have finished our first helping."

"He eats fast. We eat slowly. What should we do?" Mukhia asked.

"It's simple. Let's begin now, and have a fair start," his wife suggested practically. 

So the two sat down and ate the delicious khichri. "Ah! It's delicious!" Mukhia praised. 

"Leave something for the bear," his wife reminded him with her mouth full.

"Of course, we will not forget our good friend," Mukhia agreed, taking another large spoonful. "Certainly... certainly."

But the khichri was too good. Before they knew it, they had scraped the bottom of the pot. "It's over! There's no more khichri left in the vessel," his wife realized in horror. 

"I kept reminding you not to forget the bear. You ate twice as much as I did. And now you blame me!" Mukhia argued. 

"I can't blame you. The khichri was very tasty," his wife admitted. "Yet I wish you hadn't eaten so much."

Just then, they heard a heavy thumping outside. "There he comes. RUN!" Mukhia yelled. The woman dragged her terrified husband up the ladder to the attic to hide. 

The bear arrived carrying a massive load of wood. "Ah! This is the house. I can see the vessel of khichri," he smiled, throwing down the heavy load with a crash and walking in. "Ah! The khichri at last!"

He eagerly picked up the vessel, but it was completely empty. "WHAT! I've been cheated!" the bear roared in fury. 

He caught hold of a central wooden post supporting the roof and shook it with all his massive might. Up in the attic, Mukhia and his wife clung to each other in terror. "The roof!" Mukhia gasped. "Shh!" his wife hushed him. Fortunately, the sturdy roof held firm. 

"I'll take back the wood," the angry bear decided. "But how can I? I'm so hungry... and weak..."

He took the empty khichri pot and sniffed it deeply, hoping to find some scraps. As he came out of the cottage with the pot stuck on his nose, he looked up and saw the neighbor's tree. "PEARS!"

With one great leap, the bear was on the wall, and the next minute, he was up the tree, eagerly devouring the juicy pears. "Mm-m-m! Delicious!"

Watching safely from their attic window, Mukhia and his wife saw him. "Look! He's eating all our pears!" Mukhia complained. "Shh..." his wife whispered. 

The bear paused, looking at the empty khichri vessel in his paws. "Why I can gather these juicy pears in this vessel... and exchange them... for khichri!" he thought happily. 

He picked a green pear and sniffed it. "Ah! This one is not yet ripe. No one will accept it for khichri." He tossed it into his mouth. "Why waste it? I'll eat it up."

"Look at the greedy rascal eating the green ones. He'll be sick," Mukhia whispered from the window. 

"Look. The vessel is full of golden ripe pears... our vessel... our pears..." his wife mourned silently.

Suddenly, the green, unripe pears irritated the bear's nose. "AHAHAHA... AH CHE U!" The bear let out a massive, violent sneeze. 

The powerful sneeze caused him to lose his grip on the heavy clay vessel filled with ripe pears. The pot crashed to the ground below, spilling golden pears everywhere. Terrified by the loud noise and his own sneeze, the bear bounded away into the forest and never returned. 

When he was out of sight, Mukhia and his wife came running down from the attic, cheering happily as they gathered their unexpected, delicious bounty. "Pears! Juicy pears! Ours at last!"
