# Megasthenes: The Greek Ambassador to the Mauryan Court

In 326 B.C., Alexander the Great of Macedonia reached the northwestern frontier of India after his arduous Persian campaign. Having defeated King Porus (Paurava), Alexander dreamed of conquering the entire Indian subcontinent. However, his exhausted soldiers, who had been fighting far from home for eight long years, refused to march any further.

Realizing he could not force a mutinous army, Alexander reluctantly decided to return to Greece. Before departing, he appointed his trusted general, Seleucus Nicator, as the governor of the conquered Indian territories. Within a few years, Seleucus firmly established himself as the sole ruler of the Greek domains in the east.

Meanwhile, in the grand capital of Pataliputra, King Chandragupta Maurya—known to the Greeks as Sandrocottus—sat with his war council. "The constant presence of the Yavanas on our frontiers poses an imminent threat to our security," Chandragupta observed. 

Seeing that the main Macedonian army had departed with Alexander, Chandragupta launched a swift, decisive campaign to liberate the frontier. The Mauryan forces crushed the Greek garrisons and took Seleucus Nicator captive. 

Brought before Chandragupta in chains, Seleucus surrendered unconditionally. "My forces have been crushed. I am prepared to pay whatever tribute you demand," he conceded.

Chandragupta showed immense magnanimity. He demanded only the territories that historically belonged to India—Aria, Arachosia, and Paropanisadae (regions of modern-day Afghanistan and Pakistan). In exchange, Chandragupta unchained Seleucus, declared eternal friendship between their empires, and gifted the Greek general 500 formidable war elephants.

Deeply moved by Chandragupta's generosity, Seleucus wished to seal the alliance. He offered the hand of his daughter in marriage to the Mauryan emperor and appointed his most loyal and versatile courtier, Megasthenes, as his permanent ambassador to Pataliputra.

"I couldn't find a man of greater calibre and of a more versatile mind," Seleucus told Megasthenes. "Try to know the land and its people as well as you can. Find out how the friendship between our countries can be strengthened, and send regular reports."

When Megasthenes arrived at the Mauryan camp, Emperor Chandragupta welcomed him with supreme honor. "In our tradition, guests are revered like gods," Chandragupta said. He assigned his most trusted personal servant, Sevaka, to attend to Megasthenes throughout his stay in India—and quietly instructed Sevaka to keep a watchful eye on the Greek diplomat. Chandragupta also presented Megasthenes with a magnificent riding elephant and two fine horses.

A few days later, Megasthenes began his long journey toward Pataliputra, riding across the vast northern plains. Accompanied by Sevaka, he crossed the shallow waters of the Indus River, reflecting, "India, a mighty country, named after a mighty river."

Preferring horses for daily travel, Megasthenes covered the 10,000-stade (1,800 km) journey along the Royal Road. As they passed through Mathura, the capital of the Shoorasenas, Megasthenes was struck by the intense religious fervor of the locals, who worshipped a deity named Lord Vaasudeva (Krishna). 

Listening to Sevaka describe Krishna’s heroic exploits—how he grew up among cowherds, defeated the tyrant Kamsa, and vanquished multi-headed serpents—Megasthenes marveled, comparing Krishna's legendary valor to the Greek hero Heracles.

Continuing east, they reached Prayaga (modern Allahabad), where Megasthenes stood on the banks of the sacred Ganges, watching thousands of devout pilgrims bathe in its waters. "What a magnificent river the Ganges is!" he wrote in his journal. "It truly deserves to be worshipped."

As they traveled through rural villages, Megasthenes sought to understand the common people. Stopping at a simple peasant's hut, he was overwhelmed by their warmth. The peasant family eagerly invited the foreign stranger inside, sharing their meal of rice and *jaggery*—which Megasthenes described with wonder as "honey produced without bees." He also marveled at the giant banyan trees with roots growing down from their branches, and the white fluff of cotton plants, which he described as "wool growing on trees."

After weeks of travel, Megasthenes finally arrived at Pataliputra (Palibothra). He was awestruck by the sheer scale of the city. A massive moat, 600 feet wide and 30 cubits deep, encircled the city, serving both as a formidable defensive barrier and a system for sewage disposal. The city wall was fortified with 570 towers and 64 grand gates, guarded by skilled archers.

After registering with the city municipal authorities—a mandatory procedure for all foreigners—Megasthenes was granted an audience at the imperial palace. Walking through the majestic hall of gilded wooden pillars, Megasthenes remarked to Chandragupta, "I have lived in many exotic lands, O King, but the likes of this palace I have seen neither in Susa nor in Ecbatana!"

Chandragupta invited the ambassador to sit beside his throne during daily court proceedings. Megasthenes watched the Mauryan administration in action with total fascination. He observed how the empire was governed with strict efficiency: judicial matters were settled swiftly, and severe punishments were meted out for crimes (such as cutting off a hand for mutilating an artisan). 

Megasthenes carefully documented the administration of Pataliputra, noting that the city was managed by a board of thirty municipal officers (*Astynomoi*) divided into six committees of five members each. These committees oversaw industrial arts, foreigners, vital statistics (births and deaths), trade and commerce, quality control of manufactured goods, and tax collection.

During his years in India, Megasthenes traveled extensively. He accompanied Chandragupta on a royal elephant hunt in the dense forests, witnessing how wild elephant herds were lured into deep trenches using tame cow-elephants and starved into submission. He observed the grand Vasant Panchami festival, where the emperor paraded through Pataliputra accompanied by thousands of guards carrying golden vessels, trained panthers, and caged singing birds.

Disguising himself in native robes, Megasthenes walked the streets at night to observe private life. He noted that Indian society was divided into seven distinct classes: philosophers, farmers, herders, artisans, soldiers, overseers, and councillors. He was deeply impressed by the high moral character and honesty of the citizens, recording that thefts were extremely rare, contracts were rarely broken, and people left their houses unlocked. 

He also noted the immense military strength of the empire. Chandragupta maintained a standing army of 600,000 infantry, 30,000 cavalry, 9,000 war elephants, and a large navy, all managed by a War Office with six specialized boards.

Megasthenes traveled south into the Pandya kingdom, noting with surprise that the southern realm was ruled by queens who preferred women for senior administrative posts. He also visited the bustling western seaport of Surparaka (Sopara), admiring the Mauryan Empire's impressive maritime trade fleet.

After several years in India, Megasthenes' tenure came to an end. He returned to Pataliputra to bid farewell to Emperor Chandragupta. Before departing, Megasthenes thanked his faithful attendant, Sevaka. 

"Goodbye and thank you," Megasthenes smiled. "You have been a wonderful companion."
"To tell you the truth, sir," Sevaka confessed with a grin, "I was asked by the King to spy on you."
"I know!" Megasthenes laughed heartily, having been aware of it all along.

Megasthenes rode back to Greece carrying the four volumes of his masterwork, *Indica*. Although the original manuscripts were later lost to time, extensive fragments preserved by ancient Greek and Roman historians remained the first authentic, detailed, and eyewitness account of Indian civilization ever presented to the Western world.
