# Udayana: A Tale of Love and Kingdom

Long ago, King Udayana of Vatsa, aided by his wise and loyal minister Yaugandharayana, eloped with Princess Vasavadatta, the daughter of King Pradyota of Avanti. He brought his beautiful bride from her capital, Ujjaini, to his own kingdom of Kaushambi.

At Kaushambi, Udayana was so deeply in love with his new wife that he neglected all his royal duties. His indifference to the state worried Yaugandharayana immensely. "A powerful enemy in King Pradyota, and an indifferent king here," the minister fretted. 

One day, another minister, Rumanvan, burst into the royal hall. "Aruni's troops are closing in on Kaushambi—and on us! The King! I must get him away to safety. Harness the chariot!"
Yaugandharayana quickly took charge. "Your Majesty, we must leave for the village of Lavanaka immediately. There is no time to explain."

Udayana, trusting his minister implicitly, agreed. He, Vasavadatta, the ministers, and Vasantaka the court jester fled to Lavanaka. On the journey, Rumanvan warned, "Your Majesty, Aruni's troops are storming Kaushambi."
Udayana, gazing lovingly at his wife, smiled. "Let them. Right now my heart knows no storms. It is full of love."

At the camp in Lavanaka, Yaugandharayana plotted to regain the kingdom. "The king is in a daze," he thought. "I must defend Kaushambi. King Darshaka of Magadha could be the powerful ally I need. It is prophesied that Udayana will marry Darshaka's sister, Padmavati, and only then will Aruni be crushed. But with Vasavadatta by his side, Udayana will never agree, nor will Darshaka risk his sister's happiness. I must hasten the fulfillment of this prophecy."

The minister realized he needed Vasavadatta's help. He approached her, saying, "O wise queen, I need your help to save the kingdom and your lord, the king. Will you do as I say, asking no questions?"
"I have full faith in your loyalty to my lord and to me," she replied resolutely. 

Yaugandharayana then revealed his daring plan to Rumanvan: "I will remove Vasavadatta from the scene. You must persuade the king to visit Magadha. Then we rally our forces."

A few days later, while Udayana was out hunting, Yaugandharayana asked Vasavadatta to wait secretly outside the camp. Then he instructed Rumanvan to gather all the men away from the royal tents. While the camp was empty, Yaugandharayana deliberately set fire to Vasavadatta's tent. When the men saw the flames, the minister pretended to rush in to save her, but instead, he slipped away through the back to join the hidden queen. 
"My lady, you must not disclose your identity to Udayana or anyone else," he instructed. "I will leave you in the care of the Princess of Magadha." 

Hours later, Udayana returned to find his camp in ashes. "The Queen and Yaugandharayana have perished in the fire," a weeping Rumanvan reported. Udayana collapsed in overwhelming grief, clutching the charred remains of Vasavadatta's ornaments. "Vasavadatta, my dear queen! There is no life for me without you," he sobbed.

Believing his queen dead and his kingdom lost, the heartbroken king agreed to his ministers' pleas to travel to Magadha to seek King Darshaka's help. 

Meanwhile, Yaugandharayana, disguised as a wandering hermit, and Vasavadatta, dressed simply as his sister "Avantika," arrived at a hermitage near Rajagriha, the capital of Magadha. Princess Padmavati of Magadha happened to be resting there. Seeing the princess, Vasavadatta felt an immediate sisterly bond. 

When Padmavati offered charity to the hermits, Yaugandharayana stepped forward. "I want Your Highness to look after my sister, Avantika, for some time. Her husband is away, and I am a mendicant."
Padmavati graciously accepted the charge, and Vasavadatta became her companion. 

Soon after, Udayana arrived at the Magadha court. King Darshaka, deeply impressed by the valiant but sorrowful king, offered him military support—and the hand of his sister, Padmavati. Though his heart still belonged to Vasavadatta, Udayana accepted the alliance for the sake of his kingdom.

Vasavadatta, living under the alias Avantika in Padmavati's quarters, learned of the impending marriage. Though heartbroken, she realized it was necessary to save Udayana's kingdom. She silently endured her sorrow, refusing to attend the wedding festivities. 

One afternoon in the palace gardens, Padmavati, Avantika, and the maids hid in a bower as Udayana and Vasantaka walked by. From their hiding place, the women overheard Vasantaka ask the king, "Whom do you like better? The queen you lost or the one you have now?"
"I admire Padmavati," Udayana sighed softly, "but my heart—it still belongs to Vasavadatta." 
Tears of joy sprang to Vasavadatta's eyes, while Padmavati, showing true nobility, whispered, "His love is not fickle and is worth winning."

When Udayana began to weep for his lost queen, Vasantaka left to fetch water. Padmavati quickly stepped out to comfort her husband, while Avantika slipped away to avoid detection. 

Later, hearing Padmavati was unwell, Udayana went to her chamber. Finding it empty, he lay down and accidentally fell asleep. Vasavadatta, coming to check on the princess, sat by his side in the dark. Suddenly, Udayana spoke in his sleep, "O Vasavadatta! My dear wife. Why don't you answer me? Are you angry?"
"No, dear one," she whispered softly to the sleeping king. "Not angry but sad, very sad."
Realizing she had stayed too long, she rushed to leave. Her movement woke Udayana, who bolted up and chased after her, crying, "Vasavadatta! Stop!" but she vanished into the night. Udayana insisted to Vasantaka that she was alive, but his friend dismissed it as a dream.

Just then, news arrived: Magadha's armies were ready. Udayana rode into battle and successfully crushed Aruni, reclaiming Kaushambi.

Triumphant, Udayana returned to Rajagriha. Waiting for him were a chamberlain and a matron from Ujjaini, carrying messages from Vasavadatta's parents. They brought a portrait of Udayana and Vasavadatta, drawn to bless their past marriage. 

When Padmavati asked to see the portrait of the legendary queen, she gasped. "She is the exact replica of Avantika, my companion!"
"Avantika? Where is she? Send for her at once!" Udayana ordered.

At that very moment, Yaugandharayana arrived in his hermit disguise to claim his "sister." Vasavadatta entered the hall. Seeing her, Udayana shouted in disbelief and joy. Padmavati, realizing her beloved companion was the true queen, fell to Vasavadatta's feet. "Your Majesty, forgive me for treating you as a mere companion!"
"Rise, my sister," Vasavadatta smiled. "You loved me well and I was happy here."

Yaugandharayana stepped forward, bowing low. "Please pardon me, Your Majesty. I concealed the queen and plotted this deception to save Kaushambi."
Udayana, overwhelmed with happiness, forgave everyone. "The rogues! But all has ended well," he laughed. With his kingdom restored and both his beloved queens by his side, King Udayana prepared to journey back to Ujjaini to celebrate their joyful reunion.
