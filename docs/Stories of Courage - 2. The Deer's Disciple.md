# The Deer's Disciple

Long ago, the king of Kosala was very fond of hunting. Every other day, he would forcibly gather his people and set out on a grand deer hunt in the distant jungle. 
"Come, come! The king wants you!" the royal guards would order.
"But I have to sow my field today," a farmer protested.
"My cows have to be milked," a milkman pleaded.
"How dare you disobey the king's command! Come along immediately," the guards threatened. 

"Oh dear, these mandatory deer hunts are becoming too much," the townsfolk complained to each other. "We are forced to go away all day and our work completely suffers."
"Let us think of a way out of this chore," they decided. They put their heads together to find a solution. 
"If only the forest wasn't so terribly far off."
"I have an idea," one man suggested. "Let's make a deer park right here."
"But where?"
"In the small wood nearby. We could secretly dig a lake there. Make a tall fence and a strong gate to keep them in."

The townsfolk worked hard to get the deer park ready. Two months later, the preparations were complete. 
"There! The lake is dug and filled with fresh rain water."
"And there is enough sweet grass for several deer to graze comfortably."
"Now how will we get the deer here?"
"We must go to the deep jungle with clubs and dumbbells and drive them here."

On the appointed day, the entire town set out to gather the deer. "Beat the bushes till the deer come out of hiding," they yelled. "We will herd them back to the deer park and firmly close the gate."

Armed with weapons and accompanied by extremely loud music and drumming, the mob entered the jungle. 
A exceptionally wise young deer named Nandiya saw the approaching mob. *These men appear to be aggressive hunters,* he thought. *I must try to save my frail father and mother from them, even at the cost of my own life.*

He rushed to his parents. "You two keep entirely hidden behind the thick shrubbery. When the men come in this direction, I will loudly sprint out. The hunters will surely chase after me instead."
As he had guessed, when he visibly ran out of the bushes, the hunters eagerly gave chase and did not search the thicket behind him. 
"Look! A deer came out from this bush. Now let us go ahead and herd him!" a hunter shouted. 
*Good! My dear old parents are saved,* Nandiya smiled in relief as he allowed himself to be driven forward.

Along with the dozens of other panicked deer who had been rounded up, Nandiya was led to the new park. "Come, let us take them to the new enclosed park in Saket."
Deep in the jungle, his hidden parents wept. "Our son is being taken to Saket."

The villagers rushed to the king to tell him the extremely convenient news. "O king! We have gathered several deer for you in the nearby park. Now you need not travel so far for hunting to the deep forest."

The deer gathered in the enclosed park quickly realised that their fate was utterly sealed. 
"We are all completely destined to be killed sooner or later," one wept. "The king comes and slays one of us every single day."
An old, wise deer suggested a fair idea. "Let us assign turns. We will face death bravely one by one, so the rest can live in peace until their day comes."
"Agreed!" the herd sighed.

Nandiya's turn was yet to come. He spent his days peacefully grazing in the park. But his parents in the deep jungle missed him terribly. 
One day, they spotted a lone traveller. "Look! That brahmin appears to be going to the town of Saket."
In a human voice, they miraculously called out to the traveller. "O brahmin, if you are going to Saket, please take a message to our beloved son in the deer park."
"Certainly!" the startled brahmin agreed.
"Tell him that his aged parents long to see him."
"I will diligently do so."

The brahmin stopped at the deer park fence in Saket. "Which one of you is Nandiya?" he called out.
"Me!" Nandiya stepped forward. 
The brahmin faithfully repeated the sad message. 
"Yes," Nandiya sighed. "I could go to see them. I am strong enough to easily jump over this fence, but I will not do so."
"Why not?" the brahmin asked, confused.
"I am indebted to the king for feeding me and keeping me safe for so long. I also have a strong bond of honor with the other deer held captive here. I agreed to take my turn. I cannot simply run away like a coward."
"As you wish!" the brahmin shrugged, walking away.
"I will bravely face the king's arrows when my turn comes. Only then shall I decide what to do," Nandiya promised himself.

A few days later, the elder deer approached him. "It is your turn today Nandiya, to offer yourself as the king's victim."
"I am ready."

When the king arrived with his bow, he noticed a remarkable, shocking difference in his victim of the day. 
*Every day, the deer destined to die naturally runs helter-skelter when it sees me, and visibly shivers in terrifying anticipation of its death,* the king frowned. *But this particular deer stands completely calmly facing me, totally unafraid of his impending death. How incredibly strange!*

The king stood with his bow lowered, staring in surprise at Nandiya. 
"O king!" Nandiya spoke in a human voice. "Why don't you shoot your arrows?"
"I cannot," the stunned king whispered, dropping his bow.
"Why not?"
"Even this arrow, a lifeless piece of wood, seemingly recognizes your great merits and refrains from hitting you," the ashamed king replied. "But I, a human being, was not able to appreciate your merit until now. Forgive me. Go, I will spare your life."

"I am grateful. But I will spare them too," Nandiya said, gesturing to the herd. "What about the other captive deer in this park?"
"I will gladly spare them too," the king nodded.
"What of the other frightened beasts in the forest, the fishes in the sea, the birds in the sky?" Nandiya pressed gently.
"I... I spare them all!" the king declared, his heart completely changed.

Thus deeply guided by the selfless Nandiya, the king made a grand public proclamation: "Listen carefully, citizens of Saket! Hunting of all animals is henceforth completely banned in the entire kingdom."

For many days, Nandiya stayed with the humbled king, giving him wise counsel. "Modesty, gentleness, charity, sacrifice, tenderness, forgiveness and non-violence—these are the true virtues befitting a great king," Nandiya taught him. 

Only when he was sure the king's heart was permanently changed did Nandiya finally return to the deep jungle to joyfully reunite with his waiting parents.
